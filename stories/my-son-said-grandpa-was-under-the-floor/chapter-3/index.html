My Son Said Grandpa Was Under the Floor

Chapter 03 — The Quietest Sound in the Building

Chapter 3 of 15

The hammer had been useless.

Milo had figured that out within a minute of arriving. He had knelt on the concrete with the little claw hammer from the kitchen drawer and hit the floor with it and the floor had answered with a dead, fat tock that went nowhere.

So he had gone to the pegboard and taken down his grandfather’s pry bar.

Three feet of forged steel, cold at the middle where nobody’s hands ever went. He had watched Otis use it to lever a rusted-on brake drum every winter of his life. Never pull with your back, boy. Let the bar do it.

Then he had rolled the first tyre stack away.

That had taken four minutes and skinned two knuckles, and no adult in the building had turned around, because all three of them were standing at the workbench looking at paper.

Now he brought the bar down a second time.

SCRAPE.

The end of it slid, screamed across the plate, and the whole shop rang like a struck bucket.

And then, into the half-second of silence that came after — while the fluorescent tubes buzzed and the adults were still drawing breath to shout at him — something moved under the floor.

Not a knock.

A breath.

It came up through the seam the way cold comes up out of a cellar, and it was the smallest sound Milo had ever heard in his life. Quieter than the lights. Quieter than his own heartbeat in his ears.

He dropped to one knee.

He put his ear flat against the steel.

He stopped breathing so he could hear better, and he held that, and he held it, and the whole of his face went into the listening.

Nobody else in the shop reacted.

Not his mother. Not Ms. Odell. Not his uncle.

That was when Milo understood something that a ten-year-old should not have to understand, which was that being right was not going to be enough, and that he was completely alone with it.

Dean was already beside him.

He had crossed the floor in about a second and a half and now he was bending down with his hand out for the bar, and he was smiling.

Milo looked at the hand and not the face.

He knew the hand. It was a soft hand. It had a wedding band on it that his uncle turned with his thumb when he was thinking. Right now the tendons stood up on the back of it like cables under a tarp, and the fingers were open too wide.

“Milo. Enough.”

The voice was calm. The voice was completely calm and that was worse than shouting, because Milo had made a noise loud enough to be heard from the street, and his uncle was not asking what he had found.

Grown-ups ask what you found.

Milo tightened his grip on the bar.

He glanced up once — not at Dean’s eyes, at the corner of his jaw, where a muscle was working — and then straight back down at the floor.

“Let go, buddy.”

Milo did not let go.

Behind them, Ms. Odell had put the pen down on the contracts. She was standing with her weight settled, both feet planted, in a place that was not quite between Milo and anything, and she had gone very still.

“Dean,” Carmen said. “Just take it off him.”

“He’s got a grip like his grandfather.”

That was a joke. Everyone was supposed to laugh.

Milo pressed his palm flat against the plate.

Warm.

Concrete in a shop with the bay door open at five in the afternoon is cold. He had sat on this floor a hundred times. It was always cold.

This was warm, the way a thing is warm when there is a body under it.

He opened his mouth to say so.

His mother’s hands closed on his shoulders from behind.
