My Son Said Grandpa Was Under the Floor

Chapter 09 — He Didn't Run

Chapter 9 of 15

The boy got the tape off.

He did it the way you take a bandage off someone you love, with one hand flat on his grandfather’s cheek to hold the skin, working the corner up with a thumbnail, saying sorry, sorry, sorry the whole time.

Otis’s first breath through his own mouth came out ragged.

His first words were not to Carmen.

“You came back,” he said to Milo.

Carmen was on the phone before she knew she had taken it out.

“Nine-one-one, what’s the address of your emergency.”

“Kessler Auto, twenty-two-oh-nine Franklin. My father’s been —”

She stopped.

Because there was a word and she had to say it out loud in a room with her brother standing in it.

“My father’s been held. He was locked under the floor. He’s seventy-six. He’s been down there two days.”

“Ma’am, is the person who did this still present?”

Carmen turned around.

Dean was standing exactly where he had been standing.

“Yes,” she said.

Ms. Odell went for the door.

She did not run. That was the strangest part. She walked — a brisk, professional walk, the walk of a woman leaving a showing that has run long — out under the half-open roll-up door, into the orange light, to a silver sedan parked beyond it.

Nobody stopped her.

She left the contracts on the workbench.

Carmen watched the car pull out onto Franklin and she thought, with a clarity that surprised her: she left the folder. Four days of driving out here with that folder under her arm, and she had walked away without it, and Carmen understood that this was the single most useful thing anyone had done all afternoon.

Then Dean spoke.

“He got down there himself.”

Carmen turned.

Her brother was looking into the pit now. He had finally looked. His face had rearranged itself into concern — real, textured, convincing concern, the concern of a son — and he crouched at the edge of the opening with one hand braced on the concrete.

“Pop,” he said. “Pop, what were you doing.”

Carmen’s skin went cold.

“Dean.”

“He’s been confused all month.” He said it to her, and then, immediately, again, in the direction of the phone in her hand. “He’s been confused all month. He wanders. He got down there and he couldn’t get back up and he must have pulled the doors —”

“His hands are tied.”

“He does things with rags, Carm, he’s always got a rag —”

“*His hands are tied.*”

“You’re upset.”

He said it kindly.

Carmen stood in her father’s shop and listened to her brother build it in front of her, live, out loud, in real time — a whole second story, load-bearing, complete with a confused old man and a distraught sister who had had a very hard week — and she understood that he had not been improvising for the last ten minutes.

He had a version ready.

He had had a version ready since Tuesday.

“You didn’t run,” she said.

“Why would I run?”

“Because I called the police.”

Dean looked up at her from the edge of the pit, and for one moment there was nothing on his face at all, no warmth, no fear, no brother.

“Carm,” he said. “It’s going to be my word and a ten-year-old’s.”

Down in the pit, Milo had gone very still beside his grandfather.

Otis put his tied wrists over his grandson’s shoulders, awkwardly, both arms together, the only way he could.

“Don’t untie me,” the old man said.

Milo looked up.

“Leave the knot, boy.” Otis’s voice was cracked to pieces and perfectly steady. “Leave it exactly like it is. Let them cut it off me at the hospital.”

Two days in the dark, and the first thing he thought about was evidence.

Carmen sat down on the concrete beside the open doors, because her legs had stopped working, and in the distance, somewhere out past the bay door, a siren started.
