My Son Said Grandpa Was Under the Floor

Chapter 05 — Pop's Up at the VA

Chapter 5 of 15

“Pop’s up at the VA, Carmen.”

He said it gently. He said it the way you say a thing to someone who is tired and has had a long week and needs to be handed a simple fact to hold.

“He’s got a bed. He’s got a nurse. He’s fine.”

Carmen opened her mouth to agree.

Milo stopped struggling.

She felt it happen in her hands — the whole fight going out of his body at once, all of it, gone — and he turned his head and looked directly at his uncle for the first time all afternoon.

“He’s in the pit.”

Four words.

The boy said them flatly, without heat, the way you read a street sign to someone who is driving.

And Carmen was looking at her brother’s face when they landed.

Dean’s expression did not break.

That was the thing.

If it had broken she could have gone on with her life. If he had laughed, if he had rolled his eyes, if he had said Jesus, kid — any of the hundred things a man says when his nephew accuses him of something insane — she would have signed the papers and driven home and made pasta.

His expression did not break.

It froze.

The warmth stayed exactly where it was, on his face, unchanged, like a photograph of warmth. His jaw set and stayed set. His breath caught somewhere behind his collarbone and did not come out.

And his eyes went sideways.

Not to Milo. Not to her.

To the second stack of tyres.

To the bare patch of floor beside it.

Half a second. Less. Then back to Carmen, and the warmth came on again like a light being switched, and he smiled at her.

Too late.

By about one full second, her brother smiled at her too late.

Carmen’s irritation drained out of her so fast it left her cold.

She stood in her father’s shop with her hands on her son’s shoulders and she thought, very clearly and very calmly:

He looked at the floor.

He didn’t ask what the boy meant.

Nobody has asked what the boy meant.

“Kids hear things,” Dean said. “It’s an old building.”

“You looked at the floor.”

“What?”

“Just now. He said that, and you looked at the floor.”

Dean’s thumb turned the wedding band around his finger. Once. Twice.

“I looked at the mess he’s making,” he said.

That was a good answer. It was a genuinely good answer and it arrived a half-second late and it came out of a mouth that had gone dry, and Carmen heard the dryness in it, and her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

She had known this man for forty years.

She had never in her life had to evaluate him.

“Dean.”

“Carm.”

“Which VA.”

“I told you. Clairton.”

“What’s the ward.”

He said, “I don’t know the ward,” and he said it too fast, and Ms. Odell — who had not spoken, who had not looked up, who had been staring at a patch of concrete for four minutes — chose that exact moment to reach out and square the stack of contracts on the workbench so the edges lined up.

A small, tidy, pointless gesture.

The gesture of a person who needs her hands to be doing something.

Carmen looked at her.

Ms. Odell did not look back.

And under Carmen’s palms, her son’s shoulders had gone as hard as rebar, because he was gathering himself, because he had heard his mother ask a question instead of telling him to be quiet, and for the first time in two days somebody in his family was listening.

Milo tore free.
