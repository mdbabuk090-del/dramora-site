My Son Said Grandpa Was Under the Floor

Chapter 02 — Two Days Before

Chapter 2 of 15

The first time Milo said it, Carmen was draining pasta.

“There’s somebody in Grandpa’s shop.”

“There’s nobody in Grandpa’s shop.”

“Under it.”

She shook the colander. “Under it how?”

“Under the floor.” He was standing in the kitchen doorway with his backpack still on. “I heard him.”

Carmen had worked a nine-hour shift at the medical supply warehouse and her feet were a specific kind of sore that made everything sound like a demand.

“Wash your hands.”

He did not move.

“Milo.”

“I heard him.”

“There’s a pit under that floor,” she said. “It’s a mechanic thing. It’s empty. Grandpa filled the whole thing with junk in 2009 and hasn’t opened it since.”

“Then something in the junk was breathing.”

She laughed. She would think about that laugh for the rest of her life.

“Go wash your hands.”

He had been going to the shop after school since he was seven, because the shop was four blocks from the school and Carmen’s shift ended at six. Otis let him sit on an upturned bucket and hand him sockets. The arrangement had survived a divorce, two layoffs, and the year Otis stopped being able to lift a transmission by himself.

On Tuesday, Otis was not there.

Dean was.

Dean, who had not been to the shop on a weekday in eleven years, standing in the office doorway with his keys in his hand, saying Pop had a bad night and the VA kept him for observation.

Milo asked which VA.

Dean said the one over in Clairton.

Milo said Grandpa hates that one.

Dean said people don’t get to pick, kid.

The second time Milo said it, Carmen was on the phone with the landlord.

He waited until she hung up. She gave him credit for that, later, when she was tallying up everything she had refused to give him credit for at the time.

“I put my ear on it. There’s a person.”

“Milo, stop.”

“I knocked twice and something knocked back.”

“Stop.”

“Why won’t you —”

“Because your grandfather is in a hospital bed,” she said, “and I have eight hundred dollars and a brother who wants to sell his shop, and I cannot do this with you tonight.”

He stood there.

“Okay,” he said.

He went to his room. He did not slam the door, and she remembered noticing that he did not slam the door and feeling relieved that the conversation was over.

She had tried to call the VA once, on Wednesday morning, from the warehouse loading dock.

She got a menu. She pressed three. She got a different menu. A woman asked for the patient’s full name and date of birth and then asked whether Carmen was listed on the release form, and Carmen said she was his daughter, and the woman said that wasn’t the same thing, ma’am.

Her break was eleven minutes long.

She hung up.

That is the whole of it. That is the entire size of the crack her father fell through. A phone menu and a break that was eleven minutes long and a woman who was only doing her job correctly.

On Thursday morning Dean called and said Ms. Odell could meet them at four, and Carmen said fine, because the shop had not turned a profit since 2019, because her father was seventy-six, because Dean had a folder of numbers and she had eight hundred dollars, and because the alternative was a conversation she did not have the energy to have.

Milo said nothing at breakfast.

He asked to come along to the shop.

She said yes, because it was easier than a sitter.

He took his backpack.

She did not think to wonder why he needed his backpack at a place he went every day of his life.

Inside it, wrapped in his gym shirt so it would not bang against his spine on the walk, was a flashlight and a hammer.
