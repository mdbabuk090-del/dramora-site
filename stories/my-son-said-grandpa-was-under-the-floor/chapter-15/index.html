My Son Said Grandpa Was Under the Floor

Chapter 15 — The Name on the Shirt

Chapter 15 of 15

Dean took eleven years.

Unlawful restraint of a person over sixty-five, forgery of a legal instrument, conspiracy, and two counts under the elder abuse statute. The judge said several things during sentencing that Carmen expected to remember and did not. The only line that stayed with her came near the end, when the judge looked up from her notes and said that what the court found most difficult about the case was the water jug.

Carmen thought: yes.

Otis did not attend.

He was asked, and he said no, and when Carmen asked him why, he said, “Because he’d look at me,” and that was the last conversation they ever had about it.

The shop stayed.

That surprised people. Carmen got asked about it at the warehouse, at the school gate, once by a woman in a grocery aisle who did not introduce herself. After all that, you kept it?

She kept it.

Not out of principle. She kept it because on the second Saturday her father got out of the hospital he made her drive him over, and he sat on the upturned bucket in the middle of the bay with the roll-up door open and the orange light coming in, and he did not say a word for twenty minutes, and then he said:

“Fifty-one years and I never once thought about that hole.”

“Dad —”

“Fill it in,” Otis said.

They did. In November, a concrete truck came out and two men from Hazelton Ready-Mix pumped four and a half cubic yards into the service pit at 2209 Franklin Street, and Otis Kessler stood over it with his hands in his pockets and watched the dark go away.

There is a flat grey rectangle in that floor now, a slightly different shade from everything around it.

Milo works on top of it.

The shop is his three afternoons a week, officially, which means his grandfather sits on the bucket and tells him what to do and he does it wrong and then does it right.

He is thirteen. He can do brakes and an oil change and he is not allowed near the torch.

He asked, once, whether it bothered his grandfather to stand there.

Otis said, “I stood on it for fifty-one years before anybody was under it.”

In the spring, Carmen took the blue mechanic’s shirt in to be repaired.

It had a name patch on the chest — an oval one, the kind with the stitched border — and the thread had gone. It had been coming apart for years and then it had spent two days on the floor of a pit, and by the time Carmen got it back from evidence there was nothing left in that oval but the ghost of where letters used to be.

The woman at the alterations place asked what name.

Carmen stood at the counter and looked at the little order form.

She thought about the parcel map with the red line, and the woman with twenty-six years and a licence, and her brother saying he was never going to sign in a voice that expected to be understood.

“Kessler,” she said.

Then: “Wait.”

She turned the form around and wrote it herself, in the box, in block capitals.

KESSLER & GRANDSON

“That’s a long one,” the woman said. “It’s going to be small.”

“That’s fine,” Carmen said. “He knows what it says.”
