My Son Said Grandpa Was Under the Floor

Chapter 04 — Let Go of Me

Chapter 4 of 15

“That’s enough!”

Carmen hauled him up and backward by the shoulders.

She was not rough. She wanted that recorded somewhere. She was not rough with him, she was finished, and there is a difference that only matters to the person doing the hauling.

“Let go of me!”

Milo twisted in her hands. His face had gone red and screwed up, and his heels skidded on the concrete, and he fought her with a strength she did not know he had.

The pry bar came out of his fingers and hit the floor.

It landed flat, three feet from the exposed plate, and rolled a quarter turn and stopped.

Nobody picked it up.

Carmen got him back a full stride and held him there.

“You are done,” she said into the back of his head. “Do you hear me? You are done for today.”

He did not answer.

He did not look at her.

She turned him partly around and his eyes went past her shoulder and locked onto the floor again, and she moved her head to catch his line of sight, and he simply looked past her a second time.

Her son had been looking her in the eye and lying about brushing his teeth since he was four years old.

He would not look at her now.

Something in Carmen’s chest tipped over.

Her grip loosened without her deciding to loosen it. The anger was still there — she could feel it, all that hot useful fuel — but it had nothing to burn against anymore, because the boy in her hands was not being defiant.

He was being urgent.

“Milo.”

“He’s down there,” he said, to the floor.

“Sweetheart —”

“He’s down there and you won’t look.”

Behind them, Ms. Odell had not moved.

Carmen registered it the way you register furniture. The woman was standing on the shop-side of the exposed patch, three feet clear of the plate, hands loose at her sides. She had been at the workbench thirty seconds ago.

She was not standing between them and the floor. Not exactly. If you drew the line on paper you would have said she was simply standing somewhere.

But if Milo tore free and ran, he would have to go around her.

Her eyes were down.

They stayed down. Through the shouting, through the clatter of the bar, through all of it, Ms. Odell looked at a spot on the concrete about four feet in front of her shoes and did not lift her head once.

A woman had driven out here to sell a building. She had a folder and city shoes and brake dust on them. There was a child screaming in the room and she would not look at him.

Carmen filed it. She did not know she was filing it. It went into the same drawer as the wiped-clean workbench and the warmth she had not felt and the phone menu on Wednesday morning, and the drawer was getting full.

“Carm,” Dean said. “Let me talk to him.”

“No.”

It came out before she could shape it into anything polite.

Dean stopped where he was.

For a fraction of a second, her brother looked at her with an expression she had never once seen on him — not at their mother’s funeral, not at the reading of the will, not the night he told her the shop was going to eat their father alive.

He looked at her like she was a door that had just locked.

Then it was gone.

“All right,” he said, easy. “All right.”

He put his hands up, palms out, the way you do at a traffic stop.

And he turned toward her, and he began to speak, and his voice was warm and reasonable and completely steady.
