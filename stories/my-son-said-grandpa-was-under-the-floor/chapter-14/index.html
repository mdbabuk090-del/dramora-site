My Son Said Grandpa Was Under the Floor

Chapter 14 — I Told Everybody

Chapter 14 of 15

They put Milo in a room with a soft chair and a camera in the corner, and a woman named Denise sat across from him with a paper cup of apple juice she had brought without asking.

Carmen watched it on a monitor down the hall with a victim advocate beside her.

“You’re not in any trouble,” Denise said.

“I know.”

“Do you know why you’re here?”

“Because of my grandpa.”

“That’s right.”

Milo sat forward with his elbows on his knees, which is how his grandfather sits.

“Can you tell me how you knew he was under the floor?”

“I heard him.”

“What did you hear?”

“The first time it was like —” He stopped. He thought about it seriously, the way he thinks about everything, and Carmen watched her son take his time in front of a camera. “Like when someone moves in the next room and you don’t hear the person, you hear the floor.”

“When was that?”

“Tuesday. After school.”

“What did you do?”

“I told Uncle Dean.”

Carmen’s hand closed on the arm of the chair.

She had not known that.

“What did he say?”

“He said it was the water heater.”

Denise wrote something down. “Is there a water heater?”

“Yeah,” Milo said. “It’s on the other side. By the office.” He shrugged one shoulder. “I checked.”

“And then what did you do?”

“Wednesday I put my ear on it. And I knocked twice.”

“What happened?”

“It knocked back.”

“How did that feel?”

Milo looked at her.

“Bad,” he said.

“Why bad?”

“Because then it was real and I had to make somebody believe me.”

“So who did you tell?”

“Everybody.”

“Can you say their names?”

“I told Uncle Dean. I told my mom on Tuesday and Wednesday. I told my mom’s friend Reyna on the phone. I told Mr. Alvarez.”

“Who’s Mr. Alvarez?”

“He’s my teacher.”

Denise’s pen stopped.

“You told your teacher?”

“On Wednesday. At lunch.”

“What did you say?”

“I said there’s a man under the floor at my grandpa’s shop.” Milo’s ears had gone red. “He said that was a good story and I should write it down for creative writing.”

In the room down the hall, Carmen made a sound and the victim advocate put a hand on her arm.

“Did you write it down?” Denise asked.

“Yeah.”

“Why?”

“Because he said to.”

They found the exercise book in his backpack.

One and a half pages in pencil, dated Wednesday, headed THE MAN UNDER THE FLOOR, in which a boy named Miles hears knocking in his grandfather’s garage and nobody in the whole town believes him.

It was submitted into evidence.

Mr. Alvarez had marked it in green pen.

Great tension! Watch your paragraph breaks. 8/10.

“Milo,” Denise said, near the end. “Can I ask you one more thing?”

“Okay.”

“Everybody said no. Your uncle, your mom, your teacher. Everybody told you that you were wrong.”

“Yeah.”

“Why did you go back on Thursday?”

Milo looked at the corner of the room for a second.

Then he said, “Because he was still down there.”

Denise waited.

“It didn’t matter that nobody believed me,” he said. “It only mattered that he was still down there.”

Carmen drove him home.

Neither of them said anything for eight blocks.

Then she pulled the car over on Franklin, two hundred feet short of the shop, and put it in park, and sat with both hands on the wheel.

“Milo.”

“Yeah.”

“I told you no. Twice.”

He didn’t say anything.

“I want to say it out loud,” Carmen said, “and I don’t want you to make it okay for me. You can just let me say it.”

She looked over at him.

“You told me the truth and I didn’t listen. That was mine. It wasn’t yours, and it wasn’t because you said it wrong. I just didn’t listen.”

Milo looked out the windscreen at the shop.

“Okay,” he said.

And then, after a while: “You listened on Thursday.”

“Not until you made a hole in the floor.”

“Yeah,” her son said. “Well.”
