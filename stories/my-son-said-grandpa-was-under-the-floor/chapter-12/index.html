My Son Said Grandpa Was Under the Floor

Chapter 12 — The Woman Who Never Spoke

Chapter 12 of 15

Ms. Odell’s lawyer called her a transactional participant.

Carmen learned this from a hallway outside courtroom 4B, where she was not supposed to be standing, and she wrote the phrase down on the back of a parking receipt because she wanted to look at it later and be sure she had not made it up.

The woman had turned herself in on the second day.

She had come in with counsel and a printed timeline and an offer, and she had talked for six hours, and by the end of it the district attorney had a case that did not depend on a ten-year-old boy.

Carmen saw her once.

It was in the elevator lobby on the third floor, and there was nowhere for either of them to go, and they stood eight feet apart for the ninety seconds it took a county elevator to arrive.

Marjorie Odell was smaller than Carmen remembered. Grey blazer. No jewellery. She was holding her handbag with both hands in front of her like a schoolgirl.

“Mrs. Kessler.”

“Ms. Kessler.”

“Ms. Kessler.” She swallowed. “I want you to know that I did not know he was down there.”

Carmen looked at her.

“On the Thursday,” she said. “When my son was hitting the floor with a bar. You looked at my brother instead of my son.”

Ms. Odell did not answer.

“When my boy was screaming, you stood in front of the tyres.”

“I stood where I was standing.”

“You looked at the ground for four minutes.”

“I did not know he was down there,” Ms. Odell said again, and her voice did something on the last word.

Carmen waited.

“I thought,” the woman said, “that your brother had put your father somewhere.”

There it was.

Somewhere.

She had not known which hole. She had known there was a hole. She had gone home on Tuesday night, and Wednesday night, and slept, and driven back out to Franklin Street on Thursday with a folder under her arm and a pen ready, and she had made her peace with somewhere.

The elevator came.

Neither of them got in.

“I have a licence,” Ms. Odell said, to nobody. “Twenty-six years.”

“My father has a rib,” Carmen said, and walked to the stairs.

She pleaded to conspiracy to commit forgery, unlawful restraint as an accessory, and two counts under the elder abuse statute.

She was sentenced to four years, of which she served twenty-two months.

Her licence was revoked, and the revocation notice was published in a monthly bulletin that Carmen subscribed to for one year and then, deliberately, unsubscribed from, because she had started checking it on Sunday mornings and did not like what that was doing to her.

The LLC in Delaware was never charged with anything.

Carmen went to two meetings about that and understood, by the end of the second one, that a company is not a person you can put in a room. There were no charges because there was nothing to charge — a buyer had signed a purchase agreement for a property, at a price it offered, contingent on clean title. That is not a crime. That is Tuesday.

Somebody in an office two states away had drawn a red line around her father’s shop on a parcel map and priced the access, and everything that happened in that pit had grown out of that red line like a weed out of a crack.

They never even had to know.

“Doesn’t that make you crazy?” her friend Reyna asked her.

Carmen thought about it.

“No,” she said. “Dean knew.”

That was the part she could not get around, and she stopped trying to.

Nobody in Delaware told her brother to shut the doors.
