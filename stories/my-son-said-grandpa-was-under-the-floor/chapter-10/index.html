My Son Said Grandpa Was Under the Floor

Chapter 10 — The Things He Left Down There

Chapter 10 of 15

The paramedics brought Otis up on a stair chair.

He weighed nothing. Carmen stood back against the workbench with her arms crossed over her stomach and watched two young men in navy blue carry her father up out of a hole, and the second one said nice and easy, sir, and Otis said son, I built these steps, and everybody laughed, and Carmen had to walk outside.

They cut the cloth strip off his wrists in the ambulance with scissors, an inch from the knot, and dropped it in a bag.

The officer with the camera went down into the pit for nineteen minutes.

Carmen made herself go and look, once, before they taped it off.

There was a folded moving blanket on the bottom step.

There was a gallon jug of water, half gone, with a bendy straw in it.

There were four granola bars, two eaten. A plastic bucket in the far corner with a towel over it. A cushion off the office chair.

And there was a shop light on an extension cord, clamped to a pipe, switched off at the wall.

Carmen looked at the switch.

The officer followed her eyes.

“Somebody was coming down here,” he said.

“Yes.”

“Regular, by the look of it.”

“Yes,” Carmen said.

She had thought, for about ninety minutes, that the worst thing in the world was her brother locking their father in a hole.

It was not.

The worst thing in the world was the water jug. Because a man who throws his father in a pit in a rage is a man who lost control for four seconds. A man who brings down a blanket, and a cushion, and a straw so the old man can drink with his wrists tied, and a light he can switch on and off from up here —

That man is not out of control.

That man is managing an asset.

He was keeping her father alive and comfortable and quiet until Carmen signed.

“Ma’am,” the officer said. “Do you need to sit down?”

At the hospital they found dehydration, a urinary infection, bruising on both wrists, and a hairline fracture in his left seventh rib that was nine or ten days old.

Nine or ten days.

“That’s before Tuesday,” Carmen said.

The doctor looked at her chart and then at her.

“Yes.”

Otis could not talk about it for two days.

He talked about everything else. He talked about the shop and the Ferguson tractor and whether Milo had eaten. He asked four separate times whether somebody had put the tools away.

On the third morning Carmen came in with coffee and he was awake and looking at the ceiling.

“It was the tenth,” he said.

She sat down.

“He came out on the tenth with a folder,” Otis said. “Wanted me to sign the shop over so he could ‘handle the sale properly.’ I said no. He got a hold of my arm and I went into the corner of the bench.”

He touched his own ribs, mildly, like a man checking a tyre.

“He apologised. Cried about it, matter of fact.”

“And Tuesday?”

“Tuesday he brought the woman.”

Otis’s hands were flat on the blanket. The bruising had gone yellow-purple all the way round both wrists like bracelets.

“I told them both to get off my property. Dean asked me to come down in the pit and look at a crack in the wall.” The old man’s mouth moved. “Fifty-one years I’ve been going down those steps.”

Carmen could not speak.

“He didn’t push me,” her father said. “He just shut the doors.”

“Dad —”

“I heard the boy on Wednesday.” Otis turned his head on the pillow and looked at his daughter, and his eyes were wet and absolutely clear. “Knocked twice. I knocked back, hard as I could. Then I heard him go and tell you all, and I heard you tell him no.”

Carmen put her face in her hands.

“Don’t,” Otis said. “Don’t you do that. He came back, didn’t he.”
