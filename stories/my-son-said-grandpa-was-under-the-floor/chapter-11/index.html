My Son Said Grandpa Was Under the Floor

Chapter 11 — The Folder He Left Behind

Chapter 11 of 15

Ms. Odell had walked out of that shop and left the folder on the workbench.

Carmen thought about that for four days.

Then she called the detective and asked whether anyone had actually read it, and there was a pause on the line of the kind that tells you the answer is not yet, and Carmen said she would come in.

They gave her a room with a table and a copy.

The top document was a purchase agreement for 2209 Franklin Street. Six hundred and forty thousand dollars.

Carmen sat back in the chair.

The shop had been appraised in 2021 at one hundred and eighty-five.

She turned the page.

The buyer was not a person. It was three initials and the letters LLC, registered in Delaware, and the signature block on the buyer’s side was already filled in, dated eleven days before Ms. Odell first drove out to the shop.

They had a buyer before they had a seller.

Underneath the agreement was a power of attorney.

Otis Ray Kessler, granting his son Dean Robert Kessler full authority over the disposition of real property.

Dated the twelfth of August.

Signed.

Carmen looked at her father’s signature for a long time.

She had seen it on birthday cards and the back of cheques and the bottom of a bank envelope she was not supposed to have opened when she was fifteen. Otis signed with a hard downstroke on the K and a flat line under the whole name, one stroke, always, because he had learned to sign on invoices in triplicate.

There was no flat line.

The K was right. Somebody had practised the K.

“That’s not his,” she said.

The detective made a note.

“And the witness?”

Carmen turned the page sideways to read the block at the bottom.

Witnessed this day by: and then a signature, small and neat and entirely legible, the way a person signs when they sign forty documents a week and want no ambiguity about it.

M. Odell.

She had signed her own name to it.

Not initials. Not a scrawl. Her full professional signature, with her licence number printed underneath in the little box provided, because that is what the box is for, because on the twelfth of August she had not thought of herself as a criminal. She had thought of herself as a real estate agent notarising a document.

“She witnessed a forgery,” Carmen said.

“She witnessed a signature by a man who was, on the twelfth of August, already unable to —”

“No.” Carmen put her finger on the date. “On the twelfth of August my father was in that shop rebuilding a water pump. He’d have signed it himself if he was going to sign it. He wasn’t in a pit yet. He was just *saying no.*”

The room was quiet.

“She sat across a table from my father saying no,” Carmen said, “and then she witnessed his name on a piece of paper anyway.”

There was one more thing in the folder.

A single sheet at the back, printed off a county website, with a map on it.

A parcel map of six blocks either side of Franklin. Eleven lots outlined in blue and one lot outlined in red, and the red one was 2209.

Somebody had written on it in ballpoint, in a small tidy hand.

Access — no alt route. Priced accordingly.

Carmen sat and looked at that for a while.

Her father’s shop was not worth six hundred and forty thousand dollars. Her father’s shop was worth six hundred and forty thousand dollars to somebody who owned the other eleven lots and could not get a truck to any of them without going through it.

The whole thing was a driveway.

Her father had spent fifty-one years going down those steps, and her brother had put him under them, and none of it had been about the shop at all.

It had been about the way in.
