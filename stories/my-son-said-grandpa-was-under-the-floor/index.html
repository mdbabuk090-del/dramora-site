My Son Said Grandpa Was Under the Floor

For two days the boy said he heard someone below the concrete. Every adult in that shop had a reason not to look.

Chapter 01 — The Pen

Chapter 1 of 15

The pen came across the workbench first.

Ms. Odell held it out the way people hold a door for someone carrying groceries. Helpful. Patient. Already certain of the outcome.

Carmen Kessler looked at it.

Behind the pen lay a stack of paper on the steel bench her father had used for forty years to press bearings. Somebody had wiped the oil off the surface. That detail sat wrong in her chest and she could not say why.

“It’s the standard package,” Ms. Odell said.

Dean’s index finger came down on the last page.

Right on the line.

“Here,” her brother said. “That’s all it is, Carm.”

The shop smelled the way it always had. Cold rubber. Hot dust on the fluorescent tubes. Under the half-open bay door, late orange light lay across the concrete in long blades.

Carmen reached for the pen.

Then the whole building rang.

CLANG.

Milo had a pry bar over his head.

Her son was crouched on the bare floor twenty feet away, where the first stack of tyres used to be. He had rolled it aside sometime in the last ten minutes and nobody had stopped him. His ear was turned down toward the concrete. His arms came up again.

CLANG.

“Milo!”

Her hand stopped short of the pen.

He did not look at her.

That was the part that landed. Not the noise. Her son heard his name in her voice and did not turn his head, and Carmen felt her irritation trip over something underneath it.

“I’m sorry,” she said to Ms. Odell. “He’s been —”

She stopped, because she did not have a word for what he had been.

Ms. Odell did not answer. She did not look at Milo either. Her shoulders had gone stiff inside the grey blazer, and her eyes went to Dean and stayed there.

Carmen would remember that later.

Dean was already smiling.

But for one instant before the smile, his face had gone somewhere else entirely. His eyes had jumped straight past his nephew to the patch of floor. Not to the boy. Not to the noise. To the floor.

Then it was gone and he was her brother again, mild and amused, the way he had been at every funeral and every hospital hallway their family had ever produced.

“He’s ten,” Dean said. “He’s bored.”

“He’s wrecking Dad’s floor.”

“That floor’s taken worse.”

Carmen turned back to the bench and her hand did not move.

Two days. That was the thing sitting in her stomach. Two days ago Milo had come into the kitchen and told her there was somebody under the shop, and she had told him to wash his hands for dinner. Yesterday he had told her again and she had raised her voice. This morning he had not said anything at all, which she had taken as the end of it.

He had not stopped believing it. He had stopped telling her.

“Carm.”

Dean tapped the signature line twice.

“Sooner we do this, sooner it’s done.”

Behind him the second stack of tyres stood where it had stood all her life. Four tyres high, black and dusty, against the wall side of the floor.

Milo hit the concrete again.

The sound that came back was not the sound concrete makes.

Carmen did not know yet that she had heard it. It went into her the way a wrong note goes into a room full of people who are not listening to music. But her hand had stopped, and it stayed stopped, and Ms. Odell’s arm was getting tired holding the pen out over her father’s workbench.

“Where did you say Dad was?” Carmen asked.

Dean’s thumb turned the wedding band on his finger.

“The VA,” he said. “I told you. They kept him.”

Synopsis

Carmen Kessler came to her father's auto shop to sign it away. Her brother said their father was resting at the veterans' hospital. Her ten-year-old son said he was in the service pit under the floor. Only one of them was telling the truth, and the pry bar in the boy's hands was about to settle it.
