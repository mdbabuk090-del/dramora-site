My Son Said Grandpa Was Under the Floor

Chapter 07 — Daddy?

Chapter 7 of 15

Never pull with your back, boy. Let the bar do it.

Milo jammed the flat end under the hasp.

He got it in on the second try, seated it hard against the plate, and then he did the thing his grandfather had taught him on a frozen Saturday two winters ago: he did not pull.

He climbed on.

He put his whole sixty-eight pounds onto the free end of that bar and let it fall.

The bolts did not bend. They came out — all four of them, out of concrete eaten soft by forty years of oil and road salt — and the hasp tore off the plate with a shriek and the padlock swung free on the end of it, still locked, still doing its job, attached to nothing.

The doors lifted an inch.

An inch of black.

And out of the black came a sound.

It was low and it was human and it was male, and it was not a word. It was the noise a body makes when it has been trying to make noise for a long time and has almost nothing left.

It went up into the roof of that shop and hung there.

Milo froze with both hands still on the bar.

The strain went out of his face all at once. His eyes went wide — not with terror. Carmen saw them and there was no terror in them at all.

It was the shock of being right.

Two days of it. Two days of a kitchen doorway and a raised voice and go wash your hands, and it came off him in one second, and what was left underneath was a ten-year-old boy in a garage looking at a hole in the floor.

Carmen’s whole body turned.

Everything that had been in her — the irritation, the exhaustion, the eight hundred dollars, the shift on Monday, the pen, the paper, the wiped-clean bench — all of it went out of her like water out of a cracked jug, and what filled the space was one single thing, so cold and so total that she could taste metal.

“Daddy?”

She was down on the concrete. She did not remember kneeling.

“Daddy?”

Behind her, she heard her brother’s outstretched hand come down and touch his own leg.

She did not turn around. Later she would learn from Milo what Dean did in that moment, which was nothing. He stood there with his arm lowering slowly to his side and he closed his eyes for one long beat and then opened them again.

He did not run.

He did not shout.

He waited to see how bad it was going to be.

And Ms. Odell swallowed, hard enough that the boy saw her throat move, and glanced at the half-open roll-up door — at the daylight, at her car, at the four seconds it would take her to be outside — and Dean caught the glance and gave her one short, hard look.

She stopped moving.

Carmen never saw any of it.

“Help me,” she said.

Milo was already helping.

The two of them got their fingers into the gap. The doors were steel plate and they weighed what steel plate weighs, and Carmen felt something tear in her shoulder and did not care, and her son braced his sneakers on the concrete and pulled with a sound coming out of him that was not a word either.

The left door came up.

Then the right.

They went over backward on their hinges and slammed down flat against the floor with a crash that Carmen felt in her teeth.

Late orange daylight fell down the concrete steps into the service pit.

And Carmen Kessler looked into the hole under her father’s shop.
