My Son Said Grandpa Was Under the Floor

Chapter 06 — The Second Stack

Chapter 6 of 15

He hit the tyres with his shoulder.

One stack, four high, black with forty years of shop dust. Milo was ten years old and weighed sixty-eight pounds and he did not so much push it as fall into it.

It went over easily.

Too easily. It had been leaning already — Carmen saw that in the instant it moved, saw that somebody had restacked it in a hurry and not squared it up, and her father had never in his life left a stack of anything unsquared.

The tyres rolled apart across the concrete with a sound like slow thunder.

And the floor changed.

What had looked like plain oil-stained concrete for the last five minutes was not concrete.

The full outline came up out of it all at once, the way a face comes out of a puzzle picture — two horizontal steel doors set flush into the slab, seven feet by four, the same dead grey as everything around them.

Across the seam lay a steel hasp.

Bolted down. Old, corroded, the bolt heads eaten thin.

Hanging from it, a padlock.

Carmen stopped breathing.

She had been in this building her entire life. She had learned to ride a bike in this bay. She had done homework on that workbench. She had watched her father drop down into that pit ten thousand times with a drop light in his teeth, and it had never — not once, not in forty years — had a lock on it.

You do not lock a pit.

There is nothing in a pit to steal.

You lock a pit to keep something from coming out.

“Milo,” she said. “Get back.”

Her voice did not sound like her voice.

Dean did not look at the doors.

Carmen noticed this the way you notice a gunshot.

Every human instinct in the room had turned toward the floor. Her own body had turned toward it. The boy was standing over it. And her brother, four feet away, was looking at Ms. Odell.

Ms. Odell met his eyes.

Half a second.

The colour went out of her face. Carmen watched it happen — actually watched a grown woman’s skin go grey in real time — and then Ms. Odell dropped her gaze to the ground and left it there.

Nobody said anything for one full second.

Which was the loudest second of Carmen’s life.

“Leave it alone.”

Dean was reaching for Milo.

His composure was gone. Whatever had been holding his face together for the last ten minutes had let go all at once, and what was underneath was not anger. Carmen had braced for anger. Anger she could have argued with.

It was fear.

His fingers were shaking. She could see them shaking from six feet away. His breath was coming high and fast in the top of his chest, and his voice, when it came out, was thin and pitched wrong and too quick.

“Milo. Leave it alone.”

“Don’t touch him,” Carmen said.

“Carmen —”

“Don’t you touch him.”

She had put herself in front of her son without any memory of moving.

And she was not looking at the floor anymore. She had turned all the way around, away from the locked doors, away from the padlock, and she was looking at her brother’s face, studying it the way you study an X-ray, going over it feature by feature for the thing that was wrong.

She found it.

It was in his eyes. He was not looking at the pit. He was not looking at his nephew. He was watching her — watching her face — trying to work out how much she had put together.

Her brother was reading her.

Carmen had never been so frightened of anyone in her life.

Behind her, quietly, Milo picked up the pry bar off the concrete where it had fallen.
