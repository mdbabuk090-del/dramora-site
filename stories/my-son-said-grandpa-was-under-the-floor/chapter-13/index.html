My Son Said Grandpa Was Under the Floor

Chapter 13 — My Word and a Ten-Year-Old's

Chapter 13 of 15

He wrote to her three times before she went.

The first letter was four pages and explained everything. The second was two pages and explained everything differently. The third was six lines long and said only that he would like to see her, and that was the one that got her in the car.

They sat on opposite sides of a scratched perspex divider with a phone each.

Her brother had lost weight. It suited him, which Carmen resented.

“Hi, Carm.”

“Dean.”

“How’s Pop.”

“He’s at the shop three days a week. He can’t lift anything. He sits on the bucket and tells Milo what to do.”

Dean smiled at that, and it was almost real, and Carmen had to look at the divider for a second.

“And the boy?”

“He’s fine.”

“Is he.”

“He’s eleven. He sleeps with the hall light on. He didn’t used to.” Carmen let that sit there. “He’s fine.”

Dean nodded slowly.

“I want to tell you about the loan,” he said.

“I know about the loan.”

“You don’t —”

“Ninety-one thousand on a line of credit you guaranteed personally for a man in Ohio who sells parking lot lighting,” Carmen said. “Second mortgage in April. Lorraine found out in June. She took the girls to her mother’s on the ninth of August.”

Dean’s face did not move.

“Three days before Dad’s signature showed up on a power of attorney,” Carmen said.

“So you understand.”

“No.”

“Carm —”

“I understand the sequence,” she said. “Sequence isn’t a reason. A million people owe ninety-one thousand dollars. They get a second job. They tell their sister.”

“I did tell my sister.”

“When.”

“I told you the shop was going to eat him alive.” Dean leaned forward. “In March. On your porch. I said Pop can’t run it and he won’t sell it, and you said —”

He stopped.

“Say it,” Carmen said.

“You said, *then he won’t sell it.*”

She had said that.

She sat in a plastic chair in a visiting room with a phone against her ear and she remembered the porch, and the March cold, and the exact shape of that sentence in her own mouth.

“Yes,” she said. “I did.”

Dean waited for the rest of it. She watched him wait — watched her brother sit there ready to accept the smallest possible share of this, ready to split it with her, right down the middle, you said it too —

“That’s not what you want from me,” Carmen said.

“What?”

“You want me to say we both did it. You brought me here for that.”

Dean sat back.

For a long moment the only thing in his face was the thing she had seen at the edge of the pit — nothing at all, no warmth, no brother, just a man reading a face to see how it was going.

“He was never going to sign,” Dean said.

Quietly. Almost reasonably.

“That’s it, Carm. That’s the whole thing. He was never, ever going to sign, and there was no version of any of it where he did, and I had eleven days.”

Carmen looked at him.

“And what was the plan,” she said, “for after I signed?”

Dean did not answer.

“Because that’s the part I keep on,” she said. “You had a blanket down there. You had a straw. You brought him granola bars. So you weren’t going to let him die.”

“No.”

“So on Friday morning, when it’s sold — what happens? You open the doors and he comes up and he calls the police.”

Dean’s jaw worked.

“Unless you tell everyone he’s confused,” Carmen said. “Unless you’ve spent three weeks telling people he wanders. Which you had.”

She stood up.

“You weren’t keeping him alive, Dean. You were keeping him available.”

She hung the phone up on the hook while he was still talking, and she saw his mouth make her name through the perspex, and she walked out into a parking lot in the rain and sat in her car for forty minutes before she could drive.
