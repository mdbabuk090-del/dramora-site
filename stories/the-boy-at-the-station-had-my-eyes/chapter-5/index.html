Chapter 05 — Five Years of Sundays

Chapter 5 of 17

To understand what came later you have to understand what my mother was to me for five years, and I am not going to make that easy for you, because it was not easy for me.

She was the one who stayed.

Everyone stays for a while. That is the thing nobody warns you about a missing child — the enormous, roaring kindness of the first six weeks, and then the way it drains off. People are extraordinary at a crisis and hopeless at a condition. By the fourth month our friends had gone back to their own lives, which is not a crime, and by the second year the ones who remained had developed a way of talking to me where they never used his name, as though it were a swear word, as though I would not notice.

My mother used his name every single time.

She came on Sundays. Not sometimes — Sundays, the whole five years, in a way you could have set a calendar by. She let herself in at half past eleven with a bag of shopping I had not asked for and did not need, and she cooked in my kitchen, and she washed up afterwards even when I told her not to, and at some point in the afternoon she would say something like, "He liked this one," about a song on the radio, and let it sit there in the air like a normal thing to say. Because it was. That was her gift. She made him a person we were allowed to mention rather than a wound we were tiptoeing around.

She kept his photograph on her mantelpiece the entire time. Not a shrine — one frame, on the end, in the row with all the others, Lily and me and my father and Sam at three with a plastic dinosaur. When people came to her house and asked, she told them. She never once put him in a drawer to spare herself a conversation.

I want that on the record before the rest of it.

The first year I did the things you do. I did the leaflets and the appeals and the anniversary interview with the local paper. I stood outside Hollis Street station on the twenty-fourth of June for four hours with a laminated photograph, and my mother stood next to me for all four hours in the wrong shoes, and when a man walking past said something about how it was probably the father and we should all be honest about that, she told him to move along in a voice I had never heard her use.

The second year I stopped sleeping properly and started sleeping badly on purpose, which is a different thing. I would go to bed at two so I did not have to lie there at eleven.

The third year Peter left.

I am not going to be cruel about Peter. He was Sam's father and he is Lily's father and he was not a bad man; he was a man who could not live inside it. He wanted, very badly, for there to be a point at which we agreed to be sad in a manageable way and get on with the rest of our lives, and I could not give him that, and I do not think I was obliged to, and I also think it broke something in him to keep asking. He moved to his brother's, then to a flat, and now he takes Lily every other weekend and is good to her, and we can talk on the phone about school shoes for eleven minutes without one of us crying, which took us four years to be able to do.

The night he moved out my mother came at nine in the evening without being called and sat on the end of my bed until three in the morning with her coat still on.

The fourth year was the flat one. Nothing happened in the fourth year. That is what nobody puts in the films — that for a whole year there was no development, no sighting, no anniversary appeal because the paper had stopped running them, no calls from the family liaison officer because our case had gone to a central unit and then gone quiet. My son had become an administrative fact. There is a box on a form somewhere with his name in it and that year is what that box actually feels like from the inside.

And it was in the fourth year that my mother said the thing I had been waiting for someone to say.

We were in her kitchen. She had her hands in the sink and she was not looking at me, and she said, "Nora. I am going to say something, and I want you to know it is because I love you."

I said all right.

"I think you should stop."

I did not answer.

"Not stop hoping," she said. "You will never stop hoping and I would not ask you to. But stop looking. Stop the searching. Stop the ringing round and the online groups and the men who want four hundred pounds to check a database. It is eating you and it is eating Lily and I have watched it for four years and I am asking you, as your mother, to put it down."

I remember the water running. I remember the exact shape of her back.

And here is the part I have to write down honestly, because if I do not, none of the rest of this makes sense: I thought she was right.

Not that day. That day I was furious and I drove home too fast. But over the following months I came to think she was right, and I loved her for having the courage to say it, because everyone else in my life had spent four years agreeing with whatever I said in order to avoid the conversation, and my mother had walked into it on purpose.

I did stop, mostly. I let the groups lapse. I stopped opening the alerts. I kept one thing, which was a red plastic folder in the bottom of my wardrobe, and I told myself it was because of the insurance.

So when people say to me now — and they do, they say it with their heads tilted — didn't you ever suspect her? I want to take them by the shoulders.

She did not talk me out of looking for my son once, in a sinister voice, in a locked room.

She held me up for five years and then, at the moment when she had earned the right to be listened to, she used it.

That is the shape of the thing. That is why it took me until the ninth day to see even the first crack, and why the crack, when it came, was not in her. It was in a piece of paper.

I should put down what she was like about the boy in the hospital, too. Because I have been asked about that as well, in rooms with tables, by people whose job it was to ask.

She came the second morning.

She stood in the doorway of that room for a long moment before she went in, and she had one hand up at her throat, and she said, "Oh," and it came out of her like she had been hit.

Then she crossed the room and she sat down in the chair by the bed and she took his hand — the one without the cannula — and she said, "Hello, sweetheart."

He let her. He let her before he let me, in fact, which I noticed and put away.

She was wonderful with him for eleven days. She brought him a jigsaw and did not fuss when he would not do it. She learned in about ninety seconds that he did not like being asked questions, and so she talked at him instead, about nothing, about the traffic and a woman in her building and a dog she had seen, and he lay there and let it wash over him and once, on the fourth day, he laughed.

She fed him. She read the hospital menu out loud in different voices. She cut his food up small without making a thing of it, because his arm was weak on the left side and neither of us mentioned that either.

And when my legs went in that family room on the Thursday afternoon, it was my mother who took my weight, and she was crying so hard that a detective had to go and find her a chair.

I believed every second of it, and here is the thing I have had to sit with since: I was not wrong to.

She was not performing. That is what people cannot get their heads round and what I have given up trying to explain at dinner tables. My mother was not standing in that corridor holding me up while secretly congratulating herself. She was destroyed. She had been destroyed for five years.

She just was not destroyed by the same thing I was.

There is one more thing from those eleven days and I did not think about it once at the time.

On the fifth or sixth day I came back from the machine with two coffees and stopped outside the door because she was talking to him.

She had his hand in both of hers and she was leaning in close, and she was saying, "You are safe now. You are safe now, and none of it was your fault, and nobody is angry with you. Nobody is angry with you, darling."

Which is exactly the right thing to say to a child who has been taken.

I stood in the corridor with two hot cups and my eyes stinging and I thought: thank God he has her.

It was months before it occurred to me to wonder how she knew, in the first week, before he had told anybody anything, that there was something he might think was his fault.
