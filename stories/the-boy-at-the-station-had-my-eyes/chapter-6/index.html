Chapter 06 — The Twenty-Fourth of June

Chapter 6 of 17

I have to do the twenty-fourth of June, and I have been putting it off for six chapters.

I have told it perhaps two hundred times. To police, twice to a solicitor, once to a journalist I should not have let in the house, and to my mother more times than to anybody, because she was the person I told it to when I could not sleep. It has become a thing I recite. I can do it now in about ninety seconds without any part of me being present, which frightens me more than the day itself does.

So I am going to do it slowly and properly, once, and then I am going to leave it alone.

It was a Wednesday and it was hot, and I was not well.

I am going to leave that there for now. There is a fuller account of what I mean by it further on, and it costs me something to write, and it belongs with the reason all of this happened rather than with the day it happened on.

Peter had taken the day off to take Lily to a hospital appointment about her ears, and it was routine, and it took the whole day the way those things do. So it was me and Sam.

We were meant to be going to my mother's. That was the arrangement, and it had been the arrangement for about a fortnight — I was to bring Sam over in the afternoon and stay for tea, and she would have him overnight so that I could sleep, because I had not slept properly since about the March, and this had been discussed between us many times and there was nothing whatsoever unusual about it.

At ten past one she rang and cancelled.

She said she had come down with something. She said it was probably nothing but she was not having a four-year-old in the house with it, and I said fine, and I was disappointed in a low, tired way that I did not examine, because I had been looking at that night on the calendar for two weeks.

I have thought about that phone call approximately one million times.

It is not evidence of anything. She has never been charged with anything to do with that phone call and her solicitor would say, correctly, that a woman is allowed to be ill, and Reyes told me flatly that I would drive myself mad with it and that she had seen mothers do exactly that over smaller things.

But it moved us. That is the fact of it. If she had not rung at ten past one, my son and I would have spent that afternoon in her front room on Ardley Row.

Instead we went into town.

We went in on the bus because the car was making a noise.

We did the library, which was mine, and we did the pet shop window, which was his, and I bought him a carton of juice and he spilled about a third of it down himself, and I was tired in a way where that felt like an enormous event and I was short with him about it, and he went quiet for a bit and then he was fine.

He was four years and three months. He was wearing a navy duffel coat because it had been cold in the morning and had then turned hot, and he would not let me carry it, and he would not put it on. He carried it, bunched up under one arm, all afternoon, and it dragged, and I said, "You'll get it filthy," and he said, "It's MY coat."

That is the last thing my son said to me for five years and four months that I can be certain of.

There is one more sentence and it is in every account and I am not sure of it, and I want to say why. I have him saying, at the barrier, "Can we go on that one," about a train. I have said it in interviews. But I have also said it so many times that I no longer know whether I am remembering my son or remembering the sentence, and there was a psychologist who explained to me exactly how that happens, and she was not being unkind.

So: the coat. He carried the coat and told me it was his.

Hollis Street at twenty to five on a hot Wednesday in June is a great many people.

We were on the concourse. We had about eleven minutes and I had bought the tickets and I had one of those tired headaches where the light comes in wrong, and I was standing near the machines looking up at the board because our platform had not come up yet.

He was at my left hand.

I want to say something here that I have never been able to say properly in any of the two hundred tellings, because in a police interview you are answering a question and the question is always where was he, what did you see, how long.

Nobody has ever asked me what I was thinking about.

I was thinking about a Tuesday appointment I did not want to go to, and about the fact that my mother had cancelled, and about whether Peter would be back before eight, and underneath all of it, in the way that these things sit underneath, I was thinking: I cannot do this for very much longer.

That is what was in my head. Not for a second and not as a plan. As a weather.

I have said that sentence out loud exactly three times in my life. Once to my GP, once to my mother on the phone from a car park in the May, and once, four years later, to a woman in a car park in the rain who already knew.

He went at some point between 16:41 and 16:44. That is as close as anybody has ever got it.

I did not feel a hand leave mine. I have to say that plainly because it is the thing everyone wants — the moment, the tug. There was no moment. I was looking up at a board and then I looked down and the space at my left hand had a different shape in it.

The first thirty seconds I was not frightened. That is normal, apparently. You look at the obvious places with a mild irritation and you say the name in an ordinary voice.

The next thirty seconds I was frightened.

Then I was on the concourse turning round and round and shouting, and a man in a hi-vis came over, and after that it goes into the version I have recited two hundred times: the announcement, the barriers, the two transport officers, the woman who said she thought she had seen a little boy in a blue coat by the ticket office, the arrival of proper police at eleven minutes past five, the search of the platforms, the search of the train that had gone at 16:47 and was stopped at Marbury, my shoe coming off on the stairs and me not going back for it.

I rang Peter at 17:20.

I rang my mother at 17:26.

She answered on the second ring and I could not get the words out, and when I did she said, "Where are you," and I said Hollis Street, and she said, "Don't move. I'm coming now."

She was there in thirty-five minutes.

She was not ill.

I did not think about that once for five years. Not once. She turned up at that station in thirty-five minutes flat and she took Lily off Peter and she rang everybody who had to be rung and she stood next to me until two in the morning, and it did not cross my mind on that night or on any night since — until a woman in a green coat appeared in eleven pixels at the edge of a photograph — that a woman who was too ill to have her grandson at one o'clock was well enough to drive across a county at half past five.

There is one more thing about that day and it belongs to Sam, not to me, and he gave it to me in the second spring, unprompted, in the car.

We were talking about something else entirely. And he said, "I know I went with someone."

I kept my eyes on the road.

He said, "I don't remember him. I remember I wasn't scared." And then, after a while: "That's the bad bit, isn't it."

I said it was not a bad bit. I said he was four.

And he said, "He said Thursday."

Which is when I had to indicate and pull into a lay-by, because I had heard that word before, out of the mouth of a retired school secretary in Coldbrook, about a boy in a reception class who told a crying girl that his mum was coming back on Thursday and said it for a fortnight and then stopped.

A stranger does not say Thursday.

A stranger says your mum's asked me to fetch you, and a four-year-old screams, and it is a scene in a railway station with three hundred witnesses.

Somebody gave my son a specific day, because that is the only thing that works on a four-year-old, because you cannot say soon to a four-year-old, they will ask again in eleven minutes.

Somebody who had had a four-year-old.
