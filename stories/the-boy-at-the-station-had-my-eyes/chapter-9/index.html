My Daughter Gave Her Scarf to a Freezing Boy. He Had My Eyes.

Chapter 09 — The Four Notes

Chapter 9 of 17

Walter Bray took eleven days to find, and he had been dead since February, and he lived four hundred metres from the platform where my son sat down.

Sam gave him to us himself, in pieces, over about a week, once he understood that nobody in the room was going to be angry about anything he said. That took a while. The first few days he answered questions the way you hand over a wallet.

What broke it open was that he asked for a radio.

He was off the sofa and into the small back bedroom by then, which had taken six weeks, and he could not sleep in the quiet. He had spent five years sleeping where there was noise, and my flat at two in the morning is silent, and the silence kept him awake and made him strange in the mornings. So I dug out my father's old set and put it on the shelf by his bed and showed him the dial, and he lay there in the dark and turned it down until it was almost nothing, and then he slept eight hours for the first time.

The next morning at the table, out of nowhere, he said, "Walter had one of them. But bigger."

I did not look up from the toast. I had learned that much.

"Did he," I said.

"He kept it on for the shipping," he said. "It's boring. It's a man saying names."

And that was the beginning of it.

Here is what I know now, assembled out of a nine-year-old's fragments and the housing office's file and two conversations with a woman called June who runs the laundrette on Vernon Road.

After Hattie Pell passed him on — she used those words twice, and one day I will stop hearing them — he was with a man for about a year whose name we still do not have, in a flat above a shop on the other side of the city. That year is a hole. Sam does not talk about it and I do not ask, and Reyes tells me the day he wants to say something he will say it, and until then it is his and not mine.

He ran away from that flat in the winter he was seven. He was not looking for me. I want to be honest about that as well; it would be a better story if he had been. He was seven and he went where there were people and lights and a roof, and that is the station.

He lived around Hollis Street for the best part of two years.

Not in it. They move you on. He slept in a service alley behind the parade of shops on Vernon Road where the vents from the laundrette come out, and that is where June started leaving him things, and she is the second person in this story who did what she could without knowing what it was part of.

And it is where Walter Bray found him.

Walter was seventy-one. He had been a signalman for thirty-four years and he had a ground-floor flat in a block behind the station with a window that looked out at a wall. His wife had died in 2019. He did not have children. He drank, not enormously, steadily. Reyes ran him properly, because you have to, and there was nothing — nothing at all, not a thing, in seventy-one years.

He let a seven-year-old sleep on his settee for the last two winters of his life and he did not tell anybody.

I have gone round and round about that and I have landed somewhere I did not expect. He should have rung someone. Of course he should have rung someone. Every professional I have spoken to says so, and they are right, and there is a version of this where Walter Bray rings social services in the second week and my son comes home two years earlier.

But there is also the version where a man who had already been passed on twice gets passed on a third time, and runs again, and is not sitting on that bench in November.

I met Walter Bray's neighbour and I met his radio and I met the chair he sat in. I never met him. He died on the twelfth of February of a stroke, on his own, and was found on the fourteenth, and my son was outside for the last nine months of his life because after that there was nowhere.

Two things Walter did.

The first is the name.

Sam came to that flat calling himself Eli, because that is what the man above the shop had called him and by then he had stopped arguing with adults about what he was called. And at some point in the first winter Walter took his coat off him to dry it on the radiator, and he found the label.

I sew name tapes into collars. All of them, every coat, every jumper, all the way round the back of the neck where they cannot come off — not the iron-on ones, the sewn ones, because I have a needle in my hand for eight hours a day and it takes me forty seconds. I did it out of habit and I did it to everything he owned.

The coat he was wearing when he was taken was a navy duffel, and I had sewn a tape into the collar in ordinary block letters, and it said SAM VALE.

That coat was long gone by the time he was seven — outgrown, or taken off him, or lost. But somewhere in the year above the shop he had a bag, and in the bag was the collar of it. Just the collar. He had cut it, or somebody had, and he had kept it for two years without being able to read it properly, because he had been to school for two years out of five and he could not read.

Walter read it to him.

Sam told me this at the kitchen table with his knees up on the chair, and he told it as a small funny story, as the story of a man being surprised.

"He said it wrong first. He said Sammy Vale, like it rhymed. Then he did it right." He was pleased with himself, remembering. "He put it on the shelf. He said don't lose that, that's your name, that's the only bit of you you've got."

That is the only bit of you you have got.

An old signalman with a stroke coming said that to my son over a radiator, and then he called him Sam for two years, every day, until he died. It is the single most important thing anybody did for my child in five years and I cannot thank him and there is nowhere to put it.

The collar is in an evidence bag. I have seen the photograph. My own stitching, forty seconds' work in 2020, six even lines of linen thread.

That is why he said what he said on the platform. Not because he had forgotten me. Because for five years the name Sam had belonged to one dead old man and a strip of navy cloth on a shelf, and then a woman he had never seen before knelt down in the rain and used it.

You know my name?

Of course he asked. It was the only reasonable question in the world.

The second thing Walter did was the tune, and I did not find out about the tune until the end of that week, and it is the one that finished me.

Sam was in the bath. The door was open a foot the way it always has to be. And I was folding things on the landing and I heard him humming, four notes, over and over, the way children do without knowing they are doing it.

Four notes going down.

I stood on that landing with a towel in my hands and I could not move.

It is not a song. There are no words to it and there never were. It is four notes that my mother used to make at me when I was small, that her mother made at her, that is not a lullaby so much as a noise the women in my family make at a child in the dark, and I made it at both of mine from the day they were born, and I had not made it at anything for five years.

I said, through the door, as evenly as I could manage, "What's that you're humming?"

And he said, "I don't know. Walter used to say I did it in my sleep."

He did it in his sleep.

For five years, in a flat above a shop, in an alley behind a laundrette, on a dead man's settee, my son hummed four notes in his sleep that he could not have told you the origin of if you had offered him money, and nobody in the world knew what they were except me.

He does not remember my face. He remembers the smell of orange peel, and blue on the ends of a pair of fingers, and something red, and four notes going down. He remembers the word Thursday, which he does not know that he remembers, and which he says in a particular flat voice, and which we will come to.

And here is the thing that had started, very quietly, to sit wrong with me by the end of that week, though I could not have told you why and I did not say it out loud to anybody, not even Reyes.

Every fragment my son had kept was from inside my house.

Not one of them was from the station. Not one of them was of a stranger, or a car, or a hand pulling him. He had no memory whatsoever of being taken.

Whoever did it, he had gone with them easily.
