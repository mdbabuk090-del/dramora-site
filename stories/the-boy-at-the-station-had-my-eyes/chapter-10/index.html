Chapter 10 — Ardley Row

Chapter 10 of 17

It was a land registry search, and I did not run it, and it was not about my son.

That is the part people find hard to believe. They want the crack to have opened because a mother finally looked at her own mother properly. It did not. It opened because of a solicitor's letter about a shed.

My father died eleven years ago. He left the house on Ardley Row, which he had bought in 1979 and which had a workshop in the garden, and he left it to my mother outright, and there was nothing complicated about it and nobody argued. She sold it about a year after Sam went, and I knew she had sold it, because she told me she had sold it. She said the stairs were too much and the garden had got away from her and she could not be in that house any more with everything that had happened, and she moved into the flat on Bewley Court where she still lives.

I did not go to the sale. I did not go to the clearance. I was in the second year and I was not going anywhere.

What happened in November, three weeks after the station, was that a firm of solicitors wrote to me because the new owners of Ardley Row had discovered that the workshop at the bottom of the garden was not on the title. Some old error from the seventies. They wanted it signed away and they had traced it to my father's estate and therefore, in some technical way, to me.

I did not care about a shed. I signed it. But the letter came with a bundle, the way those letters do, and in the bundle was the register entry, and the register entry has a date on it.

The transfer of 14 Ardley Row completed on the fifth of July.

Sam went missing on the twenty-fourth of June.

Eleven days.

I want to describe what that was actually like, because it was not a thunderclap.

I read the date. I thought: that is wrong, that is the wrong year, they have printed it wrong. I looked at the year. The year was right. I thought: well, she must have had it on the market already, these things take months, of course — completion is the end of a process, not the beginning.

And that is true. That is completely true. A sale that completes on the fifth of July was agreed in the spring.

So I sat down at my own kitchen table at nine in the evening with my daughter asleep and my son asleep and I got out the red plastic folder from the bottom of the wardrobe, which had five years of everything in it, and I went looking for the spring.

I found it in about four minutes, because I am a person who keeps things.

My mother came to my flat for lunch on the eighth of April that year. I know because I write in a diary — not a journal, a work diary, appointments and hours, forty pages of nothing. She came for lunch on the eighth of April and on the ninth I wrote a note to myself to look at flats near her because she had spent the whole of lunch talking about how she was never going to leave Ardley Row, how she would be carried out of it, how my father had put the kitchen in with his own hands.

The eighth of April. Eleven weeks before Sam went, she was telling me she would never sell that house.

And on the fifth of July, eleven days after he was taken, the sale of it completed.

You cannot do that. You cannot agree a sale, do the searches, exchange and complete on a house in England in twelve weeks, not in an ordinary way, not while also burying nothing and telling nobody. It is possible. It is possible if you are motivated and you take a low offer and you push. It is possible in the way that everything is possible.

But she did not tell me she was selling it. In eleven weeks she never mentioned it. She sat in my flat on the eighth of April and told me she would never leave, and eleven weeks later it was sold, and the first I heard of any of it was more than a year afterwards when she said the stairs were too much.

I did not sleep. I would like to say I did something clever and calm.

At about two in the morning I got up and I did the thing I had been sitting there not doing for four hours, which was to work out what the house sold for and what she had.

I could not get the price that night — that came later, and it was thirty-one thousand under the valuation, which is its own sentence. What I could do at two in the morning was something much worse and much simpler.

I could count.

Roy Tarrant paid Hattie Pell in cash, weekly, for about eighteen months. Reyes had given me a figure for what Pell said she got, and Reyes's own estimate of what Tarrant would have taken off the top for himself, and she had said the phrase that's not nothing, that's a sum, and I had not done the arithmetic then because I had no reason to do arithmetic about my mother.

I did it at two in the morning on the back of a solicitor's letter.

It came to somewhere between eleven and nineteen thousand pounds, depending on what you believed about Tarrant.

And I sat in my kitchen and I thought: a woman who was never going to leave that house sold it in a hurry, under value, eleven days after my son disappeared, and moved into a flat, and for five years there was never any money, and I never once asked where it went.

That is not proof. I knew it was not proof at the time and I know it now. There are nine innocent explanations and I made myself write all nine of them on a piece of paper and I have that piece of paper still.

But at some point in the small hours I stopped constructing explanations and started noticing something else, which is that I was constructing them, at speed, one after another, the way you do when you are frightened of a conclusion.

I had never once done that for anybody else in five years.

In the morning I did not ring Reyes.

I want to record that, because it is the least defensible thing I did and I was asked about it directly, twice, once kindly and once not.

I did not ring the detective. I rang my mother, and I asked her to come for lunch on Sunday, the way she had come for lunch on five years of Sundays.

I do not entirely know what I was doing. I think I was giving her the chance to say the innocent thing. I think some part of me believed that if I could hear her say oh, that — God, yes, I'd had it on the market since March, I never told you because you had enough on — then I could put it down and it would be over and we could go back.

She came at half past eleven with a bag of shopping I had not asked for.

Sam was in the front room with the radio on. She went in to him first, the way she always did now, and I stood in my own hallway and listened to her say hello to him and heard him say hello back, and my hands were shaking so much I could not do the button on my sleeve.

We did the lunch. She talked. He ate. Lily talked over both of them.

And afterwards, at the sink, with her hands in the water and her back to me — which is where she has always had her important conversations, and which I had never once thought about until that afternoon — I said, as lightly as I could manage:

"Mum, when did you actually put Ardley Row on the market?"

The water kept running.

"After," she said. "After everything. You know that. I couldn't be in it."

"Only I got a letter about the workshop and the completion date's on it," I said. "The fifth of July."

Her hands stopped.

They stopped for about a second and a half, and then they carried on, and she said, in a perfectly ordinary voice, "Well, then I must have had it on before. I don't remember, love. That whole summer's a fog to me, I couldn't tell you what month anything happened in."

And there it was.

Not a lie about the house. A lie about not remembering.

My mother is the woman who told me, in that same kitchen, four years afterwards, the exact time of day the police came, what I was wearing, which shoe I had lost, that the family liaison officer's name was Gemma and that she had a stud in her nose. She has recited that afternoon to me more times than I can count. She has never once had a fog about that summer. That summer is the most precisely documented eight weeks in my mother's entire life.

I said, "Right," and I dried a plate.

And she said — and this was hers, this was not something I dragged out of her, she volunteered it into the silence over the running water —

"You want to be careful with all this, Nora. You've got him back. Sometimes when people go digging they find things that don't help anybody."

I said, "Like what?"

"I don't know," she said. "I'm only saying."

She left at four. She kissed Lily, and she put her hand on the back of Sam's head on the way past, and she called me sweetheart in the doorway, and she got into her car and drove home the way she had done a hundred times.

I stood at the window and watched her indicate at the end of the road.

Then I went upstairs and got out my phone and I rang Detective Marisol Reyes on a Sunday afternoon, and when she picked up I found I could not say the sentence, and she waited, and eventually I said:

"I need to tell you about a house."
