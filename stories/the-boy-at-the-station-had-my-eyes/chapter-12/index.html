Chapter 12 — What I Let People Think About Peter

Chapter 12 of 17

I rang Peter from the car outside my mother's flat and he answered on the fourth ring and said, "What's happened," because I had not rung him at nine o'clock at night in three years.

I could not do the sentence. I got about four words into it twice.

And Peter, who I had spent a large part of the previous five years being quietly unfair to in my own head, said, "Right. Don't drive. Where are you. Stay there."

He came and got me. He drove me home in my own car with his brother following behind in his, and he sat in my kitchen until one in the morning, and at some point in the middle of it I told him all of it: the completion date, the eleven days, the eighth of April, the fog she suddenly had about the only summer of her life she has ever been able to recite.

He listened to the whole thing without saying anything, which he has never once managed before.

Then he said, "You need to eat something," and made me toast, in my kitchen, where he has not lived for three years, and put it in front of me, and said:

"I always thought it was me."

I want to write this chapter because for five years I let a version of Peter Vale stand that was not accurate, and it was convenient to me, and quite a lot of people believed it, and some of them said it out loud to him.

Here is what actually happened to Peter.

In the first four months he was a suspect. Not formally — nobody ever arrested him and nobody ever charged him with anything — but he was interviewed six times, his car was examined, his phone was taken for eleven days, and the police went to his workplace twice, and the second time they went to his workplace they went to reception and asked for him by name.

That is what happens. It is correct that it happens. In the enormous majority of these cases it is a parent, and if the police did not do that they would be failing every other family, and I have understood this the entire time.

He lost his job in the September. Not because of that, officially.

There was a picture in the paper in the second week, in the appeal, of the two of us on the steps of the police station, and I am in the middle of it and he is slightly behind me, and there was a phrase used underneath it. And for about eighteen months, if you searched his name, that was what you got.

A man at a petrol station told him he hoped he rotted. Somebody spray-painted the word on the door of the flat he moved into.

And I knew about every single one of these things, and I did not, at any point in five years, stand up publicly and say the sentence it was not him.

I told the police it was not him. I told my mother. I did not say it in the interview with the local paper on the first anniversary, and the journalist gave me an opening the size of a barn door, and I did not take it, and I have never satisfactorily explained to myself why not.

I think it was because I was angry with him for leaving. And I think that at some level I wanted somebody other than me to be carrying something.

He did not leave in the third year because he stopped caring.

He left because we had reached a point where the two of us in one flat made both of us worse, and because I had made it clear, over about two years, in a thousand small unmistakable ways, that his grief was the wrong shape.

He wanted to keep Lily's birthday parties. I could not have a party in the house.

He wanted, at the end of the second year, to move — to sell up and go forty miles away and start again, and he had worked it all out, and he sat me down with an actual folder, and I looked at him as though he had suggested a crime. Because it meant leaving the flat that Sam might come back to.

He wanted to take the growth chart off the wall.

Every one of those things was reasonable and every one of them was, to me at the time, an act of abandonment, and I have not been able to hold both of those facts in one hand until quite recently.

The night he moved out, my mother came at nine in the evening without being called and sat on the end of my bed with her coat still on until three in the morning.

I have put that down twice now, in two different chapters, and I meant it warmly both times. I am no longer sure how to hold it. Because the man who left that night had been under suspicion for two and a half years for something my mother had arranged, and she sat on my bed and helped me be angry with him.

I have asked myself whether she did that deliberately. I do not think so. That is the honest answer and it is not a comfortable one. I think she genuinely could not stand to see me on my own, and I think it also happened to suit her that the person the world was looking at was Peter, and I think she never once let herself put those two things in the same sentence.

That is what she is like. That is what she has always been like. My mother has an extraordinary gift for doing the kind thing in a way that also happens to protect her, and for never, ever inspecting the second half of it.

Peter came back into the house in the December.

Not to live. He came for Sam, in the careful, humiliating way you have to when a child does not know you: he came for an hour on a Sunday, and sat in the front room, and did not touch him.

Sam did not remember him at all. Nothing. Not a smell, not a hand, not four notes. Peter worked nights that year and he was out of the house four evenings in five and the boy was four, and there is no fragment of him anywhere in my son.

He handled it better than I would have.

He came every Sunday for eleven weeks and did not once say I'm your dad in a voice that asked for anything back. He brought a football. He was bad at the football on purpose. Somewhere around week seven Sam started waiting by the window at about ten to two.

And in the March, Peter rang me from his car outside and said, in a completely level voice, "He asked me if I looked for him."

I said, "What did you say?"

"I said every day for five years," Peter said. "And then he said, did you look with her, and I said no, not really, not together. And he said, why not."

There was a long pause on that phone call.

Then Peter said, "I didn't have an answer, Nor."

We are not getting back together. I want to say that because people ask, including my own mother-in-law, and because it is the tidy ending that this story is constantly trying to reach for, and it would be a lie.

Too much happened. Not the big thing — we could have survived the big thing. It was the two thousand small days afterwards.

But he has Sam every other weekend now, and Lily too, and last summer he took both of them to the coast for four days and sent me eleven photographs and in the last one my son is asleep in the back of the car with his mouth open and his head against the window and my daughter's coat over him.

And in the January, before all of it went to court, Peter did the thing that I did not do for him.

He rang the local paper — the same paper, and I believe the same journalist — and gave them a statement, and in it he said that at no point had I ever, in public or in private, suggested that he was responsible, and that the coverage in the first year had been wrong, and that the person who had done it had now been charged.

He did not have to. He did it in the week the story broke, when it would do him the least good and cost him the most.

I rang him afterwards and I could not get the sentence out again, and he said, "Yeah. All right. Don't."

Five years I let people think it might have been him.

He got my son a football and did not tell him he was his father until the boy asked.
