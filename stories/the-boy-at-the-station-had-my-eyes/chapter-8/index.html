Chapter 08 — The House with the Red Door

Chapter 8 of 17

I was told not to go to Coldbrook and I went to Coldbrook.

Reyes had made it very clear. She had said, in a car park with the engine running, that if I turned up at that woman's door then every single thing Hattie Pell subsequently said becomes a thing a barrister can wave in the air, and that I would be handing the defence a gift.

She was right, and I went anyway, and I want to set down exactly what I did and did not do, because it mattered later and because I have had to account for it under oath.

I did not knock on the door. I did not speak to her. I did not go onto the property.

I drove to Coldbrook on a Tuesday morning in the third week of November and I parked on the far side of the green and I sat in my car for two hours and forty minutes and looked at a house.

It is an end terrace with a red door and a satellite dish and a low wall, on a curve, with a strip of grass in front of it and a bin that had been out since the collection.

It is an ordinary house. That is the thing I had not prepared for. I had spent five years, when I let myself, imagining the kind of place my son was in, and the imagining always had something wrong with it — a cellar, a locked thing, a smell. What I was looking at was a house with a satellite dish where a woman had put a boy who was not hers into a bedroom and fed him and sent him to school and taken forty pounds a week off a man in cash, and none of it had been dramatic for a single day.

The window on the front upstairs is the small one. Mrs Aldritt had told me which room he had, without my asking, in that kitchen with the register on the table. She said he told her once, in the reception year, that he could see the chip shop sign from his bed and that it was orange, and I sat in my car and looked up and there was a chip shop forty yards down and its sign is orange.

He lay in that room for a year and a half and watched an orange sign.

I was ten minutes' walk from a bus stop with a direct service to the station where I was standing with a laminated photograph on the anniversary.

Forty minutes' drive from my flat. I have said that number a great many times and it has stopped meaning anything, so here is one that has not: in the first year, when I still had the leaflets and the tape and the map on my wall, I had drawn a radius. Everybody does it. You take a compass and you draw the distance a man could get in an hour with a child, and you look at the ring, and you feel sick.

Coldbrook was inside the ring. Of course it was. Everything is inside the ring. That is the whole problem — the ring contains two hundred thousand houses with satellite dishes and a woman who feeds him and sends him to school, and there is no version of walking that ring.

She came out at twenty past twelve.

I had not expected her to and I had not planned for it, and when the red door opened I put both hands on the steering wheel.

She is a heavy woman in her fifties in a fleece. She put the bin bag in the bin and she stood on the step for a moment in the cold with her arms folded, looking at nothing, the way people do, and then she went back in.

She was on the step for perhaps twelve seconds.

I had thought — driving over, and this is the part I am ashamed of — that if I saw her I would know something. That there would be a thing in her face. Five years of imagining a person, and what came out of the red door was a tired woman in a fleece putting a bin bag out, and I sat forty yards away with my hands on the wheel and felt the whole architecture of my anger come down, because there was nothing there to be angry at.

She is not a monster. She is worse than that in a way I have never been able to make anybody understand at a dinner table. A monster would mean somebody wanted him. Nobody wanted him. He was a forty-pound-a-week arrangement with a woman who found him no trouble, and when the forty pounds stopped she kept him for four months on a benefit she was claiming illegally and then she gave him to a man whose surname she says she did not know, and she has never, in any interview, expressed a single sentence of regret that anybody has been able to record.

He was no trouble. That is her line. She has said it three times to police and once, later, in a courtroom.

My son was no trouble. He was seven years old and he had learned to be no trouble, and the reason he had learned it is that when he had been trouble, somewhere, at some point, in a year we still know nothing about, something had happened.

I drove home and I did not tell Reyes for four days, and then I did tell her, because I am not stupid and because it would have come out.

She was angrier than I have ever seen her, which was not very angry, which was worse. She sat in my kitchen and closed her notebook and said, "Right," and then said nothing for a while.

Then she said, "Did she see you?"

"No."

"Did you photograph the house?"

"No."

"Did you post anything, say anything to anybody, put it in one of the groups?"

"No."

"Then it's survivable," she said, and opened the notebook again. And then, after a moment, without looking up: "Which room?"

I said, "Front upstairs. The small one."

And Reyes said, "Yeah," and wrote it down, and I understood that she had already been and had already stood in it, and that she had not told me because it was mine to ask for and not hers to give.

Two things came out of Coldbrook that were not about Hattie Pell.

The first is that Mrs Aldritt's referral — the one she made in the second year, about the arm — went into a system that had my son on it under one name and a missing four-year-old on it under another, and there was never, at any point in five years, a mechanism by which those two records could touch each other.

That is not a person's failure. I have wanted it to be a person's failure very badly. There was an internal review and I was sent the outcome and the sentence I have carried out of it is that the two systems "do not routinely interface". A boy with an untreated broken arm and no birth certificate was in a school for two years and the reason nobody found him is a verb tense in a paragraph about data.

The second thing is smaller and it is the one that actually kept me awake.

Mrs Aldritt had mentioned, in passing, in that kitchen with the register on the table, that somebody had paid for a class photograph.

Not the school's file copy. A print — the eight-by-six they sell to the parents, ordered and paid for in cash at the office window in the November of the first year, for the boy in the back row at the end. There is a form and there is no name on it, only a class and a child. She does not remember taking the money and she has been made to sit and try, twice.

Hattie Pell says it was not her, and on this one thing I believe her, because Hattie Pell has never in her life spent four pounds fifty on something she could not use.

I asked Reyes, in the car, who orders a photograph of somebody else's child.

She said, "Somebody who wants to look at him."

And I said, "Tarrant?"

"Tarrant was paid to make him disappear," Reyes said. "You don't buy a picture of a job."

I did not follow it any further than that, and I want to be honest about why: because at that point in November I still had a list in my head of the people in this story, and it had four names on it, and every one of them was a stranger.

It did not occur to me that there was a fifth.

I found out where that photograph had been for four years on the fourth of December, at ten to seven in the morning, and by then I was not the one who found it.
