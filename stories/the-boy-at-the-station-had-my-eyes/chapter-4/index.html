Chapter 04 — The Room That Was Still His

Chapter 4 of 17

For a long time I intended to leave the homecoming out of this altogether, on the grounds that it belonged to the four of us and to nobody else. I have changed my mind. It is the part that is missing from every version of this story that has ever been told about us, including the ones with a photograph of my front door in them, and its absence is why people think it ended at the station.

We got back at twenty to nine at night.

I had spent the drive rehearsing. I had a whole speech about how nothing was expected of him and he could sleep wherever he liked and there was no hurry about anything, and I did not say any of it, because when I opened the front door he stopped on the mat and would not come in.

He stood there with the carrier bag of his medicines in one hand.

I said, "You can come in."

He said, "Which is mine."

He did not mean which room. It took me a second. He meant which part of the flat was his — which was the bit he was allowed to be in, the way there had been a settee in one place and a corner in another, the way for five years there had been a defined area and consequences for leaving it.

I said, "All of it. The whole flat is yours."

And he looked at me like I was being careless with something expensive.

I had kept his room.

I need to be honest about the shape of that, because it is the sort of detail that reads as devotion and was actually something closer to paralysis. I did not keep it as a shrine. What happened was that for five years I could not decide, and a thing you cannot decide about stays where it is.

His bed was there. A chest of drawers with clothes for a four-year-old folded in it. A box of the dinosaurs. On the wall, at the height a four-year-old's eye reaches, a strip of stickers he had put on himself in 2020 and which I had never taken off, and above the bed a growth chart with three pencil marks on it and a date beside each one.

I had thought about it in the car, obviously. I had had nine days.

And I had decided, on the advice of nobody, that I would show him the room and let him decide, and that is what I did, and it was wrong. I know it was wrong now. Ruth the play specialist told me afterwards, very kindly, that a child in that state should be given a small number of choices and that this was not one of them, and she was right.

He stood in the doorway of his own bedroom and looked at a room built for a much smaller boy who had been taken out of it and never came back, and his face did the thing it does, which is nothing at all.

Then he said, "Whose is this?"

"It was yours."

"It's for a baby," he said.

I said, "You were four."

And he said, "Oh," and I watched him do the arithmetic — I watched a nine-year-old stand in a doorway and work out, from a bed and a growth chart, exactly how long he had been gone and exactly how much of it there had been.

He did not sleep in there that night. He slept on the sofa in the front room with the door to the hall open and the landing light on, and I sat in the armchair until half past two and did not sleep at all, and Lily came out at some point and put a blanket on me and went back to bed, ten years old, without saying anything.

He slept on that sofa for six weeks.

We took the room apart in the second week, the two of us, on a Saturday. I asked him what he wanted to keep and he said nothing, and I said, "I'm keeping the growth chart," and he shrugged, and three years later that chart has eleven marks on it and the last four are in his own handwriting.

The things that were difficult were not the things anybody warned me about.

Food I have written about. Doors I have written about. Here are the others, and I am putting them down because when this was in the papers a great many people wrote to me about their own children coming out of care and out of worse than care, and every single letter said the same thing: nobody told me it would be the small stuff.

He could not be told to do anything twice. Once was fine. The second time, in any tone at all, and something shut behind his eyes, and he complied instantly and perfectly and was gone for about two hours.

He apologised constantly. For everything. For being in the kitchen. For the noise the tap made. In the first month I counted forty-one apologies in a single Sunday and then stopped counting because it was making me strange with him.

He hoarded food. Not much, and never anything that went off — he was careful about that, which is the part that gets me, he had worked out the system. Bread rolls, biscuits, a banana once, wrapped in a jumper at the bottom of a drawer. The advice is that you do not confront it and you do not remove it. You put a bowl of fruit in his room and you say nothing and you wait, and it takes as long as it takes. It took eleven months.

He was frightened of the bath and fine with the shower.

He did not know how old he was. He knew he was "about ten" and had been told different things by different people, and when I told him his birthday was the fourth of March he asked me how I knew, and I got out his red book, the little book they give you at the hospital when they are born, and showed him the first page.

He read his own name in my handwriting from 2016 and then he read the number underneath it, which was his weight, and he said, "Was I small?"

I said, "You were three and a half kilos. You were exactly right."

He kept the book. It is in his room now on the shelf next to the radio.

Lily was the one who got it right, and she got it right by accident, and I have thought about it for three years.

Everything I did in those first weeks I did carefully. I planned it. I read things. I sat in a chair at a measured distance and I offered choices and I did not touch him without asking, and all of that was correct and all of it made me, in his eyes, another adult managing him.

Lily just carried on.

She was rude to him. Within a fortnight she was telling him he was breathing too loudly and taking the remote out of his hand and complaining, loudly, at dinner, that he got more chips. She did it in front of me, waiting to be told off, and I understood, about four seconds in, that if I told her off I would be telling her that he was a guest.

So I said, "You've got the same as him," and she said "I have NOT," and he said "she has not," and the two of them ganged up on the chips, and my son called my daughter a liar at my own kitchen table, and I got up and put plates in the sink so they would not see my face.

The advice I had been given by three professionals was to give him space and time and low demand.

What actually brought him back into the world was an eight-year-old girl treating him like an irritating brother from the first fortnight, because she did not know she was supposed to be careful, because at eight she did not have the equipment to be careful.

She is the reason he is all right. Not me. I have never once managed to say that to her in a way she would understand and I expect I never will.

My mother came the first Sunday.

Half past eleven, bag of shopping, exactly as she had every Sunday for five years, and she stood in my hallway and took her coat off and said, "Right," and went into the kitchen and started cooking, and it was the single most normal thing that had happened to any of us since the fourth of November.

She cooked a roast. He ate all of it. She did not comment on his eating and she did not ask him a single question and she talked steadily for an hour and a half about a woman in her building and the traffic and a dog, and Lily interrupted forty times, and Sam sat at the end of my kitchen table in a jumper that still had the shop creases in it and let it all go over him.

At about three he fell asleep sitting up in the armchair with the television on.

My mother stood in the doorway and looked at him for a long moment, and then she said, very quietly, "He's got your father's hands."

And I said, "Has he?"

"Oh, he has," she said. "Look at the thumbs."

And I went and looked at my sleeping son's thumbs, and she was right, and the two of us stood in my front room and looked at a child's hands and I have never felt closer to my mother in my life than I did in that thirty seconds.

Eleven months later I would sit in a car park in the rain and she would tell me that she agreed the sale of my father's house four weeks after I rang her from a supermarket, sobbing.

Both of those things are true. I have stopped trying to make them fit.
