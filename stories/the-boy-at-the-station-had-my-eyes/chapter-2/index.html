My Daughter Gave Her Scarf to a Freezing Boy. He Had My Eyes.

Chapter 02 — Blue Ink

Chapter 2 of 17

They would not let me stay in the room with him, and I made a scene about it, and I was wrong.

The station police took us up to a room on the mezzanine level with a table and four chairs and a poster about pickpockets, and a woman from the transport office brought foil blankets and a cup of hot chocolate that nobody drank, and somewhere in the first twenty minutes a detective arrived whose name was Marisol Reyes.

She was about my age. Wet coat, flat shoes, and the specific tiredness of a person who has been called out at the end of a shift. She listened to me for four minutes without writing anything down, which I resented, and then she said one sentence that I have thought about every day since.

"Mrs Vale, if he is your son, then somebody took him. And whoever took him has had five years to get their story straight, and you have had about twenty minutes. So I need you to stop telling me and start letting him tell me."

Then she went downstairs in the rain and came back up with my bag.

She was holding it by the very edge of the handle with two fingers, and it was streaming water, and she set it on a sheet of plastic in the corner and did not give it back to me. I did not understand. I said something stupid about my keys being in it.

"It's been on the platform since he identified you," she said. "It's a scene. Everything that was on that platform is a scene until I know what happened on it."

I said, "Nothing happened on it. My daughter gave him a scarf."

"And I'll be taking the scarf too," she said. "Eventually. Not tonight."

I want to be clear that she was right about all of it, and that at the time I hated her.

They took him to St Aldate's for the medical, and because I could not prove anything yet I was allowed to sit in a corridor. A corridor, after five years. Lily fell asleep sitting up against my arm with her coat still on and her neck bare, and I sat there with my hand on her head and looked at a strip of light under a door.

A doctor came out at some point and told me things in a careful order that I understood was designed to be survivable.

He was underweight but not dangerously. He had a healed fracture in his left forearm that had set slightly wrong, which meant it had never been treated. He had scarring on both heels from footwear that did not fit. He had a chest infection they were going to treat that night. There was no evidence of the kinds of injury she was obliged to look for, and she said that part twice, slowly, because she knew what I was sitting there not asking.

I said, "How long?"

She did not pretend not to understand.

"The arm's old. Two years, maybe three. The rest of it is more recent than that."

Which told me something I did not want. It told me he had not been in one bad place for five years. He had been in more than one.

They let me in at ten past nine.

He was sitting up in a bed in a paper gown with a cannula taped to the back of his hand and his hair washed, and washed it was much lighter, and it was my hair, and I had to hold onto the end of the bed.

The scarf was on the chair beside him. Someone had tried to take it and been told no, apparently, quite firmly.

I did not run at him. I had spent the corridor hours making myself a set of rules and the first rule was that I would let him decide the distance. So I sat down in the chair across the room, which was much too far away, and I said hello, and he said hello.

Then, for a while, nothing.

Detective Reyes was in the corner with a notebook she did not open. Later she told me she stayed because she had to, and that she had been fully prepared for me to be a woman who wanted a missing boy so badly that she had made one out of a stranger on a bench. She said this without apology and I have never held it against her.

He broke the silence himself.

"Are you the lady from the station."

Not a question, quite. He had a flat way of putting things, the sentence going down at the end instead of up, as though asking properly was a thing that cost money.

I said I was.

"You said a name."

"Sam."

He looked at the blanket. "People call me Eli."

I heard Reyes's pen move for the first time.

"Who does?" she said, very lightly, from the corner.

He thought about it in a way that frightened me — not searching his memory, but doing a calculation about what it was safe to say. Then he shrugged one shoulder and said, "Everyone. For a while it was Danny. Before that I don't know."

"Do you know what your name is?" I asked.

And he said, "Sam." Just like that, quite certain, and then immediately less certain: "Walter said Sam."

Reyes wrote that down. I did not know what it meant. It would be three weeks before I did.

I asked him, because I could not stop myself, whether he knew who I was.

He looked at me for a long moment. Then he said the thing that took the floor out from under me, and he said it kindly, which was worse.

"No. But you've got the eyes."

I am going to write down what he gave me that first night, because at the time it sounded like nothing, and every single piece of it turned out to be a door.

He did not tell it as a story. He told it the way a child tells you about a dream three days later — in fragments, out of order, with no idea which parts were remarkable.

He said he knew he was called Sam because a man called Walter had told him so, and that Walter was dead.

He said that when he was small there was a smell in the house he came from. He could not name it. He worked at it for nearly a minute while all three adults in that room sat completely still, and then he said, "Orange. But not eating it. Cooking it."

Orange peel. I dry orange peel on the radiator from October onwards. My mother did it, and her mother did it, and it makes the whole flat smell like a spice tin, and I have never in my life met anybody outside my family who does it.

I did not say anything. Reyes glanced at me and I shook my head slightly and she let it go.

Then he said the other thing.

He said, "There was a hand with blue on it."

I said, "Blue?"

He rubbed at the pads of his own fingers with his thumb. "Here. Blue, on the ends. It didn't wash off."

I turned my hands over.

I have been repairing books at the county library since I was twenty-two. Gauze, wheat paste, linen thread, and a blue-black iron gall ink I mix myself in a jar for retouching lettering, and it gets into the ridges of my fingertips and it stays for four or five days, and every winter of my adult life I have had blue fingertips.

They were blue that night. They were blue while he was describing them.

I put my hands under the blanket on the end of his bed like a woman hiding something, because I could feel Reyes watching me, and because if I had held them up and said these, do you mean these — he would have agreed with me. Of course he would have. He was nine, and exhausted, and a stranger had just wept at him. He would have agreed with anything I offered him, and it would have been worth nothing.

That is the thing nobody tells you. When you finally find the child, you have to be more careful than everyone else in the room, not less. Every leading question I asked would come out of his mouth later as an answer, and any lawyer in the country would take it apart.

So I said, "Okay," and nothing else, and it was the hardest thing I have ever done.

He fell asleep about eleven, mid-sentence, the way small children do.

Reyes and I stood in the corridor.

"The peel," she said. "Is that a real thing, or is that a mother thing?"

"It's a real thing."

"Anyone else do it? Neighbours, friends, family?"

"My mother," I said. "My mother taught me."

She wrote it down without any particular weight, the way you write down a fifth item on a list, and I watched her do it, and I felt nothing at all. Not a flicker. That is worth recording too — that I stood in a hospital corridor and gave a detective my mother's habit as a piece of evidence and felt absolutely nothing.

They took a swab from the inside of his cheek at half past eleven. The result would take nine days.

I already knew. But knowing is not the same as being able to say it out loud in a room with a table in it, so we waited the nine days, and in those nine days I called my mother eleven times, and she came over twice, and she cried with me, and she made a lasagne and put it in my freezer with the date written on the lid in her small tidy handwriting.

She was the first person I called from the hospital car park.

She said, "Oh, Nora." She said, "Oh, my darling, where."

And I told her: Hollis Street. Platform four.

She was quiet for exactly as long as it takes to breathe out once, and then she said, "I'm coming now," and she drove forty minutes in the rain at sixty-three years old to sit with me until it got light.

I have gone back over that phone call so many times that I have worn the surface off it.

She did not ask me if I was sure.
