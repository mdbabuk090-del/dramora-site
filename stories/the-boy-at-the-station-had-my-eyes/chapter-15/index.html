My Daughter Gave Her Scarf to a Freezing Boy. He Had My Eyes.

Chapter 15 — What She Thought She Was Saving Me From

Chapter 15 of 17

She was on remand for six weeks and then bailed to an address in Cheltenham belonging to a woman I have never met, and in the March, through her solicitor, she asked to see me.

Reyes advised against it. My own solicitor advised against it. Peter, who I rang because there was nobody else to ring, said, "Don't, Nor," and then, after a silence, "You're going to, aren't you."

I went on a Tuesday.

It was a Travelodge car park, of all the places in the world, because neither of us could go to the other's house and because a solicitor's office felt like a thing being done to us. She was sitting in her car when I arrived. She got out when she saw me and she did not come towards me, and I did not go towards her, and we stood about four feet apart on wet tarmac in March.

She had lost a great deal of weight. That was the first thing.

She said, "Thank you for coming."

I said, "I want you to tell me. All of it, in order, and I want it from you and not from a barrister in eight months' time."

And my mother said, "All right."

She did not sit down. She stood in that car park for fifty minutes with her hands in her coat pockets and she told me, and I am going to put it here as close to how she said it as I can manage, because I have discovered that when people ask me about this — and they do — what they want is for her to have been a monster, and I cannot give them that, and I have stopped trying.

She said it started in the car park in May, on the phone.

She said: "You told me you were going to kill one of them by accident. Those were your words. And then you told me Peter would take them off you."

She said she put the phone down and stood in her hallway on Ardley Row and could not get her breath.

"I want you to understand what I was looking at," she said. "You will say I was looking at my daughter having a bad spring. I was not. I was looking at a nineteen-year-old on a telephone."

I said, "So say his name."

She looked at the tarmac for a while.

"Tobias," she said.

It is the only time I have heard my mother say her brother's name.

She said she did not sleep that summer either. She said she went to the doctor about my pills and was told, properly, that she could not be told anything. She said she rang a helpline twice and put the phone down both times because the woman kept using the word safeguarding and she understood, correctly, that safeguarding meant a file, and a file meant Peter's solicitor, and Peter's solicitor meant a hearing at which somebody would read out the words found asleep in a vehicle with both children.

"They would have gone to him," she said. "Not for a fortnight. For good. You were not well and you were on your own and there was a police report and that is how it goes, Nora, that is exactly how it goes."

I said, "So you took him."

"I took him out," she said, and she said it with a force that made me step back. "For the summer. Until you were mended. Roy Tarrant had done work at the house for your father for years and he was the only person I knew who was outside all of it, and I paid him to put that child somewhere warm with a woman who fed him, and I paid her, and I paid for his shoes, and I was going to bring him home."

"When."

"September," she said. "The plan was September."

"And what was I supposed to be doing in the meantime?"

She did not answer for a long moment.

"Getting better," she said.

I said, "You watched me put his photograph on lamp posts."

"Yes."

"You stood outside Hollis Street station with me for four hours in the wrong shoes."

"Yes."

"Why."

And my mother said, "Because by then I could not stop it."

This is the part she had rehearsed. I could hear it. She had been through it in a cell for six weeks and she had it in an order.

She said the first fortnight nearly finished her, and that she was three times on the point of walking into the police station on Vernon Road, and that what stopped her on the third time was standing on the step and understanding that the moment she said one word, two things happened at once: she went to prison, and I found out that my mother had done it.

"And you were not going to survive the second one," she said. "You were not sleeping and you were not eating and you had a three-year-old. If I had told you in the July you would have been dead by the Christmas. I decided that. I had no right to decide it and I decided it and I have been standing in that decision ever since."

I said, "You had five years."

"Every day of them," she said.

"Then what was September?"

She looked at me and her face went, and it was the only time it went.

"September was when Roy told me he wanted another twelve," she said. "And I said no, and I said bring him back, and he laughed at me, Nora. He stood in that car park and he laughed and he said, bring him back where? Walk him into the police station and tell them what? And then he said if I ever came at him he would put the boy somewhere I would not find him."

"And you believed him."

"He did it," she said. "That is not belief. He did it. He moved him twice more that year and stopped telling me where and put the price up, and I paid it every week for seventy-one weeks because it was the only string I had left in my hand, and then he died on the floor of a bookmaker's and the string went, and after that I did not know where my grandson was any more than you did."

There was a long silence in that car park.

"For three years," she said, "I had exactly what you had. Nothing. I want you to understand that I was not sitting on it. People think I was sitting on it. I would have given every day of the rest of my life to be sitting on it, because sitting on it would have meant I knew."

I asked her the question I had driven ninety minutes to ask.

I said, "Did you know he was on the street?"

"No."

"Did you look?"

And she said, "Where?"

And that was the answer. That was the whole of it, in one word, and I understood something standing there that I had not understood in four months of solicitors and disclosure.

She could not look. That was the mechanism. She could not hire anybody, she could not go to a police officer, she could not join a group or post a photograph or stand outside a station with a laminated sheet, because every single one of those things is a thing that leaves a mark, and every mark led back to her. She had made herself the one person on earth who was not allowed to search for him.

So she came to my flat every Sunday, and she cooked, and she used his name so that I would be able to bear it, and in the fourth year she stood at my sink with her back to me and told me to stop looking — and I have to write this down because it is the truth and it is unbearable — she told me to stop because she could not stand watching me do the thing she was not allowed to do.

Not to protect herself. I have gone over the timings and there was nothing to protect by then; Tarrant was three years dead and there was no thread left to pull. She told me to stop because every Sunday for five years she sat in a kitchen and watched her daughter search for a child she had personally handed to a man in a car park, and it was killing her, and she wanted it to be over.

She did not want to be caught. She wanted the searching to end.

Those are not the same thing and my barrister has explained to me that a jury does not have a box for the difference.

At the end she asked me one thing.

She said, "Is he all right? Does he sleep?"

And I heard myself say, "He sleeps with the radio on. He can't sleep in the quiet."

Something happened to her face.

"His grandfather did that," she said. "Your father. Thirty years, he had the shipping forecast on. You never knew that, you were out of the house by then."

I said, "He learned it off a dead man in a flat behind the station."

"Yes," she said. "I know. I read it in the papers with everybody else."

She stood there a moment longer.

Then she said, "I am going to plead guilty. I want you to hear it from me and not in a letter. I am not going to make that child stand in a room and be asked questions by a man in a wig so that I can have four years instead of nine."

I said, "Good."

"I know," she said.

I got in my car. She did not try to touch me and she did not say she was sorry, and I have thought about that a great deal, and I have decided that she did not say it because she knew exactly what it would be worth, which was nothing, and because saying it would have been the last thing she ever did for herself rather than for me.

She stood in that car park in her coat and let me leave.

I got about two miles before I had to pull in.
