My Daughter Gave Her Scarf to a Freezing Boy. He Had My Eyes.

Chapter 03 — Nine Days

Chapter 3 of 17

Nine days is not a long time and I would like to explain why it was the longest thing that has ever happened to me.

The swab went off on the Tuesday night. Reyes told me the turnaround was nine working days and that she would not be able to hurry it and that I should not ask her to, because every time somebody asks her to hurry a lab she has to write it down.

So there were nine days in which a nine-year-old boy was in a hospital bed on a paediatric ward and I was, legally, nobody.

I want to be precise about that, because people assume that once I said the name it was finished. It was not finished. I could not consent to his treatment. I could not take him out of the building. I could not sign the form for the chest X-ray. I was allowed to visit during visiting hours like an aunt, and a woman from the local authority sat in on the first four visits with a folder on her knee, and on the second day a very kind nurse called Bernadette asked me, gently, whether I would like her to bring me a chair from the day room, and I said yes and then went and cried in a stairwell for ten minutes because a chair was the first thing anybody had offered me.

He had been placed under an emergency order. He was, in the language of the paperwork, a child of unknown identity found in a public place. There is a box for that. I have seen the box.

The ward had a routine and I learned it the way you learn a foreign language when you are trapped in the country.

Obs at seven and again at two. Breakfast trolley twenty past eight. Doctors' round somewhere between nine and eleven, which is when parents are asked to stand back from the bed, and I was not a parent, so I stood in the corridor by the fire door and counted the tiles. Antibiotics at ten, two and six. The play specialist, whose name was Ruth, came at half past three with a trolley and never once asked him a question, which is why by the fourth day he would talk to her and not to the doctors.

He did not like the curtain being pulled round the bed. On the second morning somebody pulled it for a dressing change and he came off the bed and stood in the corner with his back to the wall and would not come out for forty minutes.

They noted it down. Everything about him was being noted down. There was already a file with his behaviours in it and I could not read the file, and that is one of the things from those nine days that has stayed lodged sideways in me — that strangers were writing an account of my son and I was not entitled to it.

He asked me on the third day whether I was going to stop coming.

He asked it the way he asked everything then, flat, at the wrong moment, in the middle of something else. We were doing a jigsaw of a tractor that neither of us cared about.

"When they say you're not it," he said, "do you stop coming."

I put the piece down.

"They won't say that."

"They might."

"They might," I said. "And no. I'd still come."

He thought about that for a while and then he said, "Okay," and put a piece of the tractor's wheel in the wrong place on purpose to see if I would say anything, which I did not, and which he found extremely funny.

That was the first time I heard him laugh. Over a wheel in the wrong place.

I brought Lily on the fifth day, and I had a great deal of advice about that, all of it against.

She had asked to come every single day since the platform. Not in a wheedling way; she asked once each morning at breakfast, formally, like a woman submitting an application, and accepted the answer, and asked again the next morning.

On the fifth morning I ran out of reasons.

I sat her down first and told her the true thing, which was that we did not know yet, that the test was not back, that the boy might not be Sam and that if he was not, we would still be glad we had been kind to him. She listened to all of it with her chin up.

Then she said, "But you already know."

I said, "Lily—"

"You do though," she said. "You knew on the platform."

Children are appalling.

In the ward she did not do any of the things I had braced for. She did not stare at him or ask about his arm or say anything about the way he ate. She walked up to the end of the bed and said, "Hello. I'm Lily. That's my scarf."

And he said, "I know."

And she said, "You can keep it, I'm just saying it's mine," and got a chair.

They played a game on her tablet for fifty minutes in near silence with their heads together, and I sat by the window and watched my two children breathe in the same room for the first time in five years and four months, and neither of them knew it, and I have never in my life had to work so hard to be quiet.

On the way out she took my hand in the lift and said, "He does the thing with his eyebrow that's on your face."

I said, "What thing."

"The thing," she said, impatiently, and did it, and it was mine, and I had to look at the lift buttons very hard.

My mother came on the second morning and every morning after.

I am going to put down properly, later on, what she was like with him in that room, because it belongs with the five years and not with the nine days, and because I stand by every word of it. But there is one thing from that week that belongs here, and I did not think about it for four months and then I could not stop.

On the sixth day the social worker asked her, in front of me, in a routine sort of way, whether she wanted to be assessed as a potential carer for the child, in the event.

It was not a strange question. It is the question they ask. If the boy turned out to be Sam, and if there were any concerns about my capacity — and there was a police report about a car park in a file with my name on it, five years old and still there — then the next thing they look for is family.

And my mother said no.

Straight away. No hesitation, no apology, nothing about her health or her age or her flat being too small. Just: "No. Thank you. He goes to his mother."

The social worker wrote something and moved on, and I remember being obscurely hurt by it in the moment, in a way I did not examine, and I remember my mother going down to the car park afterwards for a cigarette, and my mother has not smoked since 1994.

The result came at ten past four on a Thursday.

Reyes did not do it on the telephone, which I have thanked her for since. She drove to the hospital and found me in the family room with a vending-machine tea I had not touched and she sat down opposite me and she said my name once and then she said it plainly.

"It's him. It's Sam."

I do not remember the next part. My mother has told me and Reyes has told me and the two accounts agree, so I will put down theirs: I stood up, and said "okay" twice, and then my legs went, and my mother — sixty-three years old, in a hospital family room — got both hands under my arms from a standing start and held me up until I could take my own weight.

She was crying so hard that Reyes went and got her a chair.

Then I went up to the ward and stood at the end of his bed and did not have the first idea what to say to him.

He looked at me. He had worked it out from my face before I opened my mouth — he was extremely good at that, it was the most reliable skill he had, and it took two years to stop being the first thing he did when anybody walked into a room.

He said, "Is it yes?"

I said yes.

And Sam nodded, slowly, taking it in, and then he said the thing that I have never told anybody except Peter, because it is the exact measure of what those five years had done and I cannot say it out loud without my throat closing.

He said: "Am I allowed to still keep the scarf?"

That night they discharged him to me with a bag of medication and a follow-up appointment and a leaflet.

There was a form. I signed it in the corridor on a clipboard against the wall, and the nurse said, "Just there, Mum," in a completely ordinary voice, the way she must say it forty times a week.

That is the moment it happened, if you want to know. Not the platform. Not the lab result.

A woman with a biro called me Mum in a corridor and I signed my name on a line.
