My Daughter Gave Her Scarf to a Freezing Boy. He Had My Eyes.

Chapter 07 — Danny Rook

Chapter 7 of 17

The name came out of a school.

Not out of an investigation, not out of a database, not out of any of the four hundred pounds I had handed to men over the years. It came out of a woman called Mrs Aldritt who ran the office at a primary school in Coldbrook and who had kept a register in a filing cabinet for longer than she was supposed to.

Coldbrook is forty minutes from my flat. Forty minutes. You can drive it in less on a Sunday.

Reyes told me on a Tuesday. She had a way of delivering things where she told you the shape first and the details after, so you had somewhere to put them.

"We've got a name he was enrolled under," she said. "Two years, roughly year one to year three. Then he comes off the roll and doesn't reappear anywhere in the county."

"What name."

"Daniel Rook. Danny."

Rook. I said it out loud a few times in the car afterwards. It is not a name that means anything to me and I had half expected it to. You spend five years imagining that when the answer comes it will click into something — a cousin, an old neighbour, a man who once did our guttering. It did not click into anything at all. It was just a word somebody had put on my son.

Mrs Aldritt agreed to see me, which Reyes was not thrilled about and did not forbid.

She was about seventy, retired two years, and she had the register in a carrier bag on her kitchen table when I arrived, and she had clearly been up since six deciding whether to be ashamed of herself.

"I did ring," she said, before I had sat down. "I want you to know that I rang."

"Rang who?"

"Social services. Twice, in the second year. I want you to know that."

She had a photograph, too. A class photograph, twenty-six children on tiered benches in front of a mural of a hot air balloon, and she had put a sticky arrow on the back row before I came so that I would not have to hunt.

He was five and a half. He was standing at the end of the row slightly apart from the boy next to him. He was wearing a jumper that was the wrong colour for that school and his hair was too long and he was not smiling, and he was looking at the camera with my eyes, and I sat in a stranger's kitchen in Coldbrook and made a sound I have not made before or since.

Mrs Aldritt did not say anything. She got up and put the kettle on and stood with her back to me for a decent length of time, which I have always been grateful for.

When I could speak I asked her what he had been like.

"Quiet," she said. "Very quiet. But you have to understand, love, quiet is not a flag. Half of them are quiet. If quiet were a flag we would never do anything else."

I asked what was a flag.

She sat back down and she told me, and it took a long time, and I wrote it in the back of the red folder in the car afterwards because I did not trust myself to keep it straight.

He came in with no records. That happens, she said, more than you would think — a child arrives with a story about a house fire or a move from abroad and a woman with a plausible manner, and the paperwork is always coming next week. He had no birth certificate on file. The address on the form was a house on the edge of the estate belonging to a woman called Hattie Pell, who was down as an aunt and who was not on any document as anything.

He had no PE kit for two terms. He never had a book bag. He was never once picked up by the same adult twice in a row, and after the first year he was not picked up at all — he walked, at six, a mile and a quarter, across the ring road.

And in the second year, she said, he came in with his arm.

"It was strapped," she said. "Someone had strapped it, with a tea towel and a bandage, and quite competently, and he told me he had fallen down the steps at the back, and he was holding it wrong. Anyone could see it was broken. I rang that afternoon."

"And?"

"And a woman came out. Ten days later. She saw him at school and she saw the house, and the report says she had no concerns because the child presented as settled and the carer was cooperative, and I could show it to you but I would only make you angry."

I asked her the question I had driven forty minutes to ask.

"Did he ever say anything about before?"

Mrs Aldritt looked at her tea.

"Once," she said. "In the reception year, in the first term. There was a girl in his class who was upset because her mother had not come, and he said to her, quite matter-of-fact, the way they do — he said, *my mum comes back on Thursday.*"

I put both my hands flat on the table.

"He said it for about a fortnight," she said. "*Thursday.* And then he stopped saying it and he never said it again, and I want to tell you that I noticed at the time, and the truth is that I did not. I remembered it three days ago when the detective rang. I have not slept properly since."

Thursday.

Somebody told my four-year-old son that he was going away and coming back on Thursday. Somebody looked at him and gave him a specific day, because a specific day is the only thing that works on a four-year-old — you cannot tell them soon, they will ask again in eleven minutes, but you can tell them Thursday and they will hold onto it.

Somebody who knew that.

Hattie Pell still lived in the same house.

Reyes went, formally, with another officer, and I was allowed to know what was said afterwards and not before, and I was told very clearly what would happen to the case if I went there myself, so I did not go, which I want noted, because I wanted to.

Her account was this: a man she had known for years, Roy Tarrant, asked her to take a child for a few months. There was money — cash, weekly, delivered by hand. She was told the mother was an addict and the family was sorting itself out. She said this without embarrassment. She said the boy was no trouble.

After about a year and a half the money stopped, because Roy stopped coming.

She kept the boy for another four months, on the child benefit she had fraudulently claimed, and then she said she could not manage, and she gave him to a man she described as a friend of Roy's whose surname she claimed not to know, and after that she did not see him again and did not, she said, feel it was her place to ask.

Reyes told me all this in the car park behind the station with the engine running.

I said, "She gave him away."

"She passed him on," Reyes said. "That's the phrase she used. Twice."

I asked whether she would be charged. Reyes said probably, eventually, with something that would not feel like enough, and she was right about that too.

Then she said, "Mrs Vale. The name Roy Tarrant. I need you to think properly before you answer, and I need you to take your time."

And I did think. I sat in that car and I went back through fifteen years of my life, every man who had ever fixed a boiler or delivered a sofa or stood at a barbecue in my in-laws' garden, and there was nothing. Nothing at all.

"I've never heard it," I said.

"All right," she said. "It's an old name round here. He's known to us going back a long way — bit of everything, nothing organised. He died in October of the second year." She paused. "Which is a problem, because it means the person who can tell us who paid him is in the ground."

I sat there and watched the wipers go.

"Somebody paid him," I said.

"Somebody paid him," she agreed. "Weekly cash to Pell for eighteen months, plus whatever he took himself. That's not nothing, that's a sum. And it stopped when he died, which tells me the money was going through him and not round him." She put the car into gear. "People who take a child for money take it from someone. My job is now almost entirely about finding out who, and I'll be honest with you, in my experience it is nearly always somebody who was at the funeral."

I said, "There wasn't a funeral. He wasn't dead."

"No," she said. "There wasn't. You know what I mean."

I did know what she meant. And I went home and slept for six hours, which was more than I had managed in a fortnight, because at that point I still thought this was a story about strangers.
