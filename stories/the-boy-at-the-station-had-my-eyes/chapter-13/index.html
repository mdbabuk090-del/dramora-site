Chapter 13 — What Roy Tarrant Kept

Chapter 13 of 17

Roy Tarrant had been dead for three years and he convicted her anyway, because he was the sort of man who kept everything.

His sister still had four boxes in a garage in Netherfield. She had not opened them. She told the officers who came for them that she had meant to do it every year since the funeral and had got as far as the door twice.

Reyes rang me on a Thursday morning and said, "I'm not going to tell you what's in it yet. I'm going to tell you that there is one, and that I need you to sit tight for about a fortnight, and I need you to not see your mother."

I said, "There's a what."

"Sit tight, Mrs Vale."

It took nineteen days, and this is what was in the boxes.

Roy Tarrant kept receipts, which is not unusual in itself; a lot of men who live like that keep every scrap, because their whole existence is a series of arguments about who owes what. He kept them in carrier bags by year. He also kept, in a plastic wallet with an elastic band round it, the things he considered important.

There were three items in the plastic wallet that mattered.

The first was a handwritten list of dates and amounts running down two sides of a sheet of lined A4 in Tarrant's own writing. Weekly, mostly forty, sometimes sixty. The list ran for seventy-one weeks and then stopped. At the top of the sheet, on its own, was written a single letter and a figure: A — 12,400.

The second was a bank paying-in slip, one of the old counterfoil ones, for a cash deposit of four hundred pounds. Tarrant did not have a bank account. It was his sister's.

The third was a photograph of a woman getting out of a car.

It had been taken from across a road at a distance of perhaps forty metres, badly, at an angle, the way a man takes a photograph he does not want to be seen taking. The woman is mid-thirties in the picture — no. I am doing what everybody who saw it did, which is to describe it as though the identification were difficult.

It is my mother. She is sixty-one in it and she is getting out of her own car outside a café on the Marbury road, and she is wearing the green coat that hung on the back of her kitchen door for a decade.

He had photographed the person paying him. Of course he had. He was not doing it to blackmail her, or not only; a man like Tarrant photographs the person paying him for the same reason he keeps the receipts. Because one day somebody is going to say it was not them.

On the back, in biro: the date, and one word — grandma.

They put the money together over those nineteen days and it is the part I still cannot look at directly.

The house on Ardley Row sold for thirty-one thousand under valuation to a cash buyer, which is what you do when you need it finished by a date. She cleared, after the mortgage she had taken on it two years before my father died and never mentioned to me, a little over forty-one thousand pounds.

Twelve thousand four hundred went to Roy Tarrant in one payment in the July.

Then the weekly sums for seventy-one weeks, in cash, hand to hand, in a car park behind a garden centre off the Marbury road, which is nine minutes from her flat and forty minutes from mine.

She stopped paying in the October of the second year, because in the October of the second year Roy Tarrant had a heart attack in a bookmaker's on Vernon Road and died on the floor.

And the rest of the money — twenty-two thousand and some — was still sitting in an account in her name when they went in for it.

She never spent it.

Five years. She did not move, she did not take a holiday, she drove a fourteen-year-old car, she bought me a leather handbag one Christmas and I know now what that cost her and I have not been able to think about that handbag properly since. Twenty-two thousand pounds sat in a building society account and every year the interest went in and every year she did not touch it.

Reyes said, "That's not a woman who did it for money."

I said, "I know."

"I have to put it to a jury as though it were," she said. "I want you to be ready for that, because you'll hear it in a courtroom and it will not sound like the truth to you, and it will still be the correct thing for the prosecution to do."

The last thing was the station.

Hollis Street had four cameras in the year Sam went. Two of them were dummies. The footage was gone — of course it was gone, it was five years, it had been gone since the first fortnight, and the absence of it is the single reason the original investigation died.

But the transport police pull stills. In the first week of a missing child they pull stills and they put them in the file, and the file had gone to a central unit and been archived and never destroyed.

There were nineteen stills in that file and I had seen all nineteen of them, many times, in a red plastic folder in the bottom of my wardrobe, and so had four hundred other people, because they had been in the paper.

Number eleven is a wide shot of the concourse at 16:42.

It is a bad photograph of about sixty people. In the middle distance there is a woman with a pushchair, and to her left a man in a hi-vis, and behind them, small, going towards the barriers, a man in a dark jacket holding the hand of a child in a navy duffel coat.

That is the picture. That is the one that was in the paper for a fortnight in the first year. Do you know this man. And nobody did, and there was no face — his head is turned — and after about eight months the picture stopped being a lead and became a thing that hung on my wall.

In November, five years on, a digital forensics unit went back to the original file rather than the printed version.

And in the original, in the top left corner, out of focus, forty feet behind the man and the child and half cut off by the edge of the frame, there is a woman standing at the end of the ticket machines, not moving, not queuing, facing them.

You cannot see her face. You will never see her face. It is eleven pixels of head.

But she is wearing a green coat, and she is standing in a way I have watched my mother stand at a school gate a hundred times, weight on the left, right hand holding the strap of a bag across her body, and when they showed it to me in a room with a table in it I put my hand over my mouth.

Reyes said, "It's not evidential. I want to be honest with you. On its own that is a woman in a coat."

I said, "She was there."

"She was there," Reyes agreed. "Because she wanted to see him go. Or because she wanted to be sure it was done properly. Or because she could not make herself not be there. I don't know which, and neither will a jury, and it will not go in front of one." She closed the folder. "But you asked me once whether she was in the country that day, because she told you she was at her sister-in-law's in Norfolk."

"She did tell me that."

"She was on platform four," Reyes said. "And she has been telling you for five years to stop looking in the one place she knew you would find something."

I asked her one question, at the end of it, and it was not the one she was expecting.

I said, "How long have you known that you were going to arrest my mother?"

Reyes put the folder square on the table and thought about her answer for longer than I liked.

"Since the house," she said. "Since you rang me on a Sunday afternoon and could not say the sentence. Everything since then has been me making sure I could prove it, because I was not going to go to that flat and knock on that door and then not be able to finish it."

"Three weeks," I said.

"Three weeks."

I said, "You could have told me."

And Reyes said, "I could. And then every conversation you had with her for three weeks would have been a performance, and she has known you for thirty-four years, and she would have seen it in about four minutes." She stood up and put her coat on. "You are a bad liar, Mrs Vale. It is nearly the only uncomplicated thing about this entire case and I was not going to waste it."

Then she stopped at my kitchen door.

"It'll be soon," she said. "I'll tell you the night before. That much I can do."

She was as good as her word, and I wish she had not been, because being told the night before turned out to be a thing I had to do something with, and I did not do it well.
