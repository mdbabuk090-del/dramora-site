My Daughter Gave Her Scarf to a Freezing Boy. He Had My Eyes.

Chapter 17 — The Scarf

Chapter 17 of 17

She pleaded guilty in the September and she got nine years, and the judge said a number of things about breach of trust that were correct and that I could not listen to.

I did not read the reports. Lily's school was very good about it. There was a week in the October when a photographer sat in a car at the end of our road and Sam would not go out of the front door, and then there was a bigger story somewhere else and they went, the way they do.

Hattie Pell got fourteen months for the benefit fraud and nothing at all for the rest of it, because there is nothing on the books for passing a child on twice. Roy Tarrant is in a cemetery outside Netherfield. The man above the shop has never been identified and the year Sam spent there remains a hole, and I have made my peace with the fact that it may always be his and not mine.

Mrs Aldritt writes to us at Christmas. June from the laundrette sends a card. There was an inquiry, internally, into a referral in the second year that was closed with no concerns, and I was sent a letter about it with a word in it that I have decided not to repeat here.

That is the tidying up. It takes about eleven months and nobody tells you that it will be the flattest year of your life.

What I actually want to write down is the ordinary part, because the ordinary part is the thing I was promised for five years and it did not arrive in the shape anybody suggested.

He was not restored to me. That word gets used. A child is not a painting.

The boy who came home was nine and could barely read, and had a left arm that would not straighten fully, and could not be in a room with the door shut, and did not know how to be hungry in a house where there was food — he ate everything put in front of him at speed for about four months, and then one day he left half a plate and went to play, and I stood in my kitchen and cried into a tea towel over half a plate of pasta.

He called me Nora until the February.

I want to say that plainly because people find it upsetting, and because when he stopped I was not ready.

He came into the kitchen on an ordinary Tuesday and said, "Mum, where's my other shoe," and carried on looking under the table, and did not notice, and I said, "Hall cupboard," and my voice came out normal, and he went off, and I sat down on the floor.

The four notes turned out to be the way in. He would let me do them at bedtime long before he would let me touch him. For about six months I sat on the floor with my back against his bed, not on the bed, on the floor, and hummed four notes going down until he was asleep, and that is how my son and I got to know each other again — from the floor, in the dark, with the radio on very low.

He is eleven now. He is in year six with a support plan and a teaching assistant called Mr Okonkwo who he would walk through fire for. He reads well enough to be annoyed about being made to. He is small for his age and will probably stay small. He has a temper that comes from nowhere about four times a year and afterwards he is frightened of himself, and we are working on that, and it is getting further apart.

Lily is ten and has decided to be a vet. She is the only person in this family who has never once had to be careful, and she and her brother fight about the television like people who have been fighting about the television their whole lives, which is the single most healing thing I have ever been made to listen to.

Peter has him every other weekend now too. It took eighteen months. He is better at this than I expected and than I have always given him credit for.

There are two more things and then I am finished.

The first is that Sam asked to write to her.

It was last spring. He came and found me and stood in the doorway and did the thing he does where he says the whole sentence in one go because stopping in the middle is worse.

"I want to write to Grandma and I know you don't want me to."

I sat down.

I am not going to pretend I handled it well. I asked him why, which was the wrong question. He said, "Because she's the only one who knows what happened and everybody else just knows about it."

Which is true. That is exactly true, and a nine-year-old who could not read three years ago worked it out and I had not.

He writes about four times a year. I do not read them and she does not write back through me. There is a supervised arrangement and a woman from the service who sits in on the visits, and he has been twice, and he comes out quiet and goes to bed early and is fine the next day.

I have not been. I have not decided anything about that and I have stopped promising myself a date.

What I have decided is that whatever my mother did, she did not do it to him. She did it to me, and she did it with him, and he is entitled to work out for himself what she is, and if I take that off him to make my own life easier then I have done the thing she did — decided, on someone else's behalf, what they could survive.

The second thing is the scarf.

It went into evidence in the November, because Reyes was right about everything, and it came back to us fourteen months later in a paper sack with a barcode on it.

Lily was ten by then. I offered it to her. She looked at it for a second and said, "That's Sam's," in a tone that suggested I had asked her something stupid, and went back to her homework.

It has a hole in it now, low down on one end, from a nail on the gate at his school. He will not have it fixed. I have offered, and I fix things for a living, and he says no.

He wears it from October. It is far too big for him and it always was — it is not tied and it is not knotted, it just goes over the back of his neck with both ends hanging down his front, one longer than the other, exactly the way an eight-year-old put it there on a wet platform because she did not know how else to do it.

He has never once said out loud why he keeps it.

But I asked him, in the first winter, why he took it from her. Why he put his hand out at all, when he had spent five years learning not to.

And he said, "Because it was red."

He could not tell me any more than that. He did not have anything else. Somewhere in a house he cannot picture, that smelled of orange peel drying on a radiator, there was a woman with blue on the ends of her fingers who hummed four notes at him in the dark, and something in that house was red — a jumper, a chair, a blanket, I have never worked out what, and I have gone through every photograph I own.

He was on a bench with nothing left, and a stranger held out the one colour he had kept.

That is the entire reason I got my son back. Not police work, not a database, not five years of leaflets. A colour, in the hands of a child who had not been told yet that you are supposed to walk past.

She still doesn't know she did anything.

I have tried to tell her, twice, and both times she has looked at me the way children look at adults who are being slow.

"He was freezing," she says.
