My Daughter Gave Her Scarf to a Freezing Boy. He Had My Eyes.

Chapter 11 — Tobias

Chapter 11 of 17

The name came from my aunt Deborah, who is not my aunt.

She is my mother's cousin, and she is eighty-one, and she lives in a home outside Marbury with a window over a car park, and I had not seen her in nine years. Reyes went through the family the way they do — quietly, sideways, without saying why — and came back and told me there was somebody I ought to go and see, and that she was not going to be in the room, and that I should not take a notebook.

I said, "What am I asking her about?"

"Ask her about your mother's childhood," Reyes said. "Then stop talking."

Deborah knew who I was before I said. She said, "You're Adele's girl," and then she looked at my face for slightly too long and said, "Oh, you're the one it happened to."

I sat down.

We did about twenty minutes of nothing, which she was gracious about, and then I asked her what my mother was like when she was young, and Deborah folded her hands and said, "Before or after Tobias?"

I said, "Who?"

And a very old woman in an armchair looked at me and understood, in real time, that she had just done something she could not undo. Her face went through about four things. Then she said, "Oh, my love," and she reached over and put her hand on my arm, and she told me.

My mother had a younger brother. His name was Tobias. He was four.

There was a canal at the back of the house they grew up in on Ellerby Lane, with a low brick wall and a gap in it where the towpath came through, and one afternoon in August 1996 my mother, who was fourteen years older and had been left in charge for the afternoon, was on the telephone in the hall.

She was on the telephone for about eleven minutes.

They found him that evening a hundred and forty yards down.

"It was not her fault," Deborah said, and she said it the way you say a thing you have said out loud many times. "She was nineteen. Nineteen. He was a runner, that child, you could not keep a door shut on him. It was not her fault and it was said to her at the time by everybody including his mother, and she has never believed a word of it in sixty years."

I said, "Nobody ever told me."

"No," said Deborah. "They wouldn't have. Your grandmother put every photograph of that boy in a box in 1996 and the box went in the loft and that was the end of it. That was how it was done then. You didn't talk about it, you got on." She patted my arm again. "Your mother got on for sixty years, and I have watched her do it, and I will tell you what it cost if you want to know."

I said I did.

"She could not be in a room with a small child near water," Deborah said. "Not a bath, not a paddling pool, not the sea. She would go and stand somewhere else. She did it at my own daughter's christening lunch and I thought she was being rude and it was nineteen eighty-nine before I understood." She looked out at the car park. "And she has never once said his name to me. Sixty years, and I have never heard your mother say the word Tobias."

I drove home and got as far as the services on the A41 and stopped and sat in the car for fifty minutes.

Because I had just been handed the thing I had been unable to find, which was not evidence. It was a reason.

For five years I had not suspected my mother for exactly one reason, underneath all the other reasons: I could not construct a sentence in which she wanted my son gone. There was no version of her that hated him, no money I could see, no man, nothing. And now I had a nineteen-year-old on a telephone in a hallway for eleven minutes in 1996, and a four-year-old boy, and I did not have to construct anything at all.

She did not want him gone.

She wanted him watched.

I need to put down here, in order, what my mother had been looking at in the spring of that year, because I did not have it in the car on the A41 and I did get it afterwards, and it is the whole of it.

That spring I was not well.

I have written this sentence eight times and I am going to leave it plain. Lily was three. Sam was four. I was not sleeping — properly not sleeping, four or five nights running, and then a day where the walls moved. My GP had put me on something in the February and it was the wrong thing and it took until the August to sort out, by which time it did not matter what I was on.

And in the May, in a supermarket car park, I fell asleep in the driver's seat with the engine off and both children in the back, and I woke up because a woman was knocking on the window, and I had been asleep for about forty minutes.

Nothing happened. It was April-cold, the car was in shade, they were both fine, Sam was singing. Nothing happened.

But somebody had rung the police, and the police came, and they were decent about it, and there is a form.

And I did what you do at thirty when you are frightened and ashamed. I rang my mother in the car park and I sobbed down the phone at her for a quarter of an hour and told her I could not do it, that I was going to kill one of them by accident, that Peter was going to find out and take them.

I said that last part. He'll take them. I said it to her in May.

And she said all the right things and came over that night, and I went to bed and she stayed up and it was fine, and I have thought of that phone call for eleven years as one of the good ones. As the night my mother caught me.

She agreed the sale on Ardley Row four weeks later.

I asked her about Tobias on the Wednesday, in her flat, and it was the last conversation we had as the people we used to be.

I did not ambush her. I want that noted. I sat down and I had a cup of tea and I said, "Mum, why did nobody ever tell me about your brother?"

She did not go pale. She did not drop anything. She looked at me for a moment and then she said, "Who's been talking to you?" — which is not what an innocent woman says, and I heard it, and she heard herself say it, and something in the room changed temperature.

Then she got up and went to the window.

"There's nothing to tell," she said. "He died before you were born. Children died more, then."

"He drowned."

"He drowned."

"You were looking after him."

"I was nineteen," she said.

And then, still at the window, with her back to me, in a completely level voice:

"Is this what you're doing now? Is this what we're doing? You've got your boy back, Nora. He's in your house. He's eating your food. And you're driving round the county collecting little pieces of me."

I said, "That isn't what this is."

"It's exactly what this is." She turned round. "You've been different for a fortnight. You think I don't know you? I have watched you every Sunday for five years. I know the shape of your face when you have got hold of something."

We looked at each other.

And I said — and I had not planned it, and I have never been sorry — "Then tell me why you didn't ask."

"Ask what?"

"On the phone. From the hospital car park. I rang you at midnight and I told you a boy on platform four at Hollis Street had said my son's name to me." I could hear my own voice going. "Mum, I hadn't had a DNA test. I hadn't had anything. I'd had one look at a child's face in the rain. And you said *I'm coming now.*"

She did not move.

"Everybody else asked," I said. "Peter asked. Reyes asked me four separate times, in four separate ways, whether I could be sure. The doctor asked. My own sister-in-law asked. Every single person I told in those nine days asked me if I was certain, because that is the question, that is the only question, when a woman who has lost a child says she has just found him on a bench."

"I believed you," she said. "You're my daughter."

"You didn't believe me," I said. "You knew."

And my mother, who is sixty-three and who has held me up through the worst five years that a person can be held up through, stood in her own front room and said nothing at all for about six seconds.

Then she said, "You should go, love. You're tired."

I stood up and got my coat.

At the door she said my name, and I turned round, and she was standing in the middle of the carpet with her hands at her sides.

She said, "Nora. Whatever you think you have found — I never once let anybody hurt him."

I said, "I haven't accused you of anything."

"No," she said. "You haven't."

And she shut the door.

I got to the car and rang Reyes and told her, and there was a pause on the line, and then Reyes said something I did not expect and did not like.

She said, "Don't go back there on your own again."

I said, "She's not dangerous. She's my mother."

"I'm not worried about you," Reyes said. "I'm worried about the case. Every conversation you have with that woman without me in it is a conversation her barrister gets to describe to a jury however he likes." Then she said, "And Mrs Vale — she's just told you the boy wasn't hurt. Ask yourself how she knows."

I sat in the car outside my mother's flat with the phone in my lap.

Three floors up, a light went off.
