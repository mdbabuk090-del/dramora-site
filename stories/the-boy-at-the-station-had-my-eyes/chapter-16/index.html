My Daughter Gave Her Scarf to a Freezing Boy. He Had My Eyes.

Chapter 16 — What Lily Doesn't Remember

Chapter 16 of 17

Lily asked me in the February, and she picked her moment the way she picks everything, which is to say she waited until I could not get away.

We were in the car. She has always done her important talking in the car, because in the car nobody has to look at anybody.

She said, "Mum. Am I allowed to ask something bad."

I said yes.

And she said, "Why don't I remember him?"

I want to write about my daughter properly, because I have gone back through everything I have put down here and she is in all of it and she is not the subject of any of it, and that is exactly the problem, and it is not a problem I invented on the fourth of November. It is five years old.

Lily was three when her brother was taken.

There is a set of facts about the following five years that I have never assembled in one place before, and I am going to do it now, and then I am going to have to sit down.

She learned to read in a house where her mother did not sleep.

She started school four months after it happened, and I took her, and I have no memory of the day at all. None. There is a photograph of her on the step in the uniform and I am not in it because I took it, and I could not tell you one single thing about that morning.

For two years, every birthday she had was in a house with a missing boy in it. Her fourth was eleven weeks after. Her fifth was in the week of the first anniversary appeal, and I did the interview with the local paper on the Tuesday and her party was on the Saturday and I got through it, and my mother did most of it.

She has never once, in five years, been to a school event where I was not also, at some level, elsewhere.

When she was six she asked me if she was still allowed to be happy, and I said of course, and she said, "Because you go funny," and I said "when," and she said, "When I laugh a lot."

I put that in a letter to my GP. It is the sentence that got me back into treatment. My six-year-old had worked out that her own laughter had a cost and had started rationing it.

So. In the car, in the February.

She wanted to know why she does not remember him, and underneath that — I could hear it, she is not subtle, she is ten — she wanted to know whether not remembering him meant something bad about her.

I told her the true thing, which is that almost nobody remembers being three. That the memories you think you have from three are usually photographs you have been shown, and stories you have been told, and that this is not a defect, it is how the human brain is built, and that everyone in the world is like this.

She listened to the whole thing.

Then she said, "But he remembers stuff. And he was four."

Which is exactly right, and which I had walked into with my eyes open.

So I told her that too. I said he does not remember a face. I said he has four notes and a smell and a colour and a pair of hands, and that if you gave him a photograph of me from that year he could not have picked me out of six women, and that what he kept is not memory the way she means it, it is something further down.

And Lily said, "So he doesn't remember me either."

And I said, "No, sweetheart. He doesn't."

She looked out of the window for about a mile.

Then she said, "Good," and I said, "Good?", and she said — and I am going to put this down exactly as she said it, because it is the most extraordinary thing anybody has said to me in three years —

"Because if he remembered me and I didn't remember him, that'd be like I lost him and he didn't lose me. This way it's the same for both of us."

She is ten. She had been carrying that for four months.

Here is what my daughter has actually had to do, and none of it is in any of the newspaper accounts, because the newspaper accounts want her to be the sweet detail at the top of the story — the little girl who gave her scarf — and they always, every single time, put her in the first paragraph and then never mention her again.

She had to give up her mother twice. Once in the June when she was three, and once in the November when she was eight, because for about six months after he came home I was not really available to her either. I was doing hospital appointments and social workers and police interviews and a boy who could not sleep, and Lily was fine, and I let her be fine, because I needed somebody in that flat to be fine.

She lost her room. We moved him into hers in the end — his was too small and hers was the double — and she went into the small one and did not complain once, and I did not thank her properly at the time.

She lost her grandmother.

I have not written that sentence before either. On the fourth of December, at seven in the morning, my mother was arrested, and by the following week it was in the local paper, and Lily was ten years old in a primary school where every parent reads that paper.

I asked her about it, weeks later, badly, in the car again.

She said, "Ellie's mum told Ellie not to talk about it to me."

I said, "And does Ellie?"

And she said, "Ellie's all right. It's the ones who are nice to me now that I don't like."

There is one more thing and it is the thing I did wrong, and I am putting it in because this account has been generous to me in places and it should not be.

In the January, Lily's school rang.

She had been fighting. My daughter, who has never hit anybody in her life, had put a boy in year six on the floor in the playground, and the school had done what schools do, and I had to go in.

In the car afterwards I asked her what he had said.

She would not tell me. She would not tell me for four days. And on the fourth day, at bedtime, with the light off, she told me that the boy had said that her nan sold her brother, and that everybody knew, and that her family were on the news.

And I said — and I would give a great deal to have this back — I said, "You mustn't hit people, Lily. It doesn't help Sam."

It doesn't help Sam.

She said, "Okay," and turned over.

I stood on the landing for a long time.

Because that is the sentence, is it not. That is the whole of the last five years of my daughter's life in four words, said by her own mother, in the dark, on the day she had finally, once, done something for herself.

I went back in and sat on the edge of the bed and told her I had got it wrong, and that she was allowed to be angry, and that I would have hit him too, and she said, "Would you," in a very small voice, and I said yes.

And then she said, "I don't hate Nan."

"No," I said. "Neither do I. Not all the time."

"Is that allowed?"

"Yes," I said. "That's allowed."

She went to sleep about ten minutes later.

I sat there and thought about a girl who was three when this started and who has been, from that day to this, the only person in the family who has never had a single thing done for her — who was handed a mother made of glass at three and a famous brother at eight and a grandmother in a courtroom at ten, and who has come through all of it as the loudest, rudest, most entirely intact person in my house.

She gave a freezing boy her scarf on a railway platform because she had not yet been taught to walk past.

I keep saying she does not know she did anything.

The truth I have arrived at, three years on, is worse: she does know. She just does not think it is remarkable, because being the person who does the obvious kind thing at the obvious moment is not a special event in my daughter's life.

It is just what she is like, and I did not build it, and I have no idea where she got it from.
