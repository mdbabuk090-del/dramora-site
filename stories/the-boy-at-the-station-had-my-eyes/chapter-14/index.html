Chapter 14 — The Fourth of December

Chapter 14 of 17

Reyes told me the day before, which she did not have to do and which I believe she may have been slightly outside her authority in doing.

She rang on the Wednesday evening and said, "Tomorrow morning. Early. I'm telling you so that you're not asleep and so that the children are not in the room when somebody puts it on the internet."

I said, "What time."

"Seven."

Then she said, "Mrs Vale. Do not ring her tonight."

I did not ring her.

I have been asked whether I thought about it. I thought about nothing else for eleven hours. I sat in my front room with the television on mute from nine in the evening until half past three in the morning with the phone in my hand, and I did not ring her, and the reason I did not is not the reason people assume.

It was not loyalty to the case. It was not moral clarity. It was that I could not work out what I would say after hello.

There is no sentence. I went through all of them. They're coming in the morning. Get a solicitor. Why. Was he cold. Every one of them, when I got to the end of it, left the two of us on the phone in silence at one in the morning, and I could not face the silence, so I sat there for six hours and let it happen to her.

That is a thing I did. I want it written down and not softened. My mother went to bed on the third of December not knowing, and she went to bed not knowing because I could not bear a difficult phone call.

I have since read the account of the arrest three times, in three formats, and I have talked to Reyes about it twice, once officially and once in a car.

They knocked at ten to seven. She opened the door in about four seconds.

She was dressed. Coat on. Her handbag was on the arm of the chair in the front room with her purse and her reading glasses and a folded nightdress in a carrier bag inside it, and her building society passbook, and her house keys had been taken off the ring and left on the kitchen table with a note that said Nora and nothing else.

Under the note was a photograph.

It is a school photograph, eight by six, twenty-six children on tiered benches in front of a mural of a hot air balloon. The boy at the end of the back row is standing slightly apart from the boy next to him.

She had it face down under a note with my name on it and her keys on top of it, at ten to seven in the morning, on a table in a flat she had already tidied.

Reyes did not tell me about the photograph for nine days, because it was seized and processed and because she wanted the print examined before I ever put my hands on it, and when she did tell me she did it in my kitchen, sitting down, and she said, "I need you to understand what this means before I show it to you."

I said I understood.

"You do not," Reyes said, not unkindly. "It means she knew. Not that she suspected, not that she wondered — she paid four pounds fifty at a school office in Coldbrook in the November of the first year and she has had that photograph in her flat for four years, and every single Sunday that she sat in your kitchen and cooked your dinner, she had a picture of him upstairs in a drawer."

I said, "She'd lost him by then. Tarrant was dead."

"Yes," Reyes said. "She lost him in the October and she bought the photograph in the November. That's what people do when the door shuts. They buy the last thing there is."

She had been up all night. That is the finding, and it is not a guess: the kitchen had been cleaned to a standard that Reyes described in her statement as unusual, the bin was empty, there was a bag by the door for the charity shop, and the boiler had been serviced on the second of December by a man who has since confirmed she booked it a fortnight in advance and asked him whether the flat would be all right if it were empty for a while.

She had known since the Sunday. Since the sink, since sometimes when people go digging they find things that don't help anybody. She had had eleven days to work out that it was coming and she had spent them getting the flat ready and paying her accounts and taking her keys off the ring.

She did not run. She had thirty-one thousand pounds in a building society and a passport in a drawer and she sat in a chair in a coat for however long it takes a sixty-three-year-old woman to get through the last night, and at ten to seven she opened the door in four seconds because she had been listening for the car.

The neighbour opposite filmed it on a telephone from an upstairs window.

Eleven seconds. My mother coming out of her own front door between two people in coats, no handcuffs — they do not, usually, not for this, not for a woman of that age who has opened the door — and she is small on the step and she stops for a moment because there is ice, and one of the officers puts a hand under her elbow so that she does not go over.

It was on the internet by half past nine.

I have watched it once. My daughter has watched it, I am certain, though she has never said so, because a boy in year six had it on his phone in a playground in January.

That day is the only day in the whole of this in which I did not behave like a person.

I got Lily to school. I made Sam's breakfast. At about half past nine, when it went up, I turned my phone off and put it in a drawer in the kitchen and shut the drawer.

Then I took every photograph of my mother out of this flat.

There were nine. Two in the front room, one on the fridge, four in the album on the shelf, one in Lily's room and one in mine. I went round with a carrier bag like a woman clearing a house, and I did it fast, and when I got to Lily's — a strip of four from a photo booth at a fair, my mother and Lily at about six, both of them pulling faces — I stopped, and I put it back, and then I took it, and then I put it back again.

I sat down on my daughter's bedroom floor with a carrier bag of photographs of my mother and I made a noise I have only made twice in my life, and the other time was at Hollis Street station.

Sam found me.

He was ten by then. He stood in the doorway for a bit and then he came and sat down on the floor next to me, not touching, about a foot away, which is the distance he has always chosen, and he did not ask what was wrong because he knew, and he did not say it was all right because it was not.

After a while he said, "Do you want me to put them back."

I said I did not know.

And my son said, "I'll put them back. You can take them down another day if you want."

And he took the bag off me and went round the flat and put all nine of them back exactly where they had been, including the one on the fridge, and then he made me a cup of tea with too much milk in it and sat with me until Lily came home.

He was in that flat that morning because his grandmother was arrested for taking him, and he spent it putting her photographs back on my walls, and neither of us has ever mentioned it since.

The interview ran over three days and eleven hours, and she answered no comment to every question they put to her but one.

At the end of the second day they laid the photograph on the table in front of her — the green coat, the café on the Marbury road, grandma on the back in Roy Tarrant's biro — and the solicitor asked for a break, and my mother said no.

And then, on the tape:

"Was he cold? All that time. Was he cold?"

And a detective said, "You'd have to ask him."

And my mother said, "I can't. She won't let me."

Which was true. It was the only true thing she said in three days, and I did not know she had said it until eight months later, when I sat in a courtroom and heard it played out loud, and had to put my head down.

But there is a part of that tape that was not played in court, because it was not relevant to anything a jury needed, and Reyes played it to me afterwards in a room with a table, and asked me first whether I wanted her to, and I said yes, and I was wrong, and I would say yes again.

At the very end of the third day, when they had finished, and the solicitor was putting her papers away, and the recording was still running because it runs until they say the words —

my mother says, to nobody in particular, in a tired, ordinary, conversational voice:

"She'll want to know why I didn't just tell her in the September."

And the solicitor says, "Adele."

And my mother says, "I know. I know."

And then the tape ends.
