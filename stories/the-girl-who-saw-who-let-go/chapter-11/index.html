Chapter 11 — The Corridor Outside Room 214

Chapter 11 of 20

Marlene came to the rehabilitation unit on a Tuesday afternoon with flowers.

Not cheap ones. The kind with the paper wrapping and the little card, and she'd written on the card, and she'd signed it M.

I was in the corridor filling a water jug when I saw her come out of the lift.

I want to describe what happened in my body, because I've never been able to explain it properly to anyone who wasn't there. I didn't feel afraid. I felt cold, from the back of my neck downwards, and my hands kept doing what they were doing with the jug and the tap, and some part of my brain very calmly said: she is going to walk past you and go into that room.

Room 214. Ten metres.

My grandmother was asleep in there with a hip that had been screwed back together nine days earlier.

I put the jug down and walked into the middle of the corridor and stood there.

Seventeen years old. Fifty-two kilos. Standing in a hospital corridor in a school sweatshirt.

She stopped about three metres away and smiled at me and said, "Hi, Tess."

"You can't go in."

"I'm her daughter-in-law."

"You can't go in."

She looked at me for a moment with her head slightly tilted, and I watched her decide something, and then she took one step forward.

And my father came around the corner behind her.

He'd been in the car park on the phone. He told me afterwards he heard my voice before he saw either of us, and that he didn't know what I was saying, only how I was saying it.

He didn't shout. My father has never shouted in his life.

He walked up the corridor and put himself between her and me, exactly like he had in the surgical waiting room three weeks earlier, and he said one word.

"No."

"Roger, this is ridiculous. I brought—"

"I know what you brought."

A nurse had come out of the station and was standing very still behind the desk with a phone in her hand.

"There's a piece of paper being signed on Thursday," my father said. "You'll get a copy. Until Thursday, and after Thursday, you don't come to this building. You don't come to the house. You don't speak to my daughter."

"She's my—"

"You've never once said her name right." He said it quietly and it landed like a slap. "Three years. It's Tess, not Tessa, to everyone in her life. You've never noticed. You've never had to."

That was the only moment in the entire thing that I saw Marlene look at somebody like a person rather than a piece of furniture.

She looked at me.

Just for a second.

Then she put the flowers down on the floor of the corridor — carefully, upright, so they wouldn't spill — and she walked back to the lift and pressed the button and stood there with her back to us until it came.

She never came near my grandmother again.

Thursday's piece of paper was an emergency protective order.

It took eleven minutes in front of a judge and my father had to describe the footage out loud, in a small overheated room, to a woman in her sixties who watched it twice on a laptop and then took her glasses off.

The order named my grandmother and it named me.

Not my father. He wouldn't put himself on it. He said the words I'm not the one who needs protecting, and the judge looked at him for a long moment and let it go.

On the way out he stopped in the car park and had to put both hands on the roof of the car.

"Dad."

"Give me a minute."

I gave him a minute.

"Fifteen months," he said, to the car roof. "My mother sat at my table for fifteen months and did the arithmetic on how much she could tell me without losing me."

"She was wrong about you."

"She wasn't, though." He straightened up and wiped his face with the flat of his hand. "That's the thing, Tess. She was completely right about me. I would have gone white and left the room. I'd have done exactly what I did the first time."

He unlocked the car.

"That's what I'm going to have to live next to," he said. "Not that she did it. That my mother was right about what I'd do."

The detective from Delham County called that night.

Her name was Adaeze Rowan and she'd been a police officer for nineteen years, and she talked to my father for fifty minutes and then asked, at the very end, if she could speak to me.

He put her on speaker.

"Tessa? I've read your statement four times."

"Okay."

"You wrote that she stood still for six seconds and did not reach out."

"Yes."

"How do you know it was six?"

"I counted it. Off the video. Frame numbers."

There was a pause on the line, and when Detective Rowan spoke again there was something different in her voice, something that had come up out of nineteen years.

"I've got three closed files on my desk," she said. "In all three, the person who was there says she reached and couldn't get there in time."

"She didn't."

"I know," Rowan said. "That's what I've been waiting eleven years for somebody to be able to prove."
