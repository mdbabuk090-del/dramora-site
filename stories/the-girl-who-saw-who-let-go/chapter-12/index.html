My Stepmother Let Go of My Grandmother's Arm. The Camera Saw Her Do It.

Chapter 12 — One Is an Accident

Chapter 12 of 20

Detective Rowan came to the house eight days later with a woman from the district attorney's office and a cardboard box.

She was about fifty, tall, with grey locs pinned up off her neck, and she shook my hand first. Before my father's. I noticed that and so did he.

She put four files on our dining room table in a row.

"Kellner. Wexler. Okafor. Vance."

"Vance is still alive," my father said.

"Vance is why the other three are on this table."

She sat down and folded her hands.

"I want to tell you what has been happening for eleven years, because you're going to hear it from a defence lawyer in a much worse way and I'd rather you heard it from me first."

"Okay."

"Every one of these is a good file." She tapped them, one at a time. "Attending officer went out. Statements taken. Medical examiner looked at the body. Nobody was lazy and nobody was corrupt. Every single one of these was investigated properly and closed correctly."

"Correctly?"

"Correctly." Rowan didn't blink. "An eighty-one-year-old woman fell down her own stairs. There was one other person in the house and that person called it in and gave a full statement. There was no forensic finding inconsistent with a fall — and there wouldn't be, because it was a fall. That's what makes it work. She doesn't fake anything. There's nothing to detect."

My father sat very still.

"So how do you catch her?"

"You don't. Not one at a time." She turned the four files so they faced us. "One is an accident. Two in different counties, eleven years apart, under different names — nobody's ever going to put those side by side, because there's no system that puts them side by side. Counties don't talk. Falls don't get flagged. Nobody runs a search for dead elderly people who fell down stairs, because there are two hundred thousand of them a year."

She let that sit.

"One is an accident," she said. "Four is a career."

The woman from the DA's office was called Ms. Adair and she talked about the case the way you'd talk about a difficult building.

"The footage is the strongest evidence I've had in an elder case in my career," she said. "It's also the thing that will lose us the case if I lead with it."

"Why?"

"Because a defence expert will spend two hours on that footage. Frame rate. Compression. The angle of the lens. He will say — and he won't be lying, exactly — that at four frames per second you cannot establish the intention behind a hand movement." She spread her fingers. "A jury watches it. It looks terrible. Then a nice man with a doctorate explains for two hours why it isn't proof, and by the end at least four jurors are wondering whether they were fooled."

"So it's useless," my father said.

"It's not useless. It's the why." Adair leaned forward. "Nobody looks for a pattern until something makes them look. Your daughter made us look. But what convicts her isn't the hand."

"Then what does?"

She pulled one sheet out of her folder and set it down.

It was my spreadsheet. My own spreadsheet, the one I'd made at midnight on a school Tuesday, printed out on a district attorney's letterhead with a case number in the corner.

Four names. Four dates a will was amended. Four dates of death.

"Nineteen," Adair said. "Nineteen. Nineteen. Nineteen."

I looked at my father and he was looking at me.

"Coincidence is a very powerful defence," she said, "and it survives two data points, and it usually survives three. It does not survive four. Four is not a coincidence, it's a procedure, and I can put that on a whiteboard in front of twelve people in ninety seconds."

They arrested her on a Wednesday morning at 6:40 a.m. at her sister's house in the next county.

I found out because Rowan called my father, and my father came into my room without knocking and sat down on the end of my bed and said, "They've got her."

I didn't feel anything.

I've since learned that's extremely normal and I wish somebody had told me at the time. I sat there in my school uniform waiting to feel something and what I actually felt was that I needed to leave for the bus in nine minutes.

"Tess."

"Yeah."

"They're charging her with three." He said it carefully. "Kellner, Wexler and Okafor. Murder."

"And Grandma?"

"Attempted." He was watching my face. "Tess, listen to me. There's a reason for that and it isn't good news. The three dead ones are the strong charges. Your grandmother is the weak one, because she survived, and because the only two witnesses are a granddaughter and a camera."

"So they'll go after me."

"They'll go after you."

He was right.

Ms. Adair told me the same thing, more gently, in an office with a plant in it, eleven days before the trial started.

"They can't attack the pattern," she said. "The pattern is arithmetic. So they'll attack the only human being holding it."
