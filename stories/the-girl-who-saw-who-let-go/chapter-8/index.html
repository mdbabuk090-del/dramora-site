Chapter 08 — Nineteen Days

Chapter 8 of 20

Wills are public.

I didn't know that. I think most people don't. Once a will goes through probate it becomes a court record, and court records are open, and in three of the four counties you can search them from a laptop at eleven at night on a school Tuesday.

Dorothy Kellner. Will amended on the ninth. Died on the twenty-eighth.

Nineteen days.

Harold Wexler. Will amended on the second of March. Fell on the twenty-first.

Nineteen days.

I sat very still.

Then I looked up the third one, the one my father had found in Delham County — Ruth Okafor, eighty-three, whose son had married Marlene Hale eleven months before his mother went down a flight of stairs in Delham.

Amended on the fourth.

Died on the twenty-third.

Nineteen days.

I woke my father up at 1:20 in the morning.

He came downstairs in a t-shirt and read the screen over my shoulder for a long time without touching anything.

"That's not possible," he said.

"It's three for three."

"Tess, that's not—" He stopped. He crouched down so his eyes were level with the screen. "Why nineteen?"

"I don't know."

"There has to be a why."

He went and got his laptop and we sat at the kitchen table until four in the morning, and it was my father, not me, who found it, in the second page of a legal advice website written for people contesting inheritances.

He read it out loud in a very flat voice.

"*In most states, a will amended within thirty days of death may be challenged on the grounds of undue influence or diminished capacity. After thirty days, that presumption weakens considerably.*"

He looked up.

"Thirty days," he said. "Not nineteen."

"Then why—"

"Because you don't kill them on day thirty-one." He was pale. "You kill them at nineteen so the amendment is inside the window, so it looks challengeable, so if anybody ever gets suspicious the fight is about the will. Everybody argues about the money. Nobody argues about the stairs."

I didn't understand it that night. I understood it later, sitting in a courtroom, when a prosecutor explained it to twelve people in about ninety seconds and I watched four of them put their hands over their mouths.

The nineteen days weren't about the money.

The nineteen days were the disguise.

She wanted the families to fight each other over an amendment. A family at war over a will does not call the police about a staircase. They call lawyers. They stop talking to each other for a decade. Priya Kellner-Ross had spent four years furious with her own brother.

That's what the nineteen days bought her.

"Dad."

He was standing at the window with his back to me. It was starting to get light.

"Dad, Grandma has a will."

"I know."

"When did she—"

"I don't know." He didn't turn around. "I don't know, and I'm not going to be able to find out without asking her, and I can't ask her anything right now because she's got a fractured hip and a chest infection and she doesn't know any of this."

"Then we have to tell her."

"Not yet."

"Dad—"

"Tessa, not yet." He turned around then and he looked about ten years older than he had on Saturday. "The day my mother finds out that the woman living in my house has done this three times before, she is going to want to know one thing before anything else. She's going to want to know how long I've been letting it happen."

He picked up his coffee and put it down again without drinking it.

"I need something first," he said. "Something I can hand her at the same time. Something that means she's safe."

That afternoon he called a detective in Delham County and left a message that took him four attempts to record.

She called back nine days later.

By then Marlene had been in our house for two more weeks, sleeping down the hall from me, saying good morning, asking about my exams.

And on the eleventh day of those nine, I came home from school at four o'clock and her car was in the driveway.

It shouldn't have been. She worked Thursdays until six.

The front door was unlocked and the house was completely silent, and from the top of the stairs I could hear a drawer in my father's office being pushed shut.
