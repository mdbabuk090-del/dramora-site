Chapter 10 — What My Grandmother Knew

Chapter 10 of 20

They moved Grandma to a rehabilitation unit on a Friday and my father told her everything on the Saturday.

I was there. I'd insisted. He'd said no twice and then looked at me and changed his mind, and I think that was the moment he stopped treating me as a thing that had to be managed.

He did it badly. He'd written notes on his phone and he didn't look at them once. He started three sentences and abandoned all three, and in the end he just said:

"Mum, she's done it before. Three times that we know about."

My grandmother was sitting up against a stack of pillows with a cardigan over the hospital gown because she refused to be seen in only a gown.

She said, "Which counties?"

Not what. Not are you sure. Not oh my God.

Which counties.

My father's mouth opened and shut.

"Ashford," I said. "Brennan. Delham."

She nodded slowly, once, like a woman confirming a delivery.

Then she said the sentence that took the floor out from under both of us.

"I thought it might be more than one."

The silence in that room went on for a very long time.

"You—" My father's voice came out wrong. He stopped and started again. "You thought?"

"Sit down, Roger."

"Mum—"

"Sit down."

He sat.

My grandmother folded her hands on the blanket. Her knuckles are swollen and she has a wedding ring she hasn't been able to remove for nine years, and I remember looking at those hands rather than her face.

"Fifteen months ago," she said, "she told me a story about a man called Harold. About how sad it was, an old man on his back steps, and how she'd been the one to find him. She told it beautifully. It had a moral about how families should visit more."

"And?"

"And two weeks later she told me the same story about a woman called Dorothy." My grandmother looked at the window. "The same story, Roger. Not a similar one. The same rhythm, the same pause in the same place, the same little laugh at the end about how the neighbours never noticed anything."

"That doesn't—"

"People don't have two of those." Her voice was completely level. "You get one story like that in a life. Two means it's a performance."

"Why didn't you say anything?"

My father asked it very quietly and I heard everything underneath it. Fifteen months. My grandmother had sat on this for fifteen months and let herself be walked up those steps twice a day.

She took a while to answer.

"Because I'm seventy-eight," she said, "and I live in your house, and the last time I said something to you about a woman you were seeing, you didn't speak to me for a year."

He flinched. I actually saw him flinch.

"That was different."

"It was, and it wasn't." She turned her head to look at him properly. "You were forty-one years old and you were lonely and you had a daughter who had lost her mother, and I said something honest to you about Marlene in the second month, and you went white and you left the room. I'm not blaming you. I want you to hear that. But I want you to understand what I learned that day, which is that if I said it again I would lose you."

Nobody spoke.

"So I decided to be careful instead," she said. "I never went up those steps unless someone was in the kitchen. I kept my own bank account. I changed my will."

My father's head came up.

"You what?"

"I changed my will in July." My grandmother said it as calmly as she'd said everything else. "I left the house to Tessa. Held in trust until she's twenty-five, and not through you — through my solicitor. I did it because I wanted there to be nothing in this world that comes to Marlene if I die."

I couldn't feel my hands.

"When in July?" my father said.

"The eleventh. Why?"

He didn't answer.

He was doing the arithmetic and I was doing it faster, because I'd been doing it for three weeks and I could do it in my sleep by then.

The eleventh of July.

She went down the steps on the thirtieth.

Nineteen days.
