My Stepmother Let Go of My Grandmother's Arm. The Camera Saw Her Do It.

Chapter 02 — Two Minutes of Footage

Chapter 2 of 20

There is a particular feeling that comes with being seventeen in a house where you are the fourth most important person.

It isn't anger, exactly. It's more like being underwater and watching people talk on the surface.

I said it anyway.

"You made her fall."

My voice didn't work right. It cracked in the middle, the way it always does when I'm trying to hold it level, and I hated that, because a shaking voice sounds like a lie even when it isn't.

Marlene didn't get angry. She never gets angry. That's the part people don't understand about her.

"She's confused," Marlene said.

Gently. Almost kindly. Like she was covering for me.

My father was standing next to her on the porch and I could see him deciding. Not consciously. He wasn't weighing anything. He was just settling, the way water settles, into the version of the afternoon that required the least from him.

His mother had fallen. His wife had tried to help. His daughter was upset.

Clean.

So I pointed up at the roof.

"Then let's watch the camera."

I want to tell you I said it because I'm clever. I didn't. I said it because it was the only thing in that yard that outranked me.

My father followed my arm with his eyes.

He looked at the little white camera under the porch roof. The one he'd installed two summers ago after a package went missing, the one he'd forgotten about so completely that he'd never once checked the app.

Then he hesitated. And in the hesitation he looked down.

Not at Marlene. At his mother. At the seventy-eight-year-old woman lying on the paving with her cane four feet away from her hand and my sweatshirt bunched under her head.

Something arrived in his face.

He took out his phone.

The footage is black and white and it isn't very good. It's low resolution, there's a timestamp burned into the corner, and the lens makes the steps look slightly curved.

It doesn't matter. You can see everything.

You can see my grandmother mid-climb. You can see the cane in her right hand. You can see Marlene's hand under her forearm, and Marlene's head angled down toward her face, close and attentive, exactly the way a person looks when they are helping.

Then the hand lifts.

It lifts at the precise moment my grandmother transfers her weight. Not before. Not after. There is nothing hurried about it and nothing accidental about it. It is the most controlled movement in two minutes of video.

My grandmother goes down. The cane goes with her.

And then there's the part that made my father stop being slow.

Marlene doesn't reach out.

She doesn't step forward. She doesn't put a hand to her mouth. She doesn't do any of the things a person does when something terrible happens two feet in front of them.

She stands there. Perfectly still. Watching an old woman on the concrete.

For six seconds.

I've counted them since. Six seconds is much longer than you think. Count it now.

That's the whole case, honestly. Not the hand. Anyone can drop a hand.

The six seconds.

My father watched it twice.

Then he lifted his eyes and turned the phone around so the screen faced his wife, and he wasn't half a beat behind anything anymore.

"You did that to my mother?"

Marlene didn't look at the screen.

That's what I noticed. Everyone else remembers what she said. I remember that she never once looked at the screen.

She looked at him.

"Stop being so dramatic."
