My Stepmother Let Go of My Grandmother's Arm. The Camera Saw Her Do It.

Chapter 06 — Dorothy Kellner, Ashford County

Chapter 6 of 20

Priya Kellner-Ross drove down on Saturday.

She was about fifty, with grey coming in at the front, and she brought a plastic file box that she carried in with both arms like it was heavier than it was.

My father made coffee. Marlene had gone to her sister's for the weekend, which my father had suggested in a voice that made it not a suggestion.

Priya put the box on our kitchen table and said, "I need you to understand something before I open this."

"Okay."

"I have been the crazy one for twelve years." She said it flatly, without self-pity, the way you'd state a job title. "My brother thinks I'm crazy. My husband thought I was crazy until about the fourth year and now he just doesn't ask. Two different police departments have been extremely polite to me."

"I know what that's like," I said. "It's been four days."

She looked at me properly for the first time.

"Four days," she said. "God."

Here is what was in the box.

A death certificate. Blunt force trauma, head. Manner: accident.

A photograph of a staircase, taken by Priya herself, three days after, from the bottom looking up. Eleven steps, carpeted, with a handrail on the right side.

A funeral home invoice.

And a two-page statement, typed, from the in-home aide who had been present.

I read that statement standing up.

I had hold of Mrs. Kellner's left arm on the landing. She is very independent and does not like being handled. She moved away from me toward the stairs and I was not able to reach her in time.

I put it down on the table.

"Word for word," my father said. He wasn't looking at the page. He was looking out the window at the porch steps. "That's word for word what she told the officer on Tuesday."

"It's a script," Priya said. "It took me about two years to work out that it's a script. It does three things at once, and once you see it you can't unsee it."

She held up a finger for each one.

"One: she was touching her. Which explains any bruising on the arm. Two: the old woman was proud and difficult, so nobody wonders why she wasn't being properly supervised. And three—" she folded the last finger down, "—she tried. She reached and couldn't get there. That's the one that makes people put a hand on her shoulder afterwards."

I thought about Marlene on the porch with her arms at her sides.

"She didn't reach for my grandmother," I said.

Priya turned.

"Say that again."

"She didn't reach. She just stood there. Six seconds."

"There's a camera?"

"There's a camera," my father said.

Priya Kellner-Ross put both hands flat on our kitchen table and lowered her head, and her shoulders went once, hard, and then she straightened up and her face was completely dry and quite terrible.

"Twelve years," she said. "My mother's landing had no camera. My mother's landing had one witness and the witness was the woman holding her arm."

We went through the rest of the box until it got dark.

The aide's name on the statement was Colleen M. Marsh. The signature was a small, tidy, upright hand.

My father took out his phone and pulled up a photo of a grocery list on our fridge and held the two side by side.

Same hand. You didn't need an expert. Same tidy upright l, same closed o.

"Priya," he said. "Why did they close it?"

"Because an eighty-one-year-old woman fell down eleven stairs in her own house and that happens six hundred times a day in this country." She shrugged one shoulder. "And because there was nothing to take."

"What do you mean, nothing to take?"

"That's what everyone gets wrong." Priya pulled one more sheet out of the bottom of the box and turned it around so it faced us. "She didn't get a cent from my mother. Not a dollar. That's why nobody looked at her twice."

It was a copy of a will.

"My mother left everything to my brother," Priya said. "Her whole estate. She changed it three weeks before she died, and she cut me out entirely, and I spent four years being furious about it."

"Why?"

"Because my brother was engaged." She tapped the paper twice with one finger. "Eight months later he married the woman who'd been caring for our mother. And two years after that, he died in a car accident on a dry road."

The kitchen was very quiet.

"She doesn't inherit from the old people," Priya said. "She inherits from whoever does."
