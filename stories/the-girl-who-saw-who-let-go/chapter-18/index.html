My Stepmother Let Go of My Grandmother's Arm. The Camera Saw Her Do It.

Chapter 18 — Verdict, and What It Cost

Chapter 18 of 20

Guilty on Kellner.

Guilty on Wexler.

Guilty on Okafor.

Guilty on the attempted murder of Edith Vance.

I had prepared myself, over eleven months, for how it would feel to hear the fourth one, because the fourth one was mine and my grandmother's and a camera's.

It felt like nothing.

I sat in a wooden bench in a cold room and heard a foreman say a word four times and I felt absolutely nothing at all, and beside me my father made a sound I have never heard from him before or since, and Priya Kellner-Ross, two rows back, put both hands flat over her face and did not make any sound whatsoever.

Marlene stood up when they told her to.

She did not look at us. She looked at a point above the judge's head, and her face was completely composed, and it stayed composed the entire time.

She has never said one word about any of it. Not to police, not on the stand — except for the six seconds she got wrong — not at sentencing, not in the four years since.

There was no confession. There is never going to be one.

I've made my peace with that too, mostly. Some nights less than others.

Sentencing was six weeks later and it took most of a day, because there were three families.

Priya spoke for Dorothy. Harold Wexler's daughter flew in from two states away and read four paragraphs about a man who had been a postal inspector for thirty-one years and had never once, she said, been late for anything.

Ruth Okafor's family did not speak. They sent a letter that the judge read out, and it was one line long, and I still think about it.

We would like the court to know that she was not a burden to anyone.

My grandmother went last.

She stood up. She wasn't supposed to — she had a stick by then, and she was fourteen months out from the hip, and Ms. Adair had arranged for her to speak from her seat.

She stood up anyway, holding the rail, and she was seventy-nine years old.

"I have one thing to say and it isn't to the court," she said. "It's to my granddaughter, who is sitting there, and who nobody in that house had to listen to, and who made them listen anyway."

Then she sat down.

That's the only part of the entire fourteen months where I cried in public, and I did it in front of about sixty people and a journalist, and I have stopped being embarrassed about it.

Life. Three consecutive terms. No parole eligibility for thirty-five years.

Here is what it cost, because I don't want to write a story where the good thing happens and everyone goes home.

The house had both their names on it. Marlene's share didn't disappear because she went to prison — there was a civil claim from the Kellner family, entirely justified, and there was a forfeiture proceeding, and for two years there were letters from three different sets of lawyers about a building I had lived in since I was four.

We nearly lost it. My father remortgaged at forty-seven what he'd nearly finished paying off at forty-five.

He also had to say, on a public record, in a room with reporters in it, that he told his wife about his mother's will. Two of those nine hundred words in that regional paper were husband and told, and for about three weeks strangers on the internet had opinions about my father.

He never once complained about that. Not one time. I think he thought he'd earned it.

He hadn't. My grandmother said so to his face, in our kitchen, about a month after sentencing, in the flat voice she uses when something is not up for discussion.

"You signed a piece of paper for your mother without reading it," she said. "That's not a crime, Roger. That's a Tuesday."

"I told her about it."

"You told your wife about your mother's errand." She put her cup down. "If that's guilt, then every family in this country is guilty. What she did with it isn't yours."

He didn't answer.

"Look at me," she said. "The one you owe an apology to isn't me and it isn't her. She's standing at the sink pretending not to listen."

I was, in fact, standing at the sink pretending not to listen.

He turned around.

And I discovered that I had been waiting eighteen months for a specific sentence, and that I was about to get a completely different one.
