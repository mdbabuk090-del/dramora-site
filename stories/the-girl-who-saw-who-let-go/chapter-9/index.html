Chapter 09 — The Drawer in My Father's Office

Chapter 9 of 20

I should have left.

I've been told that by four different adults. Go back out the front door, stand on the pavement, call someone. Every one of them is right and every one of them has never been seventeen and standing at the top of their own stairs.

I went up.

She was at my father's desk with the bottom drawer open and a green folder in her hand, and when she saw me she didn't jump. Marlene has never jumped at anything in her life.

She smiled.

"Tess. You're early."

"That's Dad's."

"It's a bank statement." She held it up, angled toward me, entirely relaxed. "I need the account number for the insurance. Your father is not a details person."

It was a green folder. My father's bank statements are in a black concertina file on the shelf, because I have watched him take them out of it every March for as long as I can remember.

I said, "Okay."

That was the smartest thing I have ever done and it was pure animal instinct. I said okay in a bored voice, like a teenager who wants to get to her room, and I turned to go.

"Tessa."

I stopped.

"How is your grandmother?"

She'd asked me that every morning for three weeks and I'd never once answered it. My father had told me not to say a word to her and I'd been obedient about it in a way I was rather proud of.

But she'd never asked me alone in a house before.

"She's talking a lot," I said.

I don't know why I said it. That's not true — I know exactly why I said it. I wanted, for one second, to be the one holding something.

Marlene's face didn't change.

That was the terrifying part. I'd been braced for something to move and nothing moved at all. She just stood there with a green folder in her hand and looked at me with an expression of mild, pleasant interest.

"Is she," she said.

Not a question. She didn't lift the end of it.

"Yeah."

"That's good." She slid the drawer shut with her hip. "That's really good, Tess. She's a strong woman."

She walked past me to the door and stopped in the frame, close enough that I could smell her shampoo.

"You should call your dad," she said kindly. "You look pale."

Then she went downstairs and I heard the front door and her car, and I stood in my father's office for about four minutes without moving.

The green folder was gone.

We didn't know what had been in it, which is its own kind of horror. My father sat on the floor of his office that night going through the drawer piece by piece, and after two hours he sat back on his heels and said, "Property tax. Insurance. Warranty for the boiler."

"What else was in there?"

"That's what I'm telling you. Nothing." He rubbed his eyes. "There is nothing in this drawer worth driving home from work for."

"Then why—"

And then his hands stopped.

He pulled out a plastic sleeve, and inside it was a small square card, and on the card was a printed serial number and a website and the words PORCHVIEW HD — 30 DAY CLOUD STORAGE.

"Oh," he said.

"What?"

"Thirty days." He held the card up. His hand wasn't quite steady. "The footage rolls off the cloud after thirty days. This card has the login on the back."

We both looked at the calendar on the wall.

Day twenty-six.

"She didn't come back for a folder," my father said. "She came back to find out how long she had."

He was already standing up. He had his phone out and he was walking down the hall, and I heard him on the stairs saying a word I'd never heard him use in front of me.

We downloaded the file that night onto three separate drives, and my father emailed a copy to himself, to Priya Kellner-Ross, and to a detective in Delham County he had never met.

At 11:48 p.m. the detective replied.

Four words, no greeting.

Do not delete anything.

And underneath, thirty seconds later:

Calling you at 8. Is your daughter safe where she is?
