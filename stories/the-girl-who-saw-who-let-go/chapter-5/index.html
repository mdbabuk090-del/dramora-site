Chapter 05 — The Name Before

Chapter 5 of 20

I sat on my bed with the laptop on my knees and read that line about nine times.

Formerly registered as: MARSH, COLLEEN M.

People change their names. That's the first thing I told myself. People get married, people get divorced, people hate the name their mother gave them.

But you don't change your first name when you get married.

Colleen to Marlene. Marsh to Hale to Vance.

I opened a new tab and typed Colleen Marsh home health aide and got nothing useful, because the internet is not a filing cabinet, whatever people think.

So I did the boring thing instead.

The state licensing board keeps a public register. You can search by name and it gives you certification date, county of employment, and status. It's designed for families who want to check that the woman coming to bathe their father isn't a felon.

Colleen M. Marsh. Certified fourteen years ago. Ashford County.

Status: lapsed.

Marlene C. Hale. Certified nine years ago. Brennan County.

Status: lapsed.

Two certifications. Two names. Two counties. Neither of them renewed.

I sat there in the dark for a long time.

Because here's the thing about lapsed certifications: people let them lapse all the time. Nurses leave nursing. Aides get tired of aiding. It costs money to renew and you have to do continuing education hours.

But you don't usually let one lapse and then get a brand new one under a different name two years later.

That's not letting something expire.

That's starting over.

I told my father at six in the morning, in the kitchen, before Marlene was up. I had the laptop open on the counter and I'd written the dates on the back of an envelope because I was afraid I'd get them wrong out loud.

He read it twice.

He didn't say are you sure and he didn't say that could mean anything, and I have loved him for that ever since.

He said: "Ashford's about two hours north."

"Yeah."

"And Brennan?"

"Other direction. Three and a half."

He put the envelope in his shirt pocket.

"Go to school," he said. "Act normal."

"Dad—"

"Tess." He looked at the ceiling, at the floor above us where the guest room was. "Normal."

I acted normal for exactly one day, which I think was a reasonable effort.

Then at lunch I opened my phone and searched the Ashford County obituary archive, which is free, because obituaries are the one thing local newspapers never put behind a paywall.

I searched for people who had died in the years Colleen Marsh was certified there. That was too many people.

So I narrowed it.

I searched: Ashford + fall + home.

Eleven results across four years. Most of them were nothing — a roofer, a man on a ladder, a woman on ice in February.

The fourth one down was an eighty-one-year-old named Dorothy Kellner.

Died at home following a fall on the stairs. She is survived by her daughter, Priya Kellner-Ross, of Ashford.

I read the whole thing twice looking for the word caregiver and it wasn't there. Of course it wasn't. Obituaries don't list the staff.

But there was a line near the bottom.

The family requests no flowers. Donations may be made to the Ashford Meals on Wheels program, which Dorothy volunteered with for nineteen years — right up until the month before she died.

Nineteen years of driving hot food to old people's houses.

Right up until the month before she died.

That is not a woman who couldn't manage stairs.

I found Priya Kellner-Ross on the second page of a search. She ran a dental practice. There was a phone number on the website.

I looked at that number through my whole fifth period.

Then I went out to the parking lot at three o'clock and called it, and a woman answered on the fourth ring, and I said the thing I'd been rehearsing since lunch.

"My name's Tessa Vance. I'm seventeen. I think the same woman who was with your mother when she fell was with my grandmother yesterday."

There was a silence so long I checked the screen to see if the call had dropped.

Then Priya Kellner-Ross said, very quietly:

"Is your grandmother alive?"
