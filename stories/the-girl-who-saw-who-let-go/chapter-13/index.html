My Stepmother Let Go of My Grandmother's Arm. The Camera Saw Her Do It.

Chapter 13 — The Defence

Chapter 13 of 20

Her lawyer was called Warren Estes and he was the most pleasant man I have ever been afraid of.

He didn't look like television. He was about sixty, slightly rumpled, and he apologised twice to the judge for his own handwriting. Ms. Adair told me afterwards that he does that on purpose and that he has been doing it for thirty years and it works every single time.

His opening argument took eleven minutes and it was, I have to say this, extremely good.

He didn't call anyone a liar.

That was the thing. He stood up and said that this was a case about four terrible accidents and one family's need to make sense of them.

"You are going to hear," he said, "about a woman who has spent her adult life doing work that most people will not do. Bathing the elderly. Lifting them. Being in the room at four in the morning. And you are going to hear that four of the people she cared for fell down stairs, which is what elderly people do — it is the leading cause of injury death in Americans over sixty-five, and you will hear that from a doctor, not from me."

He walked to the rail.

"You will be shown a spreadsheet," he said, "made by a seventeen-year-old girl on her laptop."

He said it kindly. That was the worst part. There was no sneer in it at all.

"And I want to say now, so that nobody thinks I am being cruel later: I do not believe Tessa Vance is lying. I think she saw her grandmother fall and I think it was the worst moment of her young life, and I think she has spent every day since building an explanation that hurts less than the truth."

He turned around and looked at the jury.

"The truth is that sometimes an old woman's foot goes out from under her on a wooden step and there is nobody to blame at all. That's not a story. Nobody makes a story out of that. So we make one out of the person who was standing closest."

Then he sat down.

I looked at the jury and two of them were nodding.

The trial ran fourteen days.

I can't tell it to you in order. I've tried. What I have is pieces.

I have Priya Kellner-Ross on the stand for a whole morning, holding up perfectly, until Estes asked her whether it was true she had been estranged from her brother over their mother's will — and she said yes — and he said no further questions and sat down, and she stayed in that chair for four more seconds looking at him.

I have a forensic engineer with a scale model of my own back steps, four wooden steps built to size, sitting on a table in a courtroom in front of twelve strangers.

I have the moment they played the footage.

They played it on a screen the size of a door and the courtroom was completely silent and the timestamp in the corner said 4:51 p.m., which is a very ordinary time of day. My grandmother was three rows behind me in a wheelchair and I did not turn around, because I had promised her I wouldn't.

You could hear it when Marlene's hand came away. Not a sound in the video — the video has no sound. I mean you could hear it in the room. Twelve people breathing in at slightly different times.

And I have Estes's expert, Dr. Lindqvist, who was on the stand for two hours and eleven minutes and who was not a liar either, which is what made him so dangerous.

He explained frame rates. He explained that the camera recorded four frames per second and that between one frame and the next there is a quarter of a second in which anything at all can happen. He explained that a hand that appears to release may be a hand that has been pulled from. He explained all of it patiently, in a mild accent, with diagrams.

Ms. Adair asked him one question on cross.

"Doctor, in your analysis, how long does Mrs. Vance remain stationary after the complainant reaches the bottom of the steps?"

He checked his notes.

"Approximately six seconds."

"Thank you. Nothing further."

She sat down.

That was the whole cross-examination. Eleven words. I didn't understand what she'd done until much later, when a juror said it out loud to a journalist and I read it in a newspaper.

He proved the hand was uncertain. Nobody ever explained the six seconds.

They called me on the eleventh day.

Ms. Adair had told me it would be about forty minutes with her and maybe an hour with Estes.

It was three hours and ten minutes.

My father sat in the second row the entire time with his hands flat on his knees, and I was not allowed to look at him, and I looked at him twice.
