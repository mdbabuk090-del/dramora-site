Chapter 17 — The Second Signature

Chapter 17 of 20

He had.

He'd signed it on the eleventh of July at the kitchen table with a mug on it, and he had not read one word of it, because his mother had said sign here, Roger and he had said what is it and she had said paperwork and he had signed.

That is all that happened. I want that to be clear, because of what came after.

Ms. Adair put the copy on the corridor bench between us and my father looked at his own handwriting for a long time.

"That's me."

"Do you remember it?"

"I remember the mug." He laughed, a horrible little sound. "There was a mug on it and I moved the mug."

"Roger, I have to ask you this and I have to ask it now, before Estes does. Did you tell anyone?"

"No."

"Think."

"I didn't tell anyone. I didn't know anything, there was nothing to tell, I signed a piece of paper—"

He stopped.

I watched it arrive in him. I've seen my father's face do that twice in my life and both times were that summer.

"I said it at dinner," he said.

Here is the sentence.

It was the eleventh of July, and it was a Tuesday, and there was chicken, and my father said:

"Mum had me sign something today. She's got the solicitor doing bits and pieces again."

That's it. That's the whole thing. Eighteen words about a mug and an errand.

I was there. I was at that table and I heard it and I don't remember it at all, which I have had to make my peace with.

Marlene was at that table.

Nineteen days later my grandmother's foot came off a wooden step.

My father had to say all of this on the stand.

Ms. Adair had no choice — if she didn't put it in, Estes would, and it would look like something hidden. So on the second-to-last day of the trial my father sat in the same chair Marlene had sat in and said, out loud, to twelve strangers and a room with journalists in it, that he had told his wife his mother was changing her will.

Then Estes stood up and did his job, which I understood even at the time and hated anyway.

"Mr. Vance, you had no idea what was in that document."

"No."

"You didn't read it."

"No."

"Your mother, who you say feared this woman, chose to have you — the woman's husband — witness it, at her own kitchen table."

"Yes."

"Doesn't sound very frightened, does she?"

"She was managing me," my father said. "That's what she'd been doing for fifteen months. She wanted it witnessed by someone who wouldn't ask, so she used the person in that house who never asks anything."

I don't think he'd planned to say it.

Estes let it go and moved on, and Ms. Adair told us afterwards that it was the single most useful sentence anybody said in fourteen days, because it explained my grandmother and my father in one breath and it was obviously, painfully true.

He was very quiet in the car.

"Dad."

"Don't."

"It's not—"

"Tess, please."

We drove about four miles.

"It was on the calendar," he said suddenly. "The solicitor's appointment. It was on the calendar in the kitchen, in my mother's handwriting, in a house that woman lived in. She didn't need my dinner conversation. She'd have seen it in June."

"Okay."

"I'm not saying that because it makes me feel better." His hands were at ten and two, very precise. "I'm saying it because it's true and I've thought about it for six hours and I need one thing in all of this that's true and isn't my fault."

"That one's true," I said.

"Right."

"Dad, that one's actually true."

"Right," he said again.

We stopped at the lights and he put his hand over his eyes for a second, and then the lights changed and we went home.

Closing arguments were Wednesday. Ms. Adair spoke for fifty minutes and she started, not with the footage, but with a wooden step and a woman who had been careful for fifteen months and had told nobody why.

The jury went out at 11:40 on Thursday morning.

They came back at 2:15 the same afternoon, which everybody in that corridor understood immediately, because there is no version of a two-and-a-half-hour deliberation that is good news for the person in the dock.
