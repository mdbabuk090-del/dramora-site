Chapter 16 — Ashford County Called Back

Chapter 16 of 20

Two things happened overnight.

The first was that a reporter from a regional paper wrote nine hundred words about a seventeen-year-old and a spreadsheet, and by Thursday morning it had been picked up in four states.

The second was that a man in Ashford County read it over breakfast.

His name was Denis Okwuosa and he had been Dorothy Kellner's neighbour for twenty-two years, and he still had her answering machine.

I need to explain the answering machine, because everyone under thirty asks the same question.

Dorothy Kellner was eighty-one and did not have a mobile phone. She had a landline with a cassette answering machine that she had owned since 1997, and when she died, her daughter Priya boxed the whole house in four days because that is what you do, and the machine went to Denis with a lamp and a bread bin because he'd asked if he could have something to remember her by.

He'd never played the tape.

For twelve years there had been an outgoing message on a cassette in a machine in a spare room, and under it, on the same tape, four saved messages that Dorothy Kellner had chosen to keep.

Three of them were from Priya.

The fourth was Dorothy's own voice, recorded at 9:14 on a Tuesday evening, eleven days before she died — because that model of machine had a memo button, and because Dorothy Kellner had been a woman who wrote everything down and had recently found that writing was getting difficult.

Twenty-two seconds.

"Note for Priya. If she rings again about the papers, tell her I've done what I'm doing and I'm not discussing it. She's very pushy about that, and she isn't pushy about anything else, and I don't like it. Also the light on the stairs is going again."

They played it in court on the Friday.

Twenty-two seconds of an old woman's voice in a room with a stopped clock, and I watched juror four take her glasses off and hold them.

She's very pushy about that, and she isn't pushy about anything else.

Ms. Adair did not editorialise. She played it once, established the date through Denis Okwuosa in about four minutes, and sat down.

Estes did his best. He got Denis to agree that he couldn't say who "she" was. He got him to agree that Dorothy Kellner had a paid aide, a cleaner, and a daughter, and that any of them could be "she".

It didn't matter.

Because Ms. Adair stood up on re-examination and asked one question.

"Mr. Okwuosa, on that recording, what is the date?"

"The ninth."

"Thank you."

The ninth was the day Dorothy Kellner amended her will.

Nineteen. Nineteen. Nineteen. Nineteen.

That afternoon Detective Rowan came and sat down next to my father in the corridor, and I was two seats along with a vending machine coffee I wasn't drinking.

"Ashford has reopened," she said. "Formally. This morning."

"On the tape?"

"On the tape and on the pattern." She looked tired in a way I recognised by then. "And Brennan is going to. I'll have Wexler open by the end of the month."

"Adaeze." My father's voice was odd. "You said eleven years."

"I did."

"Kellner was twelve years ago. What was eleven years ago?"

Rowan didn't answer straight away.

"Ruth Okafor was my aunt's neighbour," she said. "Not my case. I was a patrol sergeant in a different division and I went to the funeral because my aunt asked me to, and I stood at the back of a church and looked at a woman who had married into that family fourteen months earlier and thought: *I know that face from somewhere and I don't.*"

She turned the cup in her hands.

"I ran her name that week. Marlene Hale, clean, no record. And I thought, well, Adaeze, you've been doing this too long, everybody looks like somebody."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"You couldn't have known," my father said.

"That's the thing everyone says." She stood up and buttoned her coat. "But I've thought about it for eleven years, and I want to tell you both something, because you're going to spend the rest of your lives doing this same arithmetic and you may as well hear it from someone who started earlier."

She looked down at us.

"There was nothing to find," she said. "Not in 2015. There was one dead old lady and a nice woman who called it in. The reason we've got her now is not that anybody got smarter. It's that she did it in front of a camera, and a girl looked up at the roof and remembered it was there."

Then she went back into the courtroom.

And on Monday morning, before closing arguments, Ms. Adair came out to us in the corridor with a piece of paper in her hand and a very strange look on her face, and she said:

"Roger. Did you witness your mother's will?"
