My Stepmother Let Go of My Grandmother's Arm. The Camera Saw Her Do It.

Chapter 20 — The Camera Stays Up

Chapter 20 of 20

My grandmother turned eighty-two last month.

She has a stick, not a cane — she made the distinction firmly at some point in the second year and nobody has been allowed to get it wrong since. She goes up the ramp on her own, slowly, with her hand on the rail my father built badly, and she will not be helped.

She has never once let anyone take her arm on that ramp.

Not me. Not my father. Nobody, ever, for any reason, and we don't ask.

That's the part of her that didn't come back. Everything else did — the sharpness, the terrible jokes, the way she reads two newspapers to be annoyed by both — but she will not have a hand under her forearm, and she never will again, and that is a thing that woman took and does not have to give back.

I'm twenty-one now. I'm in my third year, studying something that surprised absolutely nobody.

Priya Kellner-Ross emails at Christmas. Harold Wexler's daughter sent me a card when I got into university, which I keep in a drawer.

Detective Rowan retired in the spring. There were four of us at the pub and she made a short speech about paperwork and then, at the end, said one sentence about the twenty-two seconds on a cassette in a spare room, and had to stop.

Ashford and Brennan both closed with convictions. There has never been a fifth name, and Rowan told me once, honestly, that she doesn't know whether that means there wasn't one or just that nobody's looked.

I think about that more than I should.

People ask me what the lesson is. Journalists asked, at the time. A woman from a charity asked me to speak at a thing about elder safety and I did it badly and haven't done another one.

Everyone wants it to be about the camera.

Get a camera. Put up a camera. If they hadn't had a camera —

And it's true, and I do tell people to put one up, and Ashford County now has a leaflet.

But it isn't the lesson.

The camera didn't see anything I didn't see. It saw exactly what I saw, from a slightly worse angle, at four frames a second. Everything that mattered was already in my eyes at 4:51 in the afternoon on the thirtieth of July.

The camera's only job was to be believed.

That's all it did. It was the thing in that house whose word counted, and mine didn't, and so I pointed up at the roof — because I was seventeen and I understood, without being able to say it, that I was going to need a witness who wasn't me.

That's the lesson, if there is one. Not get a camera.

Listen to the person whose word doesn't count. They are usually the one standing in the doorway.

We had dinner outside for her birthday because the weather held.

My father did the cooking. My grandmother sat at the head of the table in a cardigan with a blanket over her knees that she complained about and did not remove. Priya came down with her husband, which happens about twice a year now and is one of the strange, good things.

At about nine o'clock my grandmother said she was going in, and stood up, and went up the ramp on her own with her hand on the rail.

Halfway up she stopped and looked back at me.

"You know I said it because I was sure you'd believe me," she said. "On the concrete. *She let go of me.*"

"I know."

"That's not why I said it." She was very matter-of-fact about it, at nine o'clock at night, four years later, on a ramp. "I said it because you were the only person out there and I thought I might be dying, and I wanted somebody to know before I did."

She went inside.

I sat in the garden for a while after everyone else had gone in, under a small white camera that has recorded eleven thousand hours of nothing.

I don't keep it up there to catch anyone. There's nobody left to catch.

I keep it up there because for one afternoon of my life it was the only witness in the world who was going to be believed, and I have never quite got over how close I came to not looking up.
