Chapter 14 — The Girl on the Stand

Chapter 14 of 20

The oath is shorter than you think it will be.

Ms. Adair took me through it slowly. The doorway. The glass. The cane in my grandmother's right hand. The hand under her forearm. The hand coming away.

I'd been coached, a bit. Not on what to say — that's not allowed — but on how. Don't say "I think". Don't say "obviously". If you don't know, say you don't know. Answer the question they asked and then stop talking.

That last one is the hardest thing a human being can do.

Then Estes stood up and said good afternoon and asked me if I'd like some water.

He started with school.

My grades, which had dropped. Two lessons I'd missed in October. Whether I'd found the last year difficult, which of course I had, and he was so sympathetic about it that I said yes three times before I understood that every yes was a brick.

Then he moved to Marlene.

"You didn't want your father to remarry."

"Not at first."

"Did you tell him that?"

"Yes."

"What did you say?"

I could have said I don't remember. It was nearly four years ago and nobody would have blinked.

"I said she was fake."

"Fake." He wrote it down, as though he didn't already have it. "That was your word, at fourteen. Before she'd done anything at all."

"Yes."

"So you decided she was a bad person nearly four years before your grandmother fell."

"I decided she was fake."

"And on the thirtieth of July, when you saw an event through a pane of glass from about eleven metres away, lasting under two seconds — you already had a conclusion available, didn't you? Ready-made. Four years old."

Ms. Adair objected. The judge allowed it.

I sat there.

Here's what I had been rehearsing for eleven days, and here's what I did not say: No. I saw what I saw. Every version of that sentence sounds like exactly what a person says when they're wrong.

So I told the truth instead, which Ms. Adair had told me to do and which I had assumed was advice rather than a strategy.

"Yes," I said. "I already didn't like her. That's true."

Estes waited. He was very good. He let the silence sit so the jury could hear the admission twice.

"But I didn't decide she let go," I said. "I only said it after my grandmother did."

He looked up.

"You'll want to be careful there."

"I'm being careful." My voice was doing the thing where it wouldn't stay level, and for once I didn't fight it. "My grandmother said 'she let go of me' while she was lying on the concrete. I hadn't said anything yet. I hadn't said one word about Marlene. You can check — it's in my statement, it's in the officer's notes, it's in what my grandmother told the paramedic before I was in the ambulance with her."

"Your grandmother was in shock."

"Then so was I. That's your argument for me, isn't it? That I was upset and I made a story." I put my hands flat on the rail because they were shaking. "You can't have both. Either being on that concrete makes you unreliable, in which case why is Marlene's version from that same afternoon so trustworthy — she was there too — or people can say true things on a bad day."

Estes did not answer that.

He looked at me for about two seconds and then he looked down at his notes, and he changed the subject to the security camera app and whether I knew how to use it.

Ms. Adair told me afterwards that this is the closest a defence barrister ever comes to saying ow.

He got me in the end, though. Of course he did. He'd been doing it thirty years and I was seventeen.

At two hours and forty minutes he asked me, gently, whether I understood that if the jury believed me, the woman in that dock would spend the rest of her life in prison.

And I said yes.

And he said: "Does that frighten you at all?"

And I said, "No."

I heard it come out of my own mouth and I knew it was wrong even as it landed. It's the truth. It has never frightened me for one second. But there is no way to say no to that question, in that room, at seventeen, without sounding like something.

Two of the jurors looked away from me.

I stepped down at 4:20 p.m. and my father was standing up before I got to the aisle, and he walked me out into the corridor and didn't say a single word about how it had gone.

He said, "Right. Chips."

We ate chips out of paper on a wall outside the court building in the cold, and neither of us talked, and it remains one of the best hours of my life.

Then, on the twelfth day, Marlene did something Ms. Adair had told us she would never do.

She took the stand herself.
