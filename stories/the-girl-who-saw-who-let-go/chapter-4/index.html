My Stepmother Let Go of My Grandmother's Arm. The Camera Saw Her Do It.

Chapter 04 — A Woman Her Age Gets Confused

Chapter 4 of 20

The older officer was named Prewitt and he had a kind face, which I've since learned is not the same thing as being on your side.

Marlene poured him coffee and sat down across from him with her hands folded.

She didn't deny anything.

That's what I wasn't ready for. I had spent all night building arguments against denials, and she never made one.

"I had her arm," she said. "She's very proud. She pulled away from me on the step and I lost her. I've gone over it about four hundred times and I don't know what I could have done differently once she started going."

"You've seen the video."

"My husband showed me the video."

Prewitt turned his cup a quarter turn. "It looks like your hand comes away first."

"It does look like that." She nodded slowly. "It's a bad angle and it's a bad camera and I have watched it and I understand why my stepdaughter thinks what she thinks."

My stepdaughter thinks what she thinks.

Four seconds, and I'd been converted from a witness into a feeling.

"She's seventeen," Marlene added. "She lost her mother when she was eleven. I came into this house three years ago. I would be surprised if she liked me."

The younger officer looked at me then. Just a glance. But it changed.

"And Mrs. Vance's account?" Prewitt said. "The mother. She's saying you let go."

"She said that on the ground with a broken hip. She was in shock." Marlene's voice stayed exactly level. She never raises it. Not once in three years. "Edith is seventy-eight. She gets confused after a bad night — she's had two falls before this one."

That was a lie.

I opened my mouth and my father said, "Tess."

Just my name. Just once, quietly, the way you'd stop a dog.

I shut my mouth. I have never forgiven myself for shutting my mouth.

They took a statement from me in the front room.

I told it exactly right. I told it in order, without adding anything, without saying I think or maybe. I told them about the six seconds.

Prewitt wrote it all down and said thank you and was very decent about it, and at the door he said the sentence that I still hear sometimes when I can't sleep.

"You did the right thing telling us."

Then, to my father, half-turned, in a lower voice that I heard anyway because I was standing in the hall:

"Between us — with an elderly fall, unless there's a mark or a witness who isn't family, the DA's not going to look at it twice."

A witness who isn't family.

I was a witness who wasn't family. I was the only person in that house who wasn't related to Marlene by marriage.

But that's not what he meant. He meant a witness who didn't stand to gain anything, and in the arithmetic of who inherits what, a granddaughter is not a neutral party.

The camera wasn't enough. I had honestly believed it would be enough.

That night my father knocked on my door, which he hadn't done in about six years.

"They're not going to do anything," I said.

"Not on this, no."

"So she just — lives here. She pours them coffee."

He leaned in the doorway with his arms folded and didn't come in.

"Two falls before this one," he said. "That's what she told Prewitt. Do you know how many falls my mother has had?"

"None."

"None." He nodded slowly. "She said it in front of a police officer, in her own kitchen, with a straight face, and she'd already decided to say it before he sat down. That's not panic, Tess. Panic doesn't come with material."

Something in the way he said material made the back of my neck go cold.

"So what do we do?"

"*We* don't do anything. You go to school tomorrow and you act completely normal." He straightened up. "I've got a call in to a lawyer who isn't ours."

"That's it?"

"That's what I can do tonight."

He said goodnight and pulled the door most of the way shut, and then he stopped and said one more thing through the gap.

"She's had three years to look at me and decide I'm slow." A pause. "I'd rather she kept thinking that a bit longer."

I lay awake for about forty minutes.

Then I got up and opened my laptop and typed her name into the state nursing board license lookup, because three weeks earlier she'd mentioned, over dinner, in passing, that she used to do in-home care.

Marlene Vance.

No results.

I tried Marlene Hale, which was the name on a piece of mail I'd seen in the recycling in February and never thought about again.

One result.

Home health aide certification, issued nine years ago.

And under it, in small grey letters, a line I had to read three times before I understood what I was looking at.

Formerly registered as: MARSH, COLLEEN M.
