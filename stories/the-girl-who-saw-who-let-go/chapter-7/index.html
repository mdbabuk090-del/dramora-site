My Stepmother Let Go of My Grandmother's Arm. The Camera Saw Her Do It.

Chapter 07 — The Wexler Stairs

Chapter 7 of 20

My father drove to Brennan County on a Tuesday and told work he had a funeral.

I wasn't allowed to go. We had a fight about it that I lost in about ninety seconds, because his argument was you are a minor and she is still living in our house and if you don't get on that bus she will know something is happening, and there's no answer to that.

He came back at nine at night with a folder and a face I didn't like.

"Harold Wexler," he said. "Seventy-six. Retired postal inspector."

"Stairs?"

"Back steps. Four of them." He put the folder on the table and didn't open it. "Concrete."

Four steps. Concrete.

Neither of us said anything for a while.

"Who was she to him?" I asked.

"Friend of the family." My father laughed, one breath, no humour in it at all. "That's the phrase in the report. A friend of the family who assisted with his care. No certification that year. No agency. No paperwork, because he was paying her cash and his daughter was two states away and grateful somebody was there."

"So there's no record she was even in the house."

"There's a record." He tapped the folder. "She gave a statement. She's in every one of these, Tess, that's the thing that took me all day to understand. She never hides. She's always the one who calls it in. She's always the last person to see them and the first person to dial 911, and she always, always gives a full statement."

"Why would she do that?"

"Because the person who calls it in isn't a suspect. The person who calls it in is a witness."

Harold Wexler's statement was almost identical. Same three moves Priya had counted out on her fingers in our kitchen.

I had his arm on the top step. He was very independent. He moved away from me and I could not reach him in time.

But there was one difference, and my father had circled it in pen.

Harold Wexler didn't die that day.

He lived eleven days. He was conscious for four of them.

"He talked?" I said.

"He talked to a nurse."

My father opened the folder and slid one photocopied page toward me. A nursing note, handwritten, timestamped 3:40 a.m.

Pt agitated, oriented x3. States repeatedly "she waited". Reassured. States he does not want "that woman" in the room. Daughter notified.

She waited.

I read it about six times.

"His daughter thought he was confused," my father said. "That's in the next page. She wrote to the county eight months later asking them to look at it again, and they wrote back and said the medical examiner had found nothing inconsistent with a fall, and she let it go."

"She let it go?"

"Tess." He looked at me for a long moment. "She was one person, saying one strange thing, about an old man who'd hit his head. Everybody let it go. Priya let it go for twelve years. I let it go for about eleven hours."

That stopped me.

"You didn't."

"I did." He said it plainly. He wasn't asking me to comfort him. "On the porch, before you pointed at that camera, I had already decided it was an accident. I want you to remember that I did that, because I'm going to spend the rest of my life remembering it."

We put everything on the dining room table that weekend, when Marlene was at her sister's again.

Three counties. Three sets of steps. Three statements in the same tidy upright handwriting.

And now I could see the fourth one, laid out next to them, in a house I had lived in my whole life.

My father stood at the end of the table with his arms folded.

"Two isn't a pattern," he said. "Two is a coincidence and a good lawyer. Three might be a pattern."

"It's three."

"It's three falls." He shook his head slowly. "Falls aren't enough. Old people fall, that's what a jury already believes when they walk in the door. We need the reason. We need to be able to say why she does it, because right now the only answer we've got is she's a monster, and juries don't convict on that."

He was right. It was the hole in the middle of everything.

Priya had said it and I hadn't understood it yet.

She doesn't inherit from the old people. She inherits from whoever does.

I went upstairs that night and made a spreadsheet, because I didn't know what else to do with my hands.

Four names. Four dates of death.

And then, because Priya had mentioned it once and it had stuck in my head like a splinter, I added a fifth column.

Date the will was last changed.
