Chapter 15 — The Thing She Said Wrong

Chapter 15 of 20

Ms. Adair had told us it wouldn't happen.

"They won't put her up. She's got nothing to gain and everything to lose, and Estes knows that better than anyone alive."

But Estes had watched me on that stand for three hours, and he had watched two jurors look away, and he had also watched a woman in the second row — juror four, the one who took notes — write something down during Ms. Adair's eleven-word cross about the six seconds and underline it twice.

He needed to explain the six seconds.

There was only one person in the world who could.

She was very good.

I want to be honest about that. Marlene sat in that chair for two hours and forty minutes and she was calm, and warm, and reasonable, and she cried exactly once, at a moment that made sense.

She talked about being twenty-three and lifting a man twice her weight out of a bath.

She talked about being the person families call and then resent.

She never raised her voice. Not once, in fourteen days, in three years, in her entire life as far as I know.

Estes walked her to it slowly.

"Marlene, at the bottom of those steps. The camera shows you standing still. Can you tell the jury what was happening?"

"I froze."

"Can you describe that?"

"I don't think I can, properly." She looked at her hands. "You'd think, after all those years, I'd be the one person who wouldn't. But she was on the ground and she wasn't moving and I just — stopped. I've thought about those seconds more than anything else in my life."

It was good. It was really good. Two of the jurors were with her; I could see it.

Then Ms. Adair stood up.

She didn't go anywhere near the hand. Not once, in an hour and ten minutes.

She asked about paperwork. She asked about the name changes and got four dull, plausible answers. She asked about the cash arrangement with Harold Wexler. She was so boring that I saw a juror in the back row stifle a yawn, and I remember being furious about it.

And then, at 3:15 in the afternoon, in exactly the same flat voice she'd been using about tax forms:

"When you froze — where was Mrs. Vance's cane?"

Marlene said, "At the bottom of the steps, near her right hand."

"Thank you."

Ms. Adair turned a page.

"And Mrs. Vance herself. What position was she in?"

"On her left side. Her legs were sort of — her right leg was turned out under her." Marlene made a small shape with her hand, and it was accurate, and everyone in that room who had seen the photographs knew it was accurate. "Her face was toward the house."

"Her face was toward the house," Ms. Adair repeated.

"Yes."

"You could see her face."

"Yes."

Ms. Adair let two seconds go by.

"Mrs. Vance, you have told this court that you were standing at the top of the steps and did not move for approximately six seconds. The complainant came to rest at the bottom of the steps, on her left side, with her face turned toward the house."

"Yes."

"From the top of a flight of steps, looking down at a woman lying on her left side facing the house — you can see the back of her head."

Silence.

"Can't you," Ms. Adair said.

I have never heard a room do what that room did.

"I— she may have turned—"

"She had a fractured hip and a fractured wrist. Dr. Feld testified on Tuesday that she was unable to change position without assistance and that she was not moved until the paramedics arrived at 5:04."

"Then I'm remembering it wrong."

"You've told us you have thought about those six seconds more than anything else in your life."

"Yes, but—"

"You described her face."

"I've seen photographs since—"

"You described her face and the position of her right leg, which is not visible in any photograph in evidence, because in every photograph in evidence she is covered to the waist by a blanket placed there by the paramedics."

Estes was on his feet. The judge said something. I didn't hear it.

I was watching Marlene's face.

For three years I had watched that face do exactly one thing, which was nothing. And for about a second and a half, in front of twelve people, it came apart — the jaw loosened, the mouth opened slightly, the eyes went unfocused and slid off the line of the question.

Exactly the way it had come apart on my porch when my father told her to get out of his house.

Then she put it back together and said, evenly, "I must be confusing it with another day."

But it was too late, because Ms. Adair had already sat down, and because juror four had already written something and underlined it twice, and because the only way to see a woman's face at the bottom of those steps is to be standing at the bottom of those steps.

Or to have knelt down beside her.

Which somebody did, for a moment, in a quarter of a second that four frames per second could never have caught.
