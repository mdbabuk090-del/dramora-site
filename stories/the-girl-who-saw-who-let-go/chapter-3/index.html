My Stepmother Let Go of My Grandmother's Arm. The Camera Saw Her Do It.

Chapter 03 — She Doesn't Leave

Chapter 3 of 20

My father took two steps toward her.

I was still on the ground with my arm across my grandmother, and I looked up and saw my father's face and I didn't recognize it.

"You never touch her again."

"I'm your wife."

She didn't say it like a threat. She said it like a fact he'd temporarily forgotten. Like reaching for a light switch in her own house.

"And she is my mother."

He pointed at her. One time. I had never seen my father point at anyone in my life.

"Get out of my house."

Here's what I expected, because I was seventeen and I'd watched television.

I expected her to scream. Or cry. Or grab a coat and slam a door.

What actually happened is that Marlene stood there for about four seconds with her face completely open — jaw loose, mouth slightly apart, eyes not focused on anything — and then she closed it again.

Like a hand closing.

And she said, "You should call an ambulance."

Which was correct.

Nobody had called an ambulance. Not me, not my father. Eleven minutes had gone by and the three of us had been having a conversation on a porch while my grandmother lay on concrete with a fractured hip.

She was right, and being right is a kind of power, and she picked it up off the ground and used it.

I called. My hands were shaking so badly I hit the wrong number twice.

While I was on the phone with dispatch, giving our address, Marlene walked back into the house.

Not out. In.

She was on the phone in the kitchen when the paramedics came through the side gate.

I heard exactly one sentence of it, through the glass, while I was holding the gate open.

"—no, I understand that, but I want to know what my position is if he says it to the police."

Not is she going to be okay. Not which hospital.

What my position is.

She had called a lawyer before anyone had called an ambulance.

I stood there holding a gate open for two paramedics with a stretcher and I thought: she has done this before.

I didn't have a reason for thinking it. It wasn't logic. It was the total absence of surprise in her voice.

You don't sound like that the first time.

At the hospital they used the word "displaced" and the word "surgery" and a number of hours that I've forgotten.

My father sat in a plastic chair with his forearms on his knees and stared at the floor.

"She's seventy-eight," he said, to nobody. "She fell down four steps."

"She didn't fall."

"Tessa."

"She didn't fall, Dad. You watched it."

He didn't answer for a long time.

Then he said the thing I've thought about more than almost anything else from that week.

"I know what I watched."

He turned his phone over in his hands.

"I just don't know what it is."

And that was fair. That was actually fair, and it took me years to admit it.

Because a hand coming off an arm is not a crime you can name. There's no word for it. Nobody teaches you the word for the thing where a person simply stops holding you up.

He didn't give me one.

At eleven that night Marlene came down the corridor toward us with two cups of vending machine coffee, and my father stood up.

He didn't say anything. He just stood up and stepped sideways, one step, so that he was between her and the row of chairs where I was sitting.

It was such a small movement. If you'd been walking past you wouldn't have seen anything at all.

She stopped about four feet away and held out a cup, and he looked at it, and he didn't take it.

"Roger."

"Go home, Marlene."

"You're being—"

"Go home." Nothing in his voice. Nothing at all, which from my father is much worse than shouting. "I'll call you when she's out of surgery."

She looked at him for a moment longer.

Then she turned around and walked back down the corridor with both cups, and her shoes were very loud on that floor.

He sat down next to me.

"You're not throwing her out," I said.

"No."

"Dad—"

"Tess, look at me." He turned in the plastic chair so he was facing me properly, and his eyes were red and completely steady. "I'm not sitting on the fence. I want you to be absolutely clear about that, because I know how tonight has looked to you."

"Then why is she—"

"Because right now the only thing I have is two minutes of video and a seventeen-year-old, and she's already told two people you hate her." He rubbed his thumb along the edge of his phone. "If I throw her out tonight she goes somewhere I can't see her, she gets a lawyer before I get one, and everything in that house that has her name on it disappears in an afternoon."

I hadn't thought about that. I was seventeen. I'd thought the video was a door you walked through.

"So she stays," he said, "where I can watch her. And you don't say one more word to her. Not one, Tess. Whatever she says to you, you come and say it to me instead."

"And then what?"

"And then I find out who she was before she was my wife."

He had already started, it turned out. He'd started at some point during the six seconds.

The police came to the house the next morning at ten. By then Marlene had been back for eleven hours, sleeping in the guest room, because the lawyer had explained that you cannot throw a spouse out of a jointly owned home just because you want to.

She made coffee for the two officers.

She was very good with them.
