My Stepmother Let Go of My Grandmother's Arm. The Camera Saw Her Do It.

Chapter 19 — The Sentence I Was Waiting For

Chapter 19 of 20

What I wanted was I'm sorry I didn't believe you.

I'd had it in my head since the porch. I'd rehearsed how I'd receive it — graciously, I thought, with a small nod, like a person who had been right for eighteen months and was too big to say so.

That is not what he said.

He dried his hands on the tea towel and said:

"I should have asked you what you thought about her when I married her."

I turned the tap off.

"You did ask. I said she was fake."

"I asked you when you were fourteen and I'd already decided." He folded the towel, unfolded it, folded it again. "That's not asking. That's checking whether you're going to be a problem."

I didn't have anything to say to that.

"I've gone back over it," he said. "That's mostly what I do now, at three in the morning, and I'm going to stop soon because your grandmother says it's self-indulgent and she's right. But every single time I get to the same place. It isn't the video. I did fine on the video. It's four years earlier."

"Dad—"

"You were fourteen and you had lost your mother and you told me the truth about what you'd seen, and I decided it was a feeling." He looked up. "That's the same thing I did on the porch. It's just the practice run."

The kitchen was quiet. My grandmother, at the table, did not say anything at all, which for her is an entire speech.

"So," my father said. "I'm sorry I didn't believe you. But mostly I'm sorry I taught you it wasn't worth telling me things."

It turns out that's the one you actually want.

I didn't forgive my grandmother for a long time.

Nobody talks about that part. Everyone in that courtroom looked at her like something holy — the woman who stood up at seventy-nine — and I sat in her kitchen for the better part of a year with something stuck sideways in my chest.

Fifteen months. She knew for fifteen months, and she let me eat dinner across the table from that woman, and she let me be fourteen and fifteen and sixteen in a house with her, and she never said one word to me.

I finally said it in February, badly, on a Sunday, about nothing.

She listened all the way to the end.

Then she said: "You're right."

Not I was protecting you. Not you were a child. She'd had a year to prepare something and what she'd prepared was two words.

"I decided you were a child and I was an adult and that made me the one who got to carry it," she said. "That's the arithmetic every old woman does and it's wrong about half the time. If I'd told you in the March, you'd have looked up at that camera in the March, and I'd have gone up those steps two hundred fewer times."

"You could have died."

"Yes."

"You could have died, Grandma, and I'd have found out afterwards that you knew—"

"Yes," she said again. "That's what I'm apologising for. Not the silence. What the silence was going to cost you if it had worked."

I cried in her kitchen for about ten minutes and she let me and she didn't pat me, because she isn't a patter.

Then she said, "Right. Are you going to look at this ramp or not, because your father has done something odd with the handrail and I'm not saying anything to him about it."

He'd taken out the four steps in October.

He did it himself over three weekends, with a hired saw and a book, and he got the gradient wrong on the first attempt and took it all up again and did it a second time.

It's not a beautiful ramp. There's a join near the bottom where the timber doesn't quite meet and you can see it if you know.

He never mentioned it once while he was building it and he has never mentioned it since.

The security camera is still up under the porch roof. It looks down at a ramp now instead of steps, which means the angle is wrong for anything, and it has recorded eleven thousand hours of a cat, and my father has offered twice to take it down.

I said no both times.

I couldn't explain why for about two years.

I can now.
