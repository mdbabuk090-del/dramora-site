She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 06 — She Says She Was Kicked

Chapter 6 of 25

Between the twenty-fifth of June and the fourth of October my daughter told me that Vanessa kicked her into the pool eleven times.

I know it was eleven because I wrote them down. Not as a record of what she was saying — I want to be exact about this, because it is disgusting and it is true — I wrote them down as a record of how often she was saying it, because a woman at the GP surgery had told me to keep a note of "any repetitive intrusive material," and I was being a good father in the only way I had been shown how.

I still have the notes. They were exhibit 41.

> 28/6 — bath time. Unprompted. "She kicked me in with her leg." Calm, not distressed. > 2/7 — in the car. Unprompted. Same words. > 9/7 — after nightmare. Distressed. Says it four or five times. > 14/7 — at breakfast, V in room. Whispered it to me. V did not hear. > 21/7 — asked me why I don't believe her.

That last one I wrote at eleven o'clock at night with the light off.

Here is what she said, and it did not change, not once, across four months and eleven tellings, and I have since learned that this is the single thing that is hardest to fake and easiest to dismiss.

She said she was kneeling up on the chair watching the door for me.

She said she heard the chair — she was very specific about hearing it before she felt it, a scrape on the stone — and then the chair went and she went with it.

She said she did not see it happen. She said that, every time, without ever being asked, from the very first telling: I didn't see her do it.

And then she said, every time: "But she was on the chair with her legs out, and then when I came up she was standing there."

She never once said she saw a foot. She never once embroidered. In eleven tellings across four months, a seven-year-old never added a single new detail, which is the exact opposite of what a child does when she is inventing, and the exact opposite of what a child does when an adult is coaching her, and I sat in a courtroom in February and listened to a very expensive man explain that to a jury for forty minutes and I had to look at the floor, because I had been given the same information for free, in my own kitchen, eleven times, and I had filed it under repetitive intrusive material.

I did ask her once, in the July, the thing you are not supposed to ask.

I said, "Emma, is it possible that you're remembering something that you were frightened of, instead of something that happened?"

She thought about it. She was seven and she genuinely thought about it, which should have told me everything about the reliability of the witness I was interrogating.

Then she said, "What's the different?"

I said, "Well — a dream feels real too, doesn't it. When you're in it."

And she said, "I know. But I wasn't asleep, I was waiting for the cake."

Vanessa knew. Obviously Vanessa knew.

Emma said it in front of her twice that summer. The first time was in the July, in the kitchen, and Emma said it to me with her back half-turned, and there was a silence of about two seconds and then Vanessa put down what she was holding and left the room, and I found her ten minutes later on the front steps with her arms round her knees.

She said, "How long is she going to think that?"

I said something about time.

And she said the cleverest thing that was said in that house all year. She said: "Don't tell her she's wrong. Please. If you tell her she's wrong she'll dig in. Just — let her have it, and let it go quiet, and don't make it a fight."

Which is, word for word, correct child psychology. Every book says it. Priya Naidu said something close to it.

It is also, word for word, the most effective possible instruction to give the only adult who is ever alone with the only witness.

Don't argue with her. Don't examine it. Don't take it seriously enough to test.

I did what she said. For four months I did exactly what she said, and it worked exactly as it was designed to. Every time my daughter told me the truth, I nodded and changed the subject, gently, the way I had been told, and my daughter learned what every child in this situation learns, which is that the truth is a thing that makes the room go strange, and that it costs you your father's attention, and that it is better kept in your own head.

By September she had almost stopped saying it.

Not because she had stopped believing it. She told Detective Vargas in the November, on video, in a room with two cameras and a woman trained to ask nothing leading, and Vargas asked her why she had stopped telling her dad.

Emma said: "Because he gets sad and then he goes and does something in the garage."

The second time she said it in front of Vanessa was the thirtieth of August, and it is the only time I ever saw the other thing.

We were in the car. All three of us. Emma in the back on the left, Vanessa in the passenger seat, me driving down the hill to the retail park because Emma needed shoes for the new school year.

Emma was talking about a girl at school who had been off with appendicitis, and then, in the flat way that children go round corners in conversations, she said: "When Vanessa kicked me in the pool did my appendix nearly come out?"

I said, "Emma."

And Vanessa turned round in the passenger seat.

I saw it in the mirror. It lasted less than a second and I did not understand what I was looking at for another six weeks.

She did not look hurt. She did not look sad, or patient, or wounded, or any of the eleven things she had looked in the kitchen in July with her arms round her knees on the front steps.

She looked at my daughter the way you look at a smoke alarm that has gone off for the third time in a week.

Then it was gone, and she said, in the softest voice in the world, "Oh, poppet," and turned back round, and put her hand on my leg.

I drove another two miles.

At the roundabout by the retail park I had to stop at the lights, and I looked in the mirror, and Emma was looking back at me, and her face said, as plainly as if she had spoken: you saw it.

I looked away.

I bought her the shoes with the lights in the heels, which were eleven pounds more than the other ones and which she had not asked for, and I let her have crisps in the car on the way home, and I did not say one single word to my daughter about what I had seen in the mirror.

That was the thirtieth of August.

The camera was on the wall for thirty-five more days.
