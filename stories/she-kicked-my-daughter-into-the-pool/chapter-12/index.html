She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 12 — What a Photograph Is Not

Chapter 12 of 25

I did not confront her.

I have been asked about that more than about anything else in this story — by people who mean well, in the way that people who have never been in a house at four in the morning with a sleeping child upstairs mean well.

At about half past four on the morning of the tenth of October I went up and stood in the doorway of my own bedroom and looked at the shape of the woman in my bed for a while, and then I went and lay on the floor of Emma's room with a cushion, which is a thing I did four more times over the following weeks and which Emma never mentioned once, though she must have woken up and seen me there.

In the morning I put the camera and the card in the boot of the car under the spare wheel cover, and I drove Emma to school, and then I drove to the police station on Fenn Street and asked to speak to somebody about an entry on their system from the eighth of August.

I did that because of a constable I met once who underlined the word no, and who told me twice to ring if anything else happened, and who left me a door.

Detective Sergeant Paloma Vargas is fifty-one and has a way of listening in which she does not move at all.

I put a laptop on the table in front of her and I took her through it from frame 190, and when I got to 214 I turned the screen round rather than looking at it again, and she looked at it for about forty seconds.

Then she said, "Right."

Then she said, "Mr Rhodes, before we go any further, I need to say two things to you and you're going to hate the second one."

The first thing. She believed me. She said it plainly and early and she never took it back: I think that photograph shows exactly what you think it shows.

The second thing. She said: "It is not going to be enough."

I want to set out her reasoning, because it is the hinge of the second half of this book and because I have heard people say, with total confidence, but they had a photograph — and the photograph was very nearly worth nothing at all.

She turned the laptop back round and pointed at the screen with a pen.

"Right. This is one still image. What's the gap between frames?"

"Five seconds."

"Five seconds. So what a defence barrister does with this — and I'm going to be him for a minute, and you're not going to enjoy it — is he says: my client is lying on a lounger in the sun. She stretches her legs out. People stretch. Her foot touches the frame of the adjacent lounger. That's all this photograph shows: contact. It does not show force, it does not show direction, and it does not show intent."

I said, "The chair's already going over."

"He says the chair was already going over. He says the child, who is kneeling up — kneeling up, on a lounger, which every parent in that jury has told their own child not to do — overbalanced, and the chair began to tip, and my client instinctively put out a foot to stop it, and was too late, and is now being accused of murder for the crime of trying to catch her."

I sat there.

Vargas said, "And the terrible thing, Mr Rhodes, is that that story fits this photograph exactly as well as yours does. Because you have got no frame 213 and a half. You have got no video. You have got the contact, and nothing on either side of it."

Then she went and got a coffee for both of us and came back and said the useful thing, which was: "But it's a door. And you don't get many. So let's do this properly and let's do it slowly, and the first rule is you do not change one single thing at home."

That was the hardest instruction I have ever been given.

I asked how long.

She said: "As long as it takes. And Mr Rhodes — if at any point you can't do it, you tell me, and we'll go and get your daughter and put you both somewhere else, and we'll take whatever case we've got at that moment. That offer doesn't expire. Say it back to me."

I said it back to her.

What she explained over the following hour is the thing I did not know and that I think most people do not know, and it changed everything about how I understood what had happened to my family.

A single act, with no witness and no injury, is almost unprovable.

But a pattern is a different animal entirely. If you can show that a person has done a similar thing before, then in certain narrow circumstances a court can be told about it — not to say she is the sort of person who would, which is forbidden, but to answer a specific question: what are the odds of the same rare coincidence attaching itself twice to the same person?

Vargas said: "Two accidents is a tragedy. Two accidents where the same woman is the only adult present, in the same nine feet of water, is arithmetic."

I said, "You know about Sarah."

And she said, "I read the file on the way in. Five weeks, closed as accidental, no post-mortem toxicology beyond the standard, coroner's inquest twenty minutes. Mr Rhodes — I'm not going to insult you. I don't know that anything happened to your wife. But I do know that nobody has ever asked."

She asked me three questions before I left, and I answered all three badly, and each of them turned out to be a road.

"Who introduced you to Vanessa Kerr?"

I said we met by chance in a car park in the April.

She wrote that down and said nothing.

"Who arranged for her to be Sarah's physiotherapist?"

I said the agency, I supposed. Sarah had a broken pelvis and eleven weeks of rehabilitation after the car accident and somebody organised the home visits and it was not me, because I was working sixty hours a week trying to keep a firm alive.

She said, "Who was it, if it wasn't you?"

I said, "My brother."

"Does anybody stand to gain anything if your daughter dies?"

And I said no.

I said no, and I meant it, and I want to explain how I could possibly have said that, because it is the single most wrong sentence in this whole account.

Emma is seven. Emma does not own anything.

Except that Sarah's mother had died the year before Sarah did, and the house at Ardwell and the money from it had gone to Sarah, and Sarah's will had put it in trust — because that is what solicitors tell you to do, and because Sarah was thirty-eight and thought she had forty years to change it — for Emma, at twenty-five.

With a clause about what happens if Emma does not reach twenty-five.

I had signed that document. I had been in the room. It was four hundred thousand pounds and a house, in the summer that Sarah's mother died, and I had not thought about it for three years because it had nothing to do with me: I could not touch it and I did not want it, and Emma was four.

I did not remember any of that in Vargas's office on the tenth of October. I remembered it eleven days later, at half past six in the morning, standing in my kitchen with the kettle going, and I had to sit down on the floor.

Because the residuary beneficiary — the person the trust falls back to if the child does not survive to twenty-five — was Sarah's estate.

And Sarah's estate, after everything, came to me.

And I was married to Vanessa.
