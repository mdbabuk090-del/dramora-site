She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 02 — Ninety Seconds

Chapter 2 of 25

The ambulance took eleven minutes and I know that because Vanessa told the operator the gate code twice and the second time she said it slowly, and I have the recording.

You can request them. I did not know that either, in June. There is a form.

The two paramedics came round the side of the house at a walk that turned into a run when they saw the deck, and the first one — a very young man with a shaved head and a wedding ring worn on a chain because of the gloves, his name was Owen Petrie and he came to the trial — went down on his knees beside Emma and put two fingers on her neck and said, "All right. All right, sweetheart," in a voice like he was carrying on a conversation they had started earlier.

They put a mask on her and they put a blanket round her and they asked me how long she had been in the water.

I said I didn't know.

He said, "Best guess. It matters."

And I could not give him one, because I had been in the utility room with a cake in my hands, and I said so, and he looked past me at Vanessa.

She was standing at the top of the steps with the cream robe pulled shut and held closed at the throat with one fist, and she was shaking. I want to be fair. She was genuinely shaking. I have gone back over that evening looking for the seam and I cannot find it there — whatever she was doing that night, her body was doing it too.

She said, "A minute? Maybe a minute and a half. I don't know. It happened so fast."

Petrie said, "But you saw her go in."

And Vanessa said the sentence. The first version of it. The one that everything else grew out of.

She said, "She was leaning over for the oranges. The dish was on the cooler. She reached across for the dish and the lounger tipped and she went in. I couldn't get to her. I can't swim."

Then she put both hands over her face and said, "I screamed. That's all I did. I just screamed."

Petrie wrote it down on a glove.

The thing about I can't swim is that it is true.

Vanessa Rhodes could not swim. She had told me so in the first month I knew her, standing at that same pool with her shoes in her hand, and I had offered to teach her and she had laughed and said absolutely not, and for two years she had gone in no further than the second step of the shallow end, and half a dozen people who had been at that house on other days would say so under oath. Her friend Dana said so. Marta said so. I said so.

It is the strongest thing she had, and she did not have to build it. It was already there.

That is the shape of the whole thing, and I did not see the shape for a very long time. She hardly ever lied. She just stood where the truth happened to be pointing.

They took Emma down the hill to St Brendan's with the blue lights going and no siren, which I asked about later and which apparently means nothing at all.

I went in the ambulance. Vanessa followed in her car and got there eight minutes after us.

I sat on the fold-down seat with my wet clothes soaking into it and I held two fingers of Emma's left hand, and Petrie's partner talked to me the entire way in a flat, kind, professional voice, telling me things about oxygen and cold water and the reflexes of children, and I understood one word in four.

At some point she said, "You got her out fast. That's the thing that matters, love. That's the whole ball game."

And I said, "I wasn't there."

And she said, "You were there enough."

I have thought about that more than she will ever know.

Emma was intubated at eight minutes past seven in the evening and extubated at eleven the following morning and she did not have a single seizure, and by the standards of the ward she was through the door and out the other side inside twenty hours.

I know now — because I asked, because I read, because for about six weeks I did nothing at night except read — that a child that age, in water that temperature, with a bystander pulling her out inside about two minutes and starting rescue breaths, is in the good part of the curve. The paediatric consultant, Dr Ilesanmi, told me on the second morning that if he had to guess he would say she was face-down for something under ninety seconds.

Ninety seconds. He picked the number out of the air and put it down in front of me, and he had no way of knowing that it was the same number as the cake.

I have never been able to put it down since.

Here is what I did with it.

For four months I made that number mean one thing: I chose the cake. I had stood up off a lounger on my daughter's birthday and gone to fetch a sponge with a sugar turtle on it, and while I was gone she had leaned out for a piece of orange, and the whole rest of her life had turned on my ninety seconds of walking.

I have since been told by three professionals that this is an ordinary and even predictable thing for a parent to do and that it has a name, and I do not care what its name is. I want to describe what it does, because it is load-bearing in this story.

It made me useless.

A man who believes that the accident was his fault does not look for a second explanation. He is already holding the explanation. It is heavy and it fits and he can carry it round all day, and every time somebody hands him a new piece — a lounger lying the wrong way, a child who will not be left in a room, a cook who gets sent home early — he cannot pick the piece up, because his hands are full.

For four months I could not pick anything up.

Vanessa did not have to do anything at all to keep me from looking at her. My guilt did the whole job for her, for free, all summer.

They put Emma in bed nine of the children's high-dependency unit, which is the one by the window, and I sat on a plastic chair beside her from a quarter past eight that Sunday night.

Vanessa came in at nine with a bag of my clothes from the house — she had thought to bring dry clothes, and shoes, and my charger, and she had put in the shirt I would have picked myself — and she stood in the doorway with it and she was crying, and she said, "I keep seeing it."

I got up and I put my arms round her because that is what you do, and over her shoulder I looked at my daughter with a tube in her mouth.

She stayed till one in the morning. She came back at seven. She did that for three nights, and the nurses on that ward — good, tired, watchful women who see the whole range of human families come through those doors — all of them said the same thing to me at some point in that week, and one of them said it while writing on a chart without looking up.

"She's very good, your wife."

I said, "I know."

The camera was still on the wall at home, one frame every five seconds, working its way through an empty deck and a tipped-over lounger and nine halves of orange going slowly still on the water, until the battery died at twenty-four minutes to eight and left one thousand one hundred and six photographs on a card that nobody asked about for four months.
