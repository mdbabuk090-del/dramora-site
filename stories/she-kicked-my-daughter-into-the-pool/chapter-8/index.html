She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 08 — Marta's Kitchen

Chapter 8 of 25

Marta Ferreira cooked in that house for eleven years and was let go on the ninth of August with a month's pay and a reference that Vanessa wrote and I signed without reading.

I have apologised to her twice. The first time she waved it off. The second time, in a courtroom corridor in the February with her handbag on her knees, she let me finish, and then she said, "Michael. I am sixty years old, I found another job in three weeks. Apologise to the girl."

Marta believed Emma from the first week and she never once wavered, and she was the only adult in the story who managed that, and the reason is not that she was cleverer than the rest of us.

It is that she was not related to any of it. She had no ninety seconds. She was not standing in a utility room holding a cake when it happened, so she did not have to spend the summer holding a story about herself in one hand while she tried to pick up a story about somebody else with the other.

She had eleven years of watching a family from the one place in a house where you actually see it, which is the kitchen, and she had two facts, and she just — added them up.

The first fact.

In July, in the kitchen, Emma said it in front of her.

Marta did not say "you were poorly, darling." Marta put down a wooden spoon and turned round and said, "She kicked you?"

Emma said yes.

Marta said, "With her foot or with her leg?"

And Emma, who had told me eleven times and had never been asked a single question about it, said: "Her leg. Like this," and put her own leg out straight from the chair she was kneeling on.

Marta told me afterward that she had asked because she wanted to know if the child would change it under pressure. She said, "Children change it. You ask twice, they make it bigger, because they want to keep you looking at them. She made it smaller. She said *I didn't see it, I only felt it.*"

I said, "Why didn't you tell me?"

And Marta said, "Michael. I told you on the fourteenth of July, in this kitchen, and you said thank you and you went out to the garage."

I have no memory of that at all. None. She is not a liar, and she gave the date without being asked, and I believe her completely, and there is nothing I can do with it.

The second fact.

This is the one that mattered, and she carried it around for seven weeks not knowing it was worth anything, because it was such a small domestic nothing that it did not occur to her that it was a fact at all.

On the Sunday — the fourteenth of June, the day of it — Marta was rostered to work until seven in the evening. Birthday supper, cold, laid out at half past seven, which is why there was a cake in the second refrigerator in the utility room in the first place.

At ten past three that afternoon Vanessa came into the kitchen and told her to go home.

She did it beautifully. She did not order her out. She said: Marta, it's their day, it's just the three of them, and Michael's got the cake, and honestly the supper's all done, and you've got your sister's thing this evening, haven't you — go on. Go.

Marta said, "But I said, the supper is not laid."

And Vanessa said, "I'll lay it."

Marta told me this on the twenty-second of August with her coat already on, standing by the door, in the middle of a conversation about something else entirely. She said it the way you would say oh, and the milkman came Tuesday.

She said: "She has never laid a table in this house in two years."

I did not do anything with it.

There it is again. That is the drumbeat of this entire book and I am not going to hide it: I was told, and I did not do anything.

But I want to try to say what receiving that information was actually like in the August, because "he ignored it" is not right either.

It was like being handed one piece of a jigsaw with no picture on the box. A woman sends the cook home four hours early on a Sunday. That is not sinister. That is a woman who wants an afternoon alone with her husband and stepdaughter, and if I had heard it in any other year of my life I would have thought it was rather sweet.

The only way that fact turns into a knife is if you already have the other pieces. If you have the fruit was on the wrong side, and the child says she was kicked and has never once changed it, and the deep end is where the woman's predecessor died, then she sent the cook home stops being domestic and starts being arrangement.

I had the pieces. I had all four of them by the twenty-second of August. I had them in the same head at the same time.

I did not lay them out on a table next to each other, because to lay them on a table you have to have a table, and a table is a thing you build when you have already decided to look.

Detective Vargas laid them out in eleven minutes in the November, in a room at the top of a police station, with a biro, on the back of a printed email. She wrote them in a vertical list. Then she drew a line under them and looked at me and said: "Mr Rhodes, has anybody ever sat down with you and put these four things on one piece of paper before?"

I said no.

She said, "No. Because each of them, on its own, belongs to a different person. And none of you talk to each other."

Vanessa let Marta go two and a half weeks after the table conversation.

The reason she gave me was this: that Marta was "encouraging" Emma, that she had heard her in the kitchen asking the child leading questions about the accident, that Emma was becoming more fixed on it and not less, and that Naidu had said the single most important thing for recovery was for the story not to be reinforced by adults.

All of which was, again, entirely correct in form. Naidu had said something like that. Marta had asked the child questions.

I said, "She's been here eleven years."

And Vanessa said: "I know. And I'll cry about it. But she's making Emma worse, and if it were the other way round — if it were me making Emma worse — you'd have got rid of me by now, wouldn't you?"

I said, "Of course."

And she said, "Well, then."

Here is what actually happened in the six months after Marta Ferreira left that house.

Emma stopped saying it.

The last of the eleven times is dated the second of September in my own handwriting. After Marta went, there was no adult left in the building who had ever, once, in the child's hearing, treated the sentence she kicked me as a piece of information rather than as a symptom.

There was me, who nodded and changed the subject.

And there was Vanessa, who was kind about it.

That is what she was buying. Not silence — she was cleverer than silence. She was buying the slow, ordinary, entirely deniable process by which a seven-year-old learns that a true thing is socially expensive, and stops.

It took her seven weeks and it cost her one cook.

And it would have worked — I want to be very clear that it would have worked completely, that we would be a family of three in that house today and Emma would be eleven years old and would have long ago been told by kind professionals that what she remembers is a thing her brain made up — if my daughter had not, in the first week of October, asked me for her mother's camera.
