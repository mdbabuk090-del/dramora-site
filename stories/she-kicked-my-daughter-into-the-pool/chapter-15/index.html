She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 15 — Helen Brandt

Chapter 15 of 25

Helen Brandt is a paediatrician at St Brendan's, which is the hospital my daughter was taken to, and she was Sarah's closest friend for nineteen years, and after the funeral she and I managed about four conversations before we stopped.

That is not a falling-out. Nobody said anything. It is just that Helen was the person Sarah rang, and after Sarah there was no reason for the two of us to be in a room, and grief does that: it takes the whole shape of a person's life and quietly removes the parts of it that were held in place by the person who died.

I rang her on the nineteenth of November, at Vargas's request, from a police station, with a detective sitting across a table not saying anything.

She picked up on the second ring and said, "Michael."

And I said, "Helen, I need to ask you about the week Sarah died," and there was a silence of about four seconds, and then Helen Brandt said:

"Oh, thank God."

She came in the next morning. She brought her phone, and the old phone it had been backed up from, and a printed email from her own solicitor, because she is that sort of person.

And she said, before anybody asked her anything: "I have a voicemail from Sarah from the fourth of March and I have never listened to the end of it."

Vargas said, "How much of it have you heard?"

Helen said, "Eleven seconds. Maybe twelve."

She said she had listened to the beginning of it on the sixth of March, the day she found out, at about two in the afternoon, standing in a corridor at work, and had got as far as Sarah saying her name and starting a sentence, and had stopped it and put the phone in her pocket.

And then, because it was the last thing that existed of a person, she had moved it from phone to phone for two and a half years, four handsets, never once opening it.

She said, "I'm a doctor. I'm perfectly aware of what that is."

They played it in a room with three people and a recording device and a box of tissues, and I asked to be there and Vargas said no, absolutely not, and she was completely right and I hated her for about a day.

I have heard it since. It is forty-one seconds long. It is my wife's voice, which I had not heard in two and a half years because we did not video anything, because there was going to be plenty of time.

I am going to put down what she says, because it went in front of a jury and because it is the closest thing to a witness statement that Sarah Rhodes ever got to give.

> "Hels, it's me. Sorry, it's late. Listen — can I ask you something horrible? Because I think I've got something wrong and I can't ask Michael, he's — he's got enough. > > The physio brought a form. In January. I've just found it again. It's a life thing, an insurance life thing, and I signed it because she said it was the agency's, for the home visits, that they all have to have it now, and I didn't read it, Hels, I didn't read a word of it because I'd had two of the codeine and I'd have signed anything. > > And it's not the agency's. > > And Gil witnessed it. Gil's signature's on it, and Gil hasn't been in this house since Boxing Day. > > [pause — eleven seconds] > > I'm probably being mad. I'm going to ask her tomorrow. She's coming for the plinth. > > Ring me back? Don't ring the house. Ring my mobile."

There is a thing that happened in that room afterward that I think about more than the voicemail.

Helen Brandt sat with her hands flat on the table and said, "She rang me at eleven at night and I was in bed and I saw it was her and I thought, I'll do it tomorrow."

And Vargas — who I have watched be extremely hard with people who deserved it — said: "Dr Brandt. Sarah rang you because she wanted a second opinion on a piece of paper. Nobody on this earth would have heard that message and thought my friend is in danger tonight. Not me. Not you. You'd have rung her back on the Thursday and she'd have told you it was all a fuss."

Helen said, "But she died on the Wednesday."

"Yes," Vargas said. "That's the point of the Wednesday."

Two things came out of that recording and both of them were doors.

The first is the word she. Not the agency — she. Sarah, three weeks before she died, using a pronoun in a voicemail, put Vanessa Kerr's hand on that document. In the February a barrister spent an hour and a half trying to break that pronoun and could not.

The second is the thing Sarah did not know she was recording, which is a date.

She says: she's coming for the plinth. On the evening of the fourth of March. Which means Sarah expected that visit — it was arranged, it was ordinary — and it means that at ten past eleven on the fourth of March, Sarah Rhodes was alive, was angry, and was intending to ask her physiotherapist a direct question about an insurance form in less than twenty-four hours.

Vargas said: "Right. So now we know what the fourth of March was for."

I said, "She asked her."

"She asked her," Vargas said. "And the only reason we have any of this at all is that on the fourth of March, Vanessa Kerr believed she was talking to a woman who hadn't told anybody."

Helen said one more thing before she left, in the doorway, with her coat on, and it is the answer to a question I asked in chapter fourteen.

I said, "Sarah told me she was thorough. In the January. She said, she's fine, she's thorough. Do you know what she meant?"

And Helen Brandt said: "Yes. She said it to me too, and she said it laughing, and then she said the rest of the sentence, which she obviously didn't say to you."

I said, "What was the rest of the sentence?"

Helen said: "*She's thorough. She knows where everything in my house is and I've never told her.*"
