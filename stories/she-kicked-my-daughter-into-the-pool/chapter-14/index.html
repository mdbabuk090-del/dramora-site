Chapter 14 — The Woman Who Came to Help

Chapter 14 of 25

Vanessa Kerr came into my house for the first time on the eleventh of December, three months before Sarah died, carrying a folded plinth and a bag of resistance bands, and I was not there.

I have had to reconstruct that winter almost entirely from other people, because the man who lived through it was doing sixty-hour weeks at a construction firm that was four months from going under, and had a wife with a fractured pelvis and a four-year-old and no capacity left over for anything at all.

That is the first thing I want to say about how a person like Vanessa gets into a family.

She does not break in. She is let in, through a door that somebody in the household is holding open, at a moment when everybody is too tired to look at who is coming through it.

The door was my brother.

Gil is three years younger than me and owns forty per cent of Rhodes & Ivers, which our grandfather started and our father ran and which builds unglamorous things very well: school extensions, three care homes, a swimming pool complex in Marbury that we were extremely proud of in 2011.

He has been in my life every day of it. He taught Emma to ride a bike, in my garden, holding the saddle, running.

When Sarah came out of hospital in the December it was Gil who said he knew someone. He said it in my kitchen with a beer, in the way you say a helpful thing: that the agency waiting list was nine weeks, and that he knew a physio through the gym, someone who did private home rehab, and that she was very good, and that she could start next week.

I said yes without asking a single question, and I have thought about that a great deal. If a stranger had rung the doorbell I would have asked for registration numbers. Because it came through my brother it came pre-cleared, and it did not occur to me for one second that Gil might be the point of entry rather than the middleman, because he was my brother, and because the entire architecture of a family is that certain doors are already open and nobody stands guard at them.

She came three times a week from the eleventh of December to the twenty-eighth of February. Tuesday, Thursday, Saturday morning. Eleven weeks. Somewhere in the region of thirty-four visits, each about seventy minutes, in a house with a woman who could not walk unaided.

I was present, in total, for four of them.

What I remember of the four: she was quiet, she was competent, she talked to Sarah about ordinary things while she worked and did not talk to her the way people talk to the injured. Sarah did not dislike her. I asked. In the January I asked whether she wanted a different physio and Sarah said no, she's fine, she's — and then she said a word that I did not think about again for two and a half years.

She said: "She's thorough."

I did not ask what she meant. I have asked ten different people since what they think Sarah meant by it, and Helen Brandt is the only one who claims to know, and Helen's answer is in the next chapter.

What Vanessa Kerr was doing in that house for seventy minutes at a time, three times a week, for eleven weeks, with a woman who could not stand up, is the thing Vargas's team spent four months establishing, and it comes down to three items.

One. She organised the medication.

Not prescribed — she had no authority to prescribe anything and never claimed to. She organised it. There was a plastic weekly pill organiser on the shelf in the kitchen with fourteen compartments, and at some point in the January it stopped being filled by Sarah and started being filled by the physiotherapist at the end of the Saturday session, because Sarah's hands were bad in the mornings and because it was, genuinely, a helpful thing to do.

Thirty-four visits. Eleven weeks of being the person who knew exactly what was in that house, in what quantity, and when it would run out.

I did not know this. Marta knew it — Marta saw it every Saturday and thought it was kindness, and told the police so in a statement in the December, and told them the exact position of the organiser on the shelf, because she had wiped that shelf twice a week for eleven years.

Two. She learned the house.

The gate code. Which door does not lock unless you lift it. That the pool light switch is inside the utility room and not on the deck. That Michael Rhodes goes up at half past ten and sleeps like the dead. That there is a four-year-old at the top of the stairs who does not wake.

You cannot subpoena that. It does not exist on any record anywhere. It is simply what a person has after thirty-four visits, and no ordinary friend of the family would ever have accumulated it.

Three. She learned Sarah.

This is the one I cannot be objective about, so I am going to put down what Vargas said and leave it.

She said: "Eleven weeks, seventy minutes at a time, one-to-one, in her own home, with a woman in chronic pain who can't get out of a chair by herself. Mr Rhodes, that is more concentrated access to a person than most marriages get in a year. She knew what your wife was frightened of. She knew what she was ashamed of. She knew what she'd do and wouldn't do, and she knew — because everybody who came into that house that winter knew — that the one thing on earth Sarah Rhodes wanted was to get back in that water."

The last documented rehabilitation session was the twenty-eighth of February, and it was a good day, and it is the day Sarah went into the pool and I lifted her out and we both cried.

Vanessa Kerr was in the house that afternoon. She was there when it happened. She stood on the deck with a towel over her arm and watched a woman with a healing pelvis get into two metres ten of water and be physically unable to get out of it without a second person.

Six days later Sarah was face-down at that end.

And on the fourth of March, at seven in the evening, twenty-six hours before, the agency's system says she attended for an *equipment collection*: the plinth, the bands, the ankle weights.

She had no clinical reason to be in that house that evening. She was collecting furniture.

Nobody has ever been able to establish what was said in that visit, because there were three people present and one is dead and one has never given an account and one was four years old.

But Sarah rang Helen Brandt at ten past eleven that night — the fourth, not the fifth — and left a voicemail forty-one seconds long, and Helen Brandt kept it for two and a half years without ever once listening to it all the way through, because it arrived in a week that ended with her best friend dying and she could not bear it.

I have one more thing for this chapter and it is the smallest and it is the one that finished me.

The supermarket car park. The twelfth of April, five weeks after the funeral. The chance meeting where a woman I dimly recognised put her hand on my arm and said she was so terribly sorry, and cried, and told me she had thought about writing.

I have that car park on CCTV now. Vargas got it because it was still within retention on a commercial system that keeps everything for three years, and I sat in a room and watched fourteen minutes of it.

Her car is already there when I arrive. It has been there for fifty-one minutes.

She does not go into the shop at any point.

She is sitting in a silver Astra in the third row with a view of the trolley bay, and when my car comes in she waits eleven minutes — until I come out with two bags and a four-year-old — and then she gets out.

I watched it four times.

The thing I could not stop watching is not her. It is me: a man in a coat crossing a car park with his daughter's hand in his, eleven weeks a widower, walking straight at it, with absolutely no idea that anything at all is happening.
