She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 13 — The Fifth of March

Chapter 13 of 25

I have told the night Sarah died about two hundred times. To police once, badly, at the time. To a coroner's officer. To four separate friends in the first month. To Emma, in a smoothed-down version, for two years.

It has become a thing I recite, and reciting is the enemy of remembering, and by the October I could get through the whole of it in about ninety seconds without any part of me being in the room. That is what Vargas took away from me over three sessions in a room at the top of Fenn Street with the blinds down, and it was the worst week of the whole thing, and I include the trial.

She did it by refusing to let me tell it in order.

She would say: "Stop. Before that. What did the kitchen smell like."

Or: "Don't tell me what you did next. Tell me where your shoes were."

Here is the recited version, the ninety-second one, so you can see the shape of what I had been carrying.

The fifth of March, two years and three months before the pool. Sarah was eleven weeks out from a car accident on the coast road in which a van had come across the line and hit her nearside wing, and she had a fractured pelvis and two broken ribs and a lot of pain and a course of physiotherapy at home three times a week, and she was on a rotating cast of painkillers and, for the previous nine weeks, zopiclone to sleep.

She hated all of it. She hated the not-swimming most.

That is the piece everybody who knew her fastened on to afterward and it is why nobody, including me, questioned a single thing. Sarah Rhodes had swum four times a week her whole adult life. County squad at fifteen. She swam in the sea in November. When she was told she could start swimming again — gently, no diving, no cold — she went into that pool the same afternoon and cried, and I stood at the edge with a towel and cried too.

That was on the twenty-eighth of February. Six days before.

On the night of the fifth of March I went up at half past ten. She said she would come up in a bit. She was in the kitchen with the radio on.

At twenty past six the next morning I woke up alone and went downstairs and the radio was still on and the side door was unlocked, and I went out onto the deck in bare feet in the dark.

She was at the deep end, face-down, in eleven degrees of water.

Post-mortem: drowning. Blood zopiclone in the range you would expect from a therapeutic dose taken some hours before. No alcohol. No injury but the ones from the accident, healing. Verdict at inquest: accidental death. Twenty minutes. The coroner said the word tragic twice and put his pen down.

That is the recitation. That is what I said two hundred times.

Now here is what Vargas got out of me in three sessions, and I have marked the four things I had never once said out loud in two and a half years, because I did not know they were things.

One. I did not put her to bed.

I said she said she'd come up in a bit. That is not what happened. What happened is that Sarah was sitting at the kitchen table with her leg up on the second chair and a mug, and I said goodnight, and she said, without looking up, "In a bit."

She was angry. Not at me — I was very clear about this and Vargas made me say it four different ways to be sure. She was angry at something on her phone, which was face-up on the table, and she had been angry at it since about nine o'clock, and when I came in she had turned it face-down.

I had never told anybody that. Not because I was hiding it. Because for two and a half years the story in my head began at she said she'd come up in a bit, and the phone face-down was in the frame before that, and nobody had ever made me look at the frame before.

Two. The tablets.

Her zopiclone was in a blister strip in the drawer of her bedside table, and she took one at about half past ten, every night, upstairs, with the glass of water she kept there.

Sarah did not take her sleeping tablet in the kitchen. She took it in bed, because she had been told to, because it worked in twenty minutes and she had a fractured pelvis and could not be halfway up a staircase when it landed.

Nobody has ever explained to me how a woman who took her tablet upstairs at half past ten came to have that drug in her blood at the deep end of a swimming pool at some point before midnight, having never come upstairs at all.

The bedside glass was full in the morning. I remember that with total clarity now. In two and a half years I had never once put those two facts side by side.

Three. She would not have gone in alone in the dark in March.

That one I fought Vargas on, hard, for about twenty minutes, because it is the exact thing everybody said at the time — you know Sarah, she'd swim in a puddle — and I had said it myself and believed it.

Then Vargas asked me, quietly, whether Sarah had been able to get out of the pool on her own since the accident.

And I sat there.

She could get in. There are steps at the shallow end. Getting out of a pool means putting your weight on your arms and swinging your hips over a coping stone, and Sarah Rhodes could not do that in March with eleven weeks of healing pelvis, and on the twenty-eighth of February, on the good day, the day she cried, I had gone down the steps and stood in the water and lifted her out myself.

I lifted her out. On the only occasion she had been in that pool since the accident, six days earlier, she physically could not leave the water without another person in it with her.

I have not been able to write that paragraph without stopping since I first said it out loud in that room.

Four. The nine minutes.

This one is not mine. Vargas found it and told me about it in the November and I include it here because it belongs to the fifth of March.

Sarah's phone — face-down on the kitchen table, angry — had one incoming call that night at 22:41, lasting nine minutes and eleven seconds, from a mobile number that was on the standard billing record and that nobody in the original investigation ever looked at, because there was no investigation, because nothing had happened except that a woman with a fractured pelvis had gone swimming in March after taking her tablets.

The number was on a pay-as-you-go handset registered to nobody, bought for cash in a shop in Winterhold in the January.

Its records show that in the eleven weeks it existed it called four numbers.

Sarah's, once, on the fifth of March, at 22:41.

And three others, forty-odd times, all of them belonging to my brother Gil.

Vargas closed the folder at the end of the third session and said, "That's enough for today."

I said, "You think Vanessa was in the house."

She said, "I think somebody was in the house. Two people can drown a swimmer, Mr Rhodes. One person can't, not a county swimmer, not without marks. But then again, one person can sit on the edge — and that's not drowning her, is it, that's just failing to help her out."

I said, "There'd be nothing to find."

"There'd be nothing to find," she agreed. "There never is. That's rather the point of it."

And then she said the sentence that took the whole case out of my house and put it somewhere I could not follow.

"Mr Rhodes — who told you your wife's physiotherapy had ended on the twenty-eighth of February?"

I said, "Vanessa did."

And Detective Vargas said: "The agency has her down as attending your home on the fourth of March. Not the twenty-eighth. And it's not a rehabilitation session on their system, it's logged as an equipment collection at seven in the evening."

The fourth of March. The night before.
