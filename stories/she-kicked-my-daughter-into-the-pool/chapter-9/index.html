She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 09 — The Girl Who Stopped Swimming

Chapter 9 of 25

That autumn my daughter did fourteen things that I wrote down at the time as symptoms and that a woman with a police badge later read back to me as a list of precautions.

I am going to put the whole list here. It is the thing I would want to read if I were somebody else's father, sitting up at one in the morning with a laptop, wondering whether the thing in the back of my own head is worth anything.

1. She would not go into the garden. Not once, from the fifteenth of June to the ninth of October. 2. She would not walk past the kitchen window on the side that shows the pool. She went round through the dining room. Every time. Approximately four hundred times. 3. She would not be in a room alone with Vanessa. If I left, she left, within about twenty seconds, and she always had a reason, and the reasons were good. 4. When she could not avoid it, she stood so that a piece of furniture was between them. The kitchen island. The back of the sofa. A dining chair she would pull out and stand behind and hold on to with both hands like a lectern. 5. She stopped closing her bedroom door. 6. She started keeping her shoes in her bedroom instead of in the hall. 7. She asked me, in July, how old you have to be to be allowed to phone the police. 8. She began to ask, every single time I left the house, three questions: where, how long, and who is going to be here. 9. She would not eat anything she had not seen come out of a packet or off a chopping board in front of her. This started the week Marta left. 10. She wet the bed twice in August, aged seven and a half, having not done so since she was three. 11. She stopped asking to have friends over. 12. She asked to sleep in my room. I said yes for a fortnight and then, on advice, gently stopped it. 13. She learned to swim. 14. She never once, in four months, said she was frightened of the water.

Numbers thirteen and fourteen are the ones I cannot get past.

She learned to swim in secret, at school, in the last three weeks of the summer term, in the twenty-minute lunchtime session that the Year Three teacher ran in the tiny heated pool for the children who had not passed the summer badge.

She had never gone before. She signed herself up on the Monday after she came home from hospital. Her teacher, Mrs Ilsley, assumed I knew, because what kind of parent would not know, and it went in the newsletter and I did not read the newsletter.

I found out in the September when I was clearing her book bag and found a laminated card with a picture of a seahorse on it and the words 25 METRES UNAIDED and a date in July.

I stood in the hall and looked at it for a long time.

Then I went upstairs and I sat on the end of her bed and I said, "Monkey, why didn't you tell me you'd done this?"

And Emma — cross-legged, in pyjamas, eight weeks after a woman had put her in two metres ten of water — said:

"Because you'd tell her."

That is the whole book in four words, and I got it in September and I still did not move.

I want to try, once, to explain that, and then I am going to stop apologising in this account, because I have been told that the apologising is its own kind of self-regard and I think that is fair.

A child who is frightened of water is a child who has had an accident.

A child who quietly teaches herself to swim, and hides it, and hides it specifically from the adult who might tell the other adult — that is not fear of water. That is a person doing a risk assessment.

I could see it. That is what I need to say. I could see it in September and I could see it in the mirror in the car in August and I could see the fruit dish on the wrong side in July. I saw everything. My eyes worked all summer.

There is a difference between seeing and looking. Seeing is passive; it happens to you; you can carry an enormous amount of it around and never do a single thing about it, and I did, for four months, and my daughter took her own precautions in the meantime because she had correctly assessed that the adult in her house was not going to.

Number thirteen on that list is not a symptom. It is a seven-year-old getting herself out of a situation that her father could not face.

The other thing that happened that autumn is that Emma started asking about her mother.

Sarah died on the fifth of March, two years and three months before the pool. Emma was four and a half. She has three memories, and only three, and she has never acquired a new one: a red coat, a song about a magpie, and the sound of her mother laughing at something on the radio in a car.

For two years she had asked about her almost never — which the books say is normal, and which was easier for me, and I took the easiness.

That October she asked about her four or five times a week.

She asked what her voice was like. She asked whether she was tall. She asked whether she was good at swimming, and I said yes, very, county squad as a girl, and Emma went quiet and then said, "But she still drowned."

I said, "She'd taken tablets to help her sleep, love. She shouldn't have gone in the water after them."

And my daughter said: "Why did she go in, then?"

I did not have an answer for that in October and I want to tell you that I have one now, and I do, and it took a courtroom and a nine-year sentence to get it, and it is in chapter fifteen of this.

But in October all I had was the sentence I had been using for two years, the smooth one, the one worn round like a stone in a river: your mum did a silly thing when she was very tired.

Emma listened to it. And then she asked me the last question, and she asked it in the doorway of the utility room, with her hand on the frame, in a voice with nothing whatever loaded into it:

"Was Vanessa here that night?"

I said no.

That is not a lie. I have gone over this with a lawyer and with a police officer and with myself at four in the morning more times than any of them, and the answer I gave my daughter in the doorway of the utility room on the seventh of October was not a lie.

It was the truth as I held it. Vanessa Kerr, as she was then, had finished as Sarah's rehabilitation physiotherapist on the twenty-eighth of February, six days before. She was not on the property on the fifth of March. She had no key. I did not see her from the end of February until the twelfth of April, when I met her by chance in a supermarket car park and she said she was so terribly sorry, and put a hand on my arm, and cried.

Every single word of that paragraph is true and I would swear to all of it.

It is also — every word of it — a thing I know because she told me.

The car park was not chance. The twenty-eighth of February was not the end of anything. And on the night of the fifth of March there is a nine-minute gap in the phone records that a woman in an insurance office would eventually make somebody care about.

But I did not know any of that on the seventh of October, standing in a doorway lying to my daughter with a clear conscience.

Two days later she asked me for the camera.
