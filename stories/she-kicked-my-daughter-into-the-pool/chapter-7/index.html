She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 07 — Why a Seven-Year-Old Is Not a Witness

Chapter 7 of 25

In the last week of July I did go and ask.

I want that on the record, because when this came out people said a lot of things about me on the internet, and the thing they said most was that I had done nothing. I did do something. I did the thing a reasonable, frightened, guilty man does, which is that I went and asked three professionals whether my child could be right, and all three of them, correctly and in good faith, told me that she probably was not.

I am going to set out what each of them actually said, because between them they built the wall I spent the next ten weeks behind, and because not one of them said anything untrue.

One. The child psychologist.

Priya Naidu again, at the follow-up in the third week of July, in a room with a box of tissues placed exactly where you would expect it.

I told her Emma was saying she had been kicked.

Naidu did not blink. She said this is very common. She said that when a child has a period of hypoxia — no oxygen — the memories immediately around it do not get written down properly, and the brain, which cannot tolerate a hole, fills the hole. She said the filling material is always whatever is nearest to hand: the last thing you were frightened of, the person you were arguing with that morning, a bad dream, a television programme.

She said, and this is the sentence I carried around like a stone for three months, "The confabulation is not a lie. She believes it completely. That's what makes it so convincing."

I said, "So how do you tell the difference?"

And Priya Naidu — who I want to say again is a serious person, who later gave evidence and gave it honestly, and who cried in the corridor afterward — said:

"Honestly? At this age, from the inside, you often can't. You look at the physical evidence."

There was no physical evidence. Dr Ilesanmi had looked. There were four fingermarks on a forearm and they were mine.

Two. The police.

I rang the non-emergency number on the twenty-ninth of July and a very decent constable came out to the house eight days later and sat in my kitchen for fifty minutes and drank a cup of tea.

He was about thirty. He wrote everything down. He asked good questions and one very good one, which was whether Emma had ever said anything like this about Vanessa before the accident, and the answer was no, and he wrote no and underlined it, and I watched him underline it and felt the floor go.

Then he explained, kindly, what he could and could not do.

He said: no witness. He said: no injury. He said: no scene — nobody attended, the pool has been drained and refilled, the furniture has been moved, and it is now six weeks. He said the only account of the event comes from the one adult who was present, and that account has been consistent since minute one, and is supported by the position of the fruit dish.

I said, "The fruit dish was on her right. The pool was on her left."

He stopped writing.

I have thought about that moment for a long time. He stopped writing, and he looked at me, and something went across his face which I read at the time as pity and which I now think was closer to ah, hell.

And then he said, "Mr Rhodes, can I ask you something? Do you actually think your wife tried to kill your daughter?"

And I said, "No."

I said no. He asked me directly, in my own kitchen, with his pen up, and I said no, because it was true, because I did not think it, because thinking it was not available to me.

He closed the notebook.

He said he would put it on the system as a "concern noted, no further action," and that if anything else happened — anything at all, he said, and he said it twice — I was to ring, and to ask for it to be linked to that entry.

He gave me his card. I put it in the drawer in the hall with the batteries and the takeaway menus, and in the November Detective Vargas asked me if I still had it and I went and got it and it was there, and she looked at it for a while and said, "He did his job, this lad. He left you a door."

Three. The solicitor.

Eleven days later, four hundred pounds, an hour and ten minutes, a man called Desmond Fry in an office above a dental practice.

He was the only one of the three who was not kind, and he was the only one who was any use.

He listened to the whole thing without interrupting once, and then he said: "Right. You've got a child's account, uncorroborated, made after an oxygen event, against a woman with no history, no motive that you've told me about, and a physical presentation that's consistent with the accident. If you go to war on that you will lose, and — listen to me — when you lose, your wife will be the parent who was falsely accused, and you will be the man who accused her. Think about what that does to your daughter's living arrangements if this marriage ends."

I said, "So I do nothing."

And Desmond Fry said the most useful sentence anybody said to me that year.

He said: "You do nothing loudly. You do everything quietly. Motive is what you haven't got. Go and find out about money."

I did not do it.

I want to be honest about why, because "I was in shock" is not the whole answer and the whole answer is worse.

I did not go and look at the money because the money was, at that point, entirely mine.

The house was mine, bought with Sarah, eleven years earlier. The firm — Rhodes & Ivers, thirty-one people, my grandfather's name on the first half of it — was mine and my brother Gil's, sixty-forty. Vanessa had come into that marriage with a car and a storage unit and about eleven thousand pounds.

If I went and looked at money, I would find that everything pointed at her, because everything I owned would point at anyone who married me.

That is what I told myself. That is not why.

Here is why.

To go and look at the money would have meant sitting down at a table and putting a pen on paper and writing at the top of it: what does she gain. And I could not put that sentence on a page in the same house as a woman who had brought me the right shirt, and I could not put it on a page without also, immediately, in the very next line, having to write down the name of the person who had died in that pool two years earlier.

Because if you go looking for who benefits from Emma Rhodes drowning at the deep end of that pool, you have already accepted that somebody once benefited from Sarah Rhodes drowning at the deep end of that pool.

And in July I was not able to survive that sentence.

So I put Fry's four hundred pounds in the bin with the rest of that summer, and I went home, and I cut up oranges for my daughter with the pith off, and I did not look at money for eleven more weeks.

The person who eventually looked at the money was not me. It was a clerk in an insurance office in Winterhold who was clearing an old queue, and she did it in November for an entirely unrelated reason, and she has no idea to this day that she did anything at all.
