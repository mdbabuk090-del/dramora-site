She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 23 — Thirty-Two Years

Chapter 23 of 25

The jury were out for eleven hours and forty minutes across two days.

I have read since that this is a good length — that under four hours frightens prosecutors and over three days frightens everybody — and at the time it was simply the longest thing that has ever happened to me, including the nine days in June when we waited to find out what my daughter's brain was going to be like.

I did not stay in the building. Vargas would not let me. She sent me to a café on the corner with a family liaison officer and she said, "If you sit in that corridor you will look at that door for eleven hours and you will never get it out of you," and she was right about that as she was right about most things.

They came back at twenty past two on the second afternoon.

Guilty of the murder of Sarah Rhodes. Guilty of the attempted murder of Emma Rhodes.

I did not hear the second one. I have been told that I stood up when the first one was read and that a court usher put a hand on my arm, and I have no memory of any of it. What I remember is the foreman's shoes, which were brown, and which I looked at for the entire ninety seconds.

She was sentenced eleven days later: life, with a minimum term of thirty-two years.

She was thirty-six. She will be sixty-eight.

The judge spoke for twenty-six minutes and there are four sentences in it that I have kept.

He said, of Sarah: "She telephoned her friend late at night because she had begun to understand something, and she did not want to trouble her husband with it. She intended to ask you a question the following evening. You made certain she never asked anybody anything again."

He said, of the eleven weeks of physiotherapy: "You were admitted to that house because a family needed help. You are not the first person to have understood that the front door of a home in difficulty stands open, but you are one of very few to have walked through it twice."

He said, of Emma: "She told her father eleven times. She was seven years old, she was consistent, she was accurate, and she was not believed — and I make no criticism whatever of Mr Rhodes for that, because you had arranged the whole of that summer so that he could not. That is perhaps the most chilling feature of this case. You did not silence the child. You made her unbelievable."

And then the fourth one, which he said near the end, and which is the reason I have been able to put the rest of this down on paper at all:

"Nothing in the evidence before me suggests that this was done in temper, or in despair, or in any of the ordinary derangements that bring people to this court. It was done carefully, over years, for money, by a person of good education and no cruelty of manner, who was liked by everyone who met her. There will be a great deal written about your character. I have seen no evidence that you have one."

She did not react to any of it.

She sat in the dock in a navy cardigan with her hands in her lap and she looked at the judge with an expression of polite attention, the way you look at somebody explaining a parking permit.

When it was over she stood up before the officer touched her, and she straightened the cardigan, and she went down the stairs.

That is the last time I ever saw her.

What I want to write about is not the verdict. It is the four days afterward.

Everybody had told me — Vargas, the liaison officer, the woman I see on Tuesdays, three separate people who had been through it and wrote to me — that the end of a trial is not a relief and that you should be ready for it.

I did not believe them. I thought that was something people said.

Here is what actually happens. For nine months your entire body is a machine for getting to a date. Everything is before the trial or after the trial. You sleep badly on purpose. You do not make plans. You become, without noticing, a person whose whole personality is a case number.

And then at twenty past two on a Wednesday afternoon twelve strangers say a word, and the machine has nothing to do, and you go home, and your daughter is in the kitchen doing a jigsaw, and there is no next thing.

I sat in the car on my own drive for forty minutes.

Then I went in and Emma said, "Well?" — she had been told a verdict was coming, we had agreed the words in advance with Doherty — and I said the sentence we had agreed, which was: "The judge said you were telling the truth."

And Emma said, "Okay." And went back to the jigsaw.

Four minutes later, without looking up, she said: "Is she coming out ever?"

I said, "Not until you're grown up. Not until you're older than I am now."

And she said, "Okay," again, and put in a piece, and then she got up and came round the table and stood against me with her arms round my middle and her face in my jumper for about a minute, and then she went back and sat down and carried on.

That is the whole of my daughter's reaction to the end of it. About sixty seconds against my jumper.

The woman I see on Tuesdays says that is because she had already had her reaction, at ten past six in the morning on the tenth of December, on a windowsill, and that everything after that was administration.

There are two things I have to put down about the aftermath and neither of them is uplifting.

The first is that I was not, in the end, a very good father for about a year afterward. The letters people write to me assume that the verdict is where a family starts getting better. What actually happened is that the year after the trial was much worse than the year of it, for both of us, and there was a period in the autumn when Emma was nine when she was doing extremely badly at school and I was not sleeping and we were, I think, genuinely in some trouble.

We got out of it because of an eleven-week course at a place in Marbury that costs a lot of money and had a waiting list, and I have thought a great deal about the families who have exactly what we had except the money, and I do not have anything useful to say about it except that it is obscene.

The second is that people were extraordinarily unkind about me on the internet, and I read all of it, for about five months, every night, and the thing they said was that any real father would have known.

I want to say to anybody reading this who is on the receiving end of that:

They are saying it because the alternative is unbearable. If a man can sit at his own kitchen table for four months with all the pieces in his own head and not see it, then it could happen in their house. So they need me to be stupid, or negligent, or somehow complicit, because that puts a wall between my family and theirs.

There is no wall. That is the only thing I know now that I did not know before.

I was not stupid. I was tired, and I was guilty about ninety seconds, and I loved somebody, and every single piece of information that arrived came dressed as an ordinary domestic nothing — a woman sending a cook home early, a child being brave about the water, a fruit dish on the wrong side of a chair.

The pieces do not arrive labelled.

They arrive as your life.
