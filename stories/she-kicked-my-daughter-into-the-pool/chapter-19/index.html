She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 19 — Six in the Morning

Chapter 19 of 25

Vargas rang me at eleven minutes past midnight and said one sentence: "Is your daughter in the house with you now?"

I said yes.

She said, "Is her door open?"

I said yes.

She said, "Go and sit outside it. Take your phone. Don't lock anything, don't pack anything, don't wake anybody, don't do anything at all. We're coming at six. If anything happens before six you ring me, not 999, me, and I'll be nine minutes."

I said, "What am I looking for?"

And Paloma Vargas said: "Nothing. You're not looking for anything. You're just going to be a man sitting in a corridor. Can you do that for six hours?"

I sat on the landing floor with my back against Emma's doorframe from twenty past midnight until ten to six.

The bedroom door at the other end was shut. There was no light under it after about one.

I have been asked what I thought about, and the truthful answer is that I did not think about Sarah, or the pool, or the photographs, or my brother on his floor with his back against a sofa. For five and a half hours I thought about almost nothing at all, in the way you do at the very end of something, and the one thing that kept arriving was completely useless: the sound of a stapler.

Vanessa had a stapler she used for the school forms. She kept it in the drawer with the batteries and the takeaway menus and a card from a constable, and she was the only person in the house who ever used it, and every September there were forty forms and she did all of them at that kitchen table on a Sunday night, and the sound of it went through the house.

I sat on the floor outside my daughter's bedroom and could not stop hearing a stapler, and at some point around four in the morning I understood that what I was doing was grieving, in advance, for a woman who did not exist, and that I was going to have to do it privately and probably for years, because there is no version of this in which a man is allowed to be sad about that.

At ten to six I went down and unlocked the side door and put the kitchen light on and stood there.

Four vehicles came up the hill without any blue lights at all. That is standard, apparently, and it is also, Vargas said afterward, the thing she does deliberately at addresses where there are children: you come at first light, quietly, and you park down the drive, and nobody in the house wakes to a strobe on their ceiling.

She came in first with a woman officer behind her.

She said, "Where is she?"

I said, "Upstairs. Second on the left."

And Vargas said, "Right. Go and stand in your daughter's room, please, and shut the door, and stay there whatever you hear."

I did not see the arrest.

I stood in a room with a horse-print duvet and a grey rabbit with a chewed ear and my seven-year-old asleep with her mouth open, and I heard four sets of feet on a staircase I have walked up eleven thousand times, and then a door, and then a woman's voice saying something short.

There was no screaming. There was nothing thrown. There was a long stretch of low talking and then the sound of somebody being told to get dressed, and then, quite clearly, in the voice she used for tradesmen and school receptionists, my wife said:

"Can I take my glasses?"

Emma woke up when they came back down the stairs.

She lay there for a second with her hair over her face and listened to the feet going down, and then she sat up.

She said, "Is that her?"

I said yes.

She said, "Are they taking her out?"

I said yes.

And my daughter got out of bed, and went past me to the window, and knelt up on the little padded seat under it with her hands on the sill, and watched a woman being put into the back of a car in the dark.

I stood behind her with both hands on her shoulders. I did not tell her not to look. I am not sure whether that was right or wrong and I have stopped asking.

The car doors shut. The car went down the drive.

And Emma turned round and put her face into my dressing gown and cried in a way she had not cried at any point in the six months since June — not once, not at the hospital, not in the night, not after the nightmares — and she cried for about eleven minutes, and then she said, into my chest, the thing that I will hear on my deathbed:

"So I was right."

I said yes.

I said: "You were right. You were right the whole time, and you told me, and I didn't believe you, and that's mine, monkey, that's not yours."

She thought about it.

Then she said, "Are you cross with me?"

And that is the moment — not the frame, not the voicemail, not the man in the mill flat — that is the moment I would take out of my life if I were allowed to take one out. A seven-year-old who had been quietly, correctly, absolutely right for six months, sitting on a windowsill at ten past six in the morning, checking whether being right had made her father angry.

The house was searched for two days.

They took the computer, both our phones, the pool cover, the ceramic dish out of the utility room cupboard where it had been since Marta washed it in the June, and the camera, and the card, and the folding tripod, which nobody had moved off the low garden wall since October and which had a woodlouse living in it.

They took eleven items out of a storage unit in Winterhold, including a box file with the Ardwell trust deed photocopied in it, and a printout of the residuary clause with a single line drawn beside it in pencil.

Nessa Doyle, known as Vanessa Rhodes, was charged four days later with the attempted murder of Emma Rhodes and, eleven weeks after that, following the disclosure from Cumbria and the reopening of a file about a bath in Kendal, with the murder of Sarah Rhodes.

She pleaded not guilty to both and she never, at any stage, from that morning to this day, said one single word about any of it to anybody.

That is the thing people find hardest to believe, and it is the thing I find easiest. She did not confess, she did not explain, she did not break down in an interview room at three in the morning, she did not write to me.

In nine months of proceedings the only thing she ever said in my hearing was in a corridor at the Crown Court in the February, when she went past me between two officers, and she stopped for about a second and a half and looked at me.

And she said: "You wouldn't have looked, you know. If she hadn't left the camera out."

That is all. Nine words. Then they took her through the door.

She is right, of course. That is what makes it the only true thing she ever said to me.
