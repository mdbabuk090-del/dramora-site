She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 16 — The Policy

Chapter 16 of 25

The document Sarah signed in the January without reading it was a whole-of-life policy on her own life for four hundred thousand pounds, written into trust, with the benefit assigned to a limited company called Calder Bay Rehabilitation Services Ltd.

I had never heard of it.

It was incorporated in the October before Sarah died — five months before — with one director, Gil Rhodes, and one shareholder holding ninety-eight per cent of it, and that shareholder is listed under her legal name at the time, and the correspondence address is a storage unit on the industrial estate at Winterhold.

It has never traded. It has never filed anything but dormant accounts. It exists to be a name on a form.

And then here is the part that finishes it, and that a jury took forty minutes over.

The policy was never claimed.

I want to explain why that matters, because when Vargas first told me I said the stupid thing, which is: well then it can't have been about money, can it, she never took the money.

Vargas let me finish and then said, "Mr Rhodes, think about what happens if she claims it."

An insurer receiving a claim on a five-month-old policy, on a woman who died in an accident at home, does not pay it out with a sympathy card. They investigate. A five-month-old policy on a young life, taken out by a company nobody has heard of, claimed after an accidental death — that is the single loudest thing you can do in the entire industry.

She did not claim it. She let four hundred thousand pounds sit there and did nothing at all.

Because in the March of that year, Vanessa Kerr had something far better than a claim.

She had a widower with a house on the hill above Calder Bay, a construction firm, a four-year-old, and the entire remainder of his life in front of him, and she was thirty-two, and she was very, very patient.

Vargas put it like this, in the kitchen, at the point when we were finally allowed to talk in my own house: "The policy wasn't the plan. The policy was the insurance. It's what she'd have fallen back on if you'd married somebody else, or moved to Ireland, or turned out to be broke. She got the bigger thing, so she left it in the drawer."

The policy sat, unclaimed, in a file at Rowntree Provident for two years and eight months, until a claims clerk called Ruth Adeyemi, working through a queue of dormant single-premium policies flagged for a routine solvency exercise, opened it, looked at the date of the life assured's death — which was on the file, because the insurer had been notified by the trust administrator as a matter of routine and had simply never been asked to pay — and did the thing that turned this entire case.

She did not report a suspicion. She was not suspicious. She had a form to complete that asked why an eligible benefit had never been claimed, and she rang the number on the file for the beneficiary company, and got a dead line, and then, being conscientious, rang the phone number written on the original application form for the life assured's next of kin.

Which was mine.

She rang me at ten past eleven in the morning on the twenty-first of November to ask, entirely politely, whether the family were aware that there was an unclaimed benefit in respect of the late Mrs Sarah Rhodes.

I stood in my kitchen and said, "I'm sorry — the late what?"

I put the phone down and I sat on the floor with my back against the dishwasher, and that is when I remembered the trust.

Not the insurance. The other one. The one from Sarah's mother.

Ardwell, and the money from the sale of Ardwell, four hundred and eleven thousand pounds, held in trust for Emma Rhodes until her twenty-fifth birthday, with the residue falling back into her mother's estate if she does not reach it.

And her mother's estate had come to me. All of it, in the June after the funeral, and I had signed the papers in an office above a bank and had felt nothing about it except a dull hatred of the furniture.

I got out the folder. I sat on the kitchen floor and read the whole thing, and then I read the schedule at the back where the trustees are named.

Trustees: Michael James Rhodes. Gilbert Andrew Rhodes.

My brother is a trustee of my daughter's money.

He had been since the summer Sarah's mother died, because Sarah's solicitor had said you need two and I had said, well, Gil.

That afternoon Vargas came to the house for the first time.

She sat at my kitchen table with the two folders and a notebook, and she laid it out the way she had laid out the four facts in August, in a vertical list, in biro, with a line under it.

She said: "Right. Here's what I'm now able to say to the Crown Prosecution Service, which I could not say to them a month ago."

> One. The woman had exclusive one-to-one access to Sarah Rhodes for eleven weeks and controlled her medication. > > Two. She obtained Sarah Rhodes's signature on a four-hundred-thousand-pound life policy in favour of a company she owns and Gil Rhodes directs. > > Three. Sarah Rhodes discovered it, said so in a voicemail, and intended to confront her about it on the fourth of March. > > Four. Sarah Rhodes was found dead in the pool on the morning of the sixth of March, having been unable, six days earlier, to leave that pool without physical assistance. > > Five. Two years and three months later, the same woman was the only adult present when the child of that marriage went into the same end of the same pool. > > Six. And on the day the child would have inherited four hundred and eleven thousand pounds, or failed to, the beneficiaries of her failing to are her father — and, as trustee, Gil Rhodes.

Then she put the pen down.

I said, "It's not enough, is it."

And Paloma Vargas said: "It's enough for a warrant. It is not, on its own, enough for a jury. Twelve people are going to look at all of that and one of them, at least one, is going to say *but it's all a bit convenient, isn't it, and where's the actual evidence she did anything.*"

She turned the notebook round and tapped the page with the pen.

"What we need," she said, "is one of these six things to be a person who talks."

I said, "Gil."

She said, "Gil. And Mr Rhodes — I'd like you to understand what I'm asking you for, because I'm not going to dress it up. If your brother talks, he does it to save nine years of his own life, and the price of it is that you are going to sit in a witness box and listen to a man you've known since the day he was born explain how he introduced her to your wife."

I said, "He didn't know. Gil wouldn't have known about Emma."

Vargas looked at me for a long moment.

Then she said, very gently, "Mr Rhodes, on the fourteenth of June — the day of the pool — your brother telephoned your house landline at twenty-six minutes past three in the afternoon. It's on the bill. Sixteen seconds."

Twenty-six minutes past three.

Sixteen minutes after Marta was sent home.
