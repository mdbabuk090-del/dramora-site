She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 20 — Gil

Chapter 20 of 25

My brother gave four interviews across nine days in the December and a fifth in the January, all of them voluntary, all of them with a solicitor beside him, none of them under any deal, because there was no deal — the Crown do not do deals in this country, they simply take into account what a man did and when he did it.

What Gil did was ring a police station at ten past nine at night without being asked to, four hours after his brother sat on his sofa, and then answer every question that was put to him for eleven hours in total, including the ones that put him in prison.

He was charged with fraud by false representation over the witnessing of the policy, and with a second offence relating to what he did with the money, and the Crown examined for four months whether there was a case against him on the murder, and concluded that there was not.

I was told that on a Tuesday in the April, in a room at the CPS office, by a lawyer who was very careful with me.

She said: "Mr Rhodes, I want to be clear about what we're saying, because it matters. We are not saying your brother is innocent of your wife's death. We are saying we cannot prove he knew, and there is a real difference, and you are going to have to live in that difference."

I have lived in it for two years now and I am going to try to set out what is in it, because half the letters I get are from people who want me to say that Gil is as guilty as she is, and the other half are from people who want me to say that he was a victim, and I cannot do either.

Here is what my brother did.

He introduced a woman he had known for two months into a house containing a disabled woman, a four-year-old, and eleven weeks of one-to-one access, and he did it because he was a hundred and nine thousand pounds down and she had been kind to him about it.

He signed a document he did not read for eleven thousand pounds.

He took the money.

He sat in a church at his sister-in-law's funeral eleven weeks later, and he thought he was going to be sick, and he said nothing.

He was made a trustee of a four-hundred-and-eleven-thousand-pound trust for his niece, and he had never read the deed, and when a woman rang him and read out the residuary clause and asked whether he understood what it meant for the two of them, he told her not to talk like that, and he said nothing.

On a Sunday afternoon in June he was frightened enough to telephone my house to hear who picked up the phone, and when she picked up the phone and said everything was fine, he put the phone down and went to lunch, and he said nothing.

He sat with me in a hospital corridor until two in the morning and put his coat over my legs, and he said nothing.

For four months of that summer, while I was going quietly mad in a house with a fruit dish on the wrong side of a chair, my brother knew that there was a company with his name on it, and a policy, and a phone call about a residuary clause — and he came for lunch, and he took Emma to the retail park for shoes once when I could not face it, and he said nothing.

That is what is in the difference. He did not want it to happen and he arranged every condition for it and then he did not look, in exactly the same way I did not look, and the only distinction between my brother and me — the only one, and I have gone at this from every angle there is — is that I did not take eleven thousand pounds.

I am not sure that is as big a distinction as I would like it to be.

He got five years in the July and served two and a half.

I did not go to his sentencing. I have not seen him since the ninth of December except in the witness box, where I looked at the back wall.

He wrote to me nine times in the first year. I read all nine and answered none. In the tenth letter, which came in the December after, he stopped apologising, which I noticed, and he asked me only one thing: whether Emma had been told what her uncle did, and whether I would let him know before she was told, so that he could write to her rather than have her hear it from a document.

I wrote back four lines. I said: she knows. I told her in the September. She asked me two questions and the second one was whether you knew about the pool, and I told her the truth, which is that nobody knows.

He wrote once more after that. It says one paragraph and I am not going to put it in a book.

Emma asked to see him in the spring of the year after, when she was nine.

I said no.

She asked again in the summer and I said no again, and then in the autumn she asked a third time, and she had clearly thought about it, because she said: "You don't have to come in. You can wait outside. It's not for you."

I sat down on the bottom stair.

I said, "Monkey, why do you want to?"

And Emma said, "Because he was there before. He's the only one who's still — " and she stopped, and started again, and got it right the second time, the way she does. She said:

"Everyone who knew Mum is gone or in prison. Marta's got a different job. You don't talk about her because it makes your face go. He used to do the thing with the bike."

She is eleven now. I have said no four times.

I do not know whether I am protecting her or whether I am using her to punish him, and the woman I see on Tuesdays says that the fact I am asking is probably the important part, and I think that is possibly the kindest lie anybody has ever told me.

There is one thing Gil said in his fourth interview that I want to end this chapter with, because it is the only piece of the whole case that is genuinely about how this happens to ordinary families, rather than about how it happened to mine.

The interviewing officer asked him why, in the three days between the phone call about the trust deed and the fourteenth of June, he did not ring me.

And my brother said:

"Because I'd have had to start at the beginning. You can't ring your brother and say watch her on Sunday. He'd have said why, and then I'd have had to say the company, and then the eleven grand, and then Sarah. It's not one sentence. It's the whole thing or it's nothing."

And then he said the part I have thought about every day since:

"So I waited to see if it'd be all right. Because if it was all right, I'd never have to say any of it."
