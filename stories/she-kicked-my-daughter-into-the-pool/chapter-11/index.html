She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 11 — One Frame Every Five Seconds

Chapter 11 of 25

I went forward through them one at a time with the right-hand arrow key, and it took me two hours and eleven minutes to get to frame 214, and I have never told anybody why I did it that way.

The card was in the reader by then. The photographs were on a screen the size of a window. I could have dragged the slider. I could have jumped to the end of the sequence in one second — I knew the accident was at about twenty past six, I knew the camera started at four minutes past, I could have done the arithmetic in my head in about eleven seconds and gone straight there.

I went one at a time.

Frame 1 is the deck at 18:04, empty, over-bright, the shadow of the cypresses lying across the water in five stripes. Frame 2 is the same. Frame 3 is the same.

Frame 19, at 18:05:30, has a small blue shape entering at the bottom right, which is the top of my daughter's head as she goes back to her chair.

Frame 40 has me in it, on the left lounger, in a white shirt, with my legs out and my ankles crossed.

Frame 63 is Vanessa arriving with the dish.

I looked at every single one of them. Two hours and eleven minutes, in a room with the door shut and a house asleep above me, going forward through a sunset in five-second steps toward a thing I already knew was going to be there.

I think I was saying goodbye to the version of my life in which it was not.

The technical facts, because they matter enormously later.

The camera was set to its highest quality still setting. Each frame is a twenty-four-megapixel photograph. That is roughly eleven times the detail of a frame of ordinary security video, and it is why the man they eventually brought in from the imaging lab was able to do what he did with the shadows in chapter twenty-two.

The frames are timestamped to the second in the file data, and the camera's clock was accurate to within about nine seconds, which was later checked against the phone network's timing on Vanessa's 999 call.

There is no sound.

And there is a five-second gap between every photograph.

Five seconds is nothing. Five seconds is also, in the account of a violent act, an eternity, and every single thing that happened in the following eight months happened in those gaps.

Frame 190. 18:19:45.

Three people. Me on the left lounger sitting up with one foot on the ground. Emma kneeling up on the right lounger, facing away from the camera, toward the house. Vanessa lying back on the middle-left one with a book on her chest and her sunglasses on. The dish of oranges on the cooler lid, the cooler exactly square between the two loungers on the right.

Frame 193. 18:20:00.

I am standing. My mouth is open. That is me saying I'll get the cake, and a jury saw it, and I had to sit and look at the floor.

Frame 196.

Me, halfway to the side door, from behind. Emma up on her knees, watching me go.

Frame 201. 18:21:40 minus a bit.

Emma still kneeling, facing the door. Vanessa's book is face-down on the foot of her lounger. Her sunglasses are folded on the arm. She has taken them off.

I sat with that one for a very long time.

Frames 202 to 213. Fifty-five seconds. Twelve photographs.

In every one of them my daughter is kneeling up on a sun lounger with her back to the deep end, watching a door, and a woman four feet away from her is lying back with her hands in her lap, looking at her.

Not reading. The book is face-down at her feet in every one of them.

Twelve consecutive photographs of a woman looking at a child.

That is the part of the sequence that I could not sleep after, and it is the part that did not go in front of the jury, because it is a woman lying on a sun lounger with her hands in her lap, which is not a crime, which is a woman resting in her own garden on a warm evening in June.

Frame 214. 18:21:45.

I am going to describe it exactly as it was described in court, because I cannot do it in my own words and have never been able to.

The frame shows the pool deck from the low garden wall, looking west. Emma Rhodes is on the right-hand lounger, kneeling, facing away, her body beginning to tilt forward and left. The lounger she is on has both left-hand feet off the ground by approximately six centimetres, and the frame of it is angled at about eleven degrees from horizontal, rotating forward, toward the water.

Vanessa Rhodes is on the adjacent lounger, lying back. Her upper body has not moved. Her hands are in her lap.

Her right leg is extended, straight, diagonally forward and to her left, and the sole of her right foot is in contact with the lower rear-right leg of the child's lounger.

That is the photograph.

One frame. One twenty-fifth of a second, out of a hundred and eleven minutes.

Frame 215. 18:21:50.

The lounger is on its side on the sandstone. The surface of the pool at the deep end is white. There is no child anywhere in the photograph. Vanessa Rhodes is sitting up, both feet now on the ground, and her head is turned to the left, toward the water.

Frame 216. She is standing at the edge.

Frame 217. She is standing at the edge.

Frame 218. She is standing at the edge.

Frame 219. 18:22:10. She has turned away from the water, has taken two steps back to the cooler, and is bent at the waist with both hands on the ceramic dish, lifting it off the lid.

Frame 220. She is at the pool edge with the dish held out at chest height, tipped forward, and there are orange halves in the air.

I got up and I was sick in the study bin.

Then I came back and sat down, because there is a thing that happens after the first shock which nobody warns you about, and which I think is the reason I did not go upstairs that night and do something that would have ruined all of it.

I got cold.

I sat in that chair from twenty past eleven until about four in the morning, and I did not cry and I did not shout, and what I did instead was go back to frame 190 and start again, and this time I wrote down times.

I made a document. It has been in front of two courts. It has forty-one lines in it and the last line is timed 03:58.

And the reason I want to end this chapter on that, rather than on being sick in a bin, is this.

At about two in the morning I got to frame 251, which is Michael Rhodes on his knees at the edge of a swimming pool with his daughter in his arms, and a woman crouched in behind him with one hand flat on his shoulder.

I looked at it for a while and I moved on. It is a small figure in the middle distance, in low orange light, and her face is a smudge of about eleven pixels across, and there is nothing in it. You cannot see a thing.

I want it on record that I went past it that night without stopping.

It was another four months before anybody enlarged it.
