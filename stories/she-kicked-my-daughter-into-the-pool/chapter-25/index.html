She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 25 — The Water That Isn't There Anymore

Chapter 25 of 25

Emma is eleven now and she swims on Thursday evenings at the municipal pool in Marbury, in the lane nearest the wall, in a squad of nine girls run by a woman with a whistle and a bad knee.

She asked to do it herself, in the January she was ten, at a kitchen table, having clearly rehearsed it, in the same way she asked to see her uncle and in the same way she asked for a vegetable patch. She has done all the important things in this family for four years and she has done every one of them from a sitting position at that table with a rehearsed sentence.

The sentence was: "There's a thing on Thursdays and I want to go, and I want you to say yes without doing your face."

I said yes without doing my face. I did the face afterward, in the car, on my own.

I go every week. I sit on the third row of the spectator seats with a paper cup of tea that I do not drink, above the shallow end, and I watch my daughter swim for fifty minutes.

For the first eleven weeks I counted.

I want to put this down honestly because I think there will be somebody reading it who does exactly this and thinks they are the only one.

I counted her strokes between breaths. Three, three, three, three. If it went to five I would be on my feet. There was a Thursday in the March when she stopped in the middle of the lane to fix her goggles and was stationary in the water with her head down for about four seconds, and I got as far as the barrier at the end of the row before I understood what I was doing, and a man sitting two seats along — whose own daughter was in the same lane, and who I have since been to the pub with twice — said, without looking at me, "Goggles."

I sat back down. My legs were going for about ten minutes.

I stopped counting on the fourth of June, this year.

There was no reason. It was an ordinary Thursday. She swam the same as she swims every week, which is competently and not brilliantly and with a terrible turn, and the woman with the whistle shouted at all nine of them about something, and at some point I realised that I had been looking at my phone.

I looked up and she was at the far end with her elbows on the tiles talking to another girl, and it had been perhaps ninety seconds since I had looked at her.

Ninety seconds.

I sat with that for a while, in the third row, above the shallow end, with a cup of tea going cold.

Then Emma pushed off the wall and swam back up the lane the way she does, with her head slightly too high, and touched the end, and looked up into the seats to check I was there — she still does that, every week, twice a session — and I put my hand up, and she went again.

There is one more thing and then I am finished.

In the September she asked me for the photographs.

Not those photographs. I want to be very clear because I felt the floor go for a second when she said it, the way I still do.

She said: "Mum's sunsets. The ones on the computer. Can I have them on a stick?"

There are four hundred and eleven of them in a folder sorted by year, going back thirteen years, taken by a woman who believed the third week of June and the first week of December were the only two weeks when that view did the thing she liked.

And there are, in a separate folder that will never be opened again by anybody, one thousand one hundred and six more, from the fourteenth of June, of which the first hundred and ninety are a perfectly ordinary evening and the last eight hundred and fifty are an empty deck going dark.

I gave her the four hundred and eleven.

She made a film out of them for a school thing. It runs for a minute and forty seconds and it is thirteen years of the same view, one summer after another, the same cypresses, the same water going gold and then grey, and in about the last twenty seconds of it the pool is not there any more and there are four apple trees.

She put music on it. She did the whole thing herself on a laptop in a bedroom and did not tell me until it was finished, which is entirely characteristic.

The title card says: The garden, by Emma Rhodes, and her mum.

She showed it to me in the kitchen with the laptop turned round on the table and her hands under her legs, watching my face the whole way through, which is a thing she does and which I do not think she knows she does.

At the end she said, "Is it all right?"

And I said yes. I said it was more than all right.

And she said, "You can watch it again if you want. I'll go upstairs."

That is where I want to leave it, because that is the actual shape of what happened to us and I am not going to tidy it into something better.

A woman came into a house that was already open, through a door held by somebody who loved us, and she took eleven weeks to learn everything and two years and three months to do the rest, and she was liked by every single person she met, and she was very nearly right about all of it.

She was wrong about one thing only.

She thought that the only witness in that garden was a seven-year-old who could be made unbelievable, and she was correct — my daughter told me eleven times, and I did not believe her, and I am the person who has to carry that.

She did not know that a child had carried her dead mother's camera out onto the deck at four minutes past six and set it to five seconds, because five was the number she could remember, and pointed it west at a sunset that had not started yet.

It sat on a garden wall through four months of weather, in plain sight, with nobody looking at it, holding the whole thing.

Emma keeps it in her room now. It does not work — the sensor went in the second winter — and she knows it does not work, and she keeps it on the shelf next to the rabbit with the chewed ear.

I asked her once whether she wanted a new one, a proper one, one that works.

She said no.

She said, "That one did enough."
