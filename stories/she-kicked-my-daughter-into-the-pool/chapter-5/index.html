She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 05 — What the Doctor Wrote

Chapter 5 of 25

The discharge summary for Emma Rhodes, seven, ran to two pages and I have read it more times than I have read anything else ever written.

Unwitnessed immersion in domestic pool, estimated submersion under 90 seconds, rescued by father, bystander rescue breaths commenced immediately... intubated on arrival... extubated 20 hours... no seizure activity... no aspiration pneumonia on repeat imaging... no evidence of non-accidental injury.

That last clause is nine words long and it closed the whole thing.

I want to be fair to the man who wrote it, because when I finally met Dr Ilesanmi again — in a corridor, in the November, by accident — I could see that it had cost him something to have been right in the way he was right.

He examined her properly. That is the thing people do not understand. He went over my daughter's body the way you would go over a car you were thinking of buying: scalp, behind the ears, inside the mouth, both arms, the trunk, the back of the legs. He was looking for grip marks on the upper arms, which is what you get if a child is held under. He was looking for a mark on the back of the head or the shoulder blades, which is what you get if a child is pushed hard from behind, from standing. He found four fingermarks on the left forearm, in a pattern consistent with a rescue grab, and I was standing there, and I said, that's me, and it was.

That is all he found.

Because there was nothing else to find.

A bare foot placed against the frame of a sun lounger and pushed does not touch the child at all.

I have gone over the physics of it more times than is good for me, and in the February a man was paid a great deal of money to explain it to a jury with a scale model on a table, so I will do it once here and then leave it.

Emma was kneeling up on the lounger nearest the deep end, facing back toward the house, watching the side door because her father had gone through it to get a cake and she was seven and there were candles coming.

That is why she never saw it. She was facing the wrong way. She was facing the wrong way because of me.

The lounger was aluminium-framed, one of a pair, and light — four and a half kilos — and it stood on four feet on wet-proof sandstone, and it was one metre ten from the coping stone at the edge of the pool.

Vanessa was on the other lounger, to Emma's right, lying back, both hands in her lap.

She did not get up. She did not lean. She did not use her hands at all.

She extended her right leg diagonally forward and left, and she put the sole of her bare foot against the rear right leg of Emma's lounger, low down, near the ground, and she pushed it away from her — forward and left, toward the water.

A four-and-a-half-kilo frame with a twenty-two-kilo child kneeling high on it has almost all of its weight above the level of the push. It does not slide. It rotates. It goes over its own front feet.

Emma did not fall in. She was thrown in, over the top of the tipping frame, head and shoulders first, at the deep end, one metre out, where the water is two metres ten.

And nothing touched her. The chair touched her. Water touched her.

Every single thing that happened to my daughter that evening was, in the medical sense, consistent with a fall.

I have thought about how long a person would have to sit and look at a pool to arrive at that.

Vanessa's behaviour in the week after was, and I will say this plainly because pretending otherwise makes me look better and I am not writing this to look better, close to perfect.

She was at the hospital at seven every morning. She did the phone calls to the school and to Emma's swimming teacher and to my mother in Ireland, so that I did not have to say the words twelve times. She had the pool drained and refilled on the Thursday, because she said she could not stand the thought of it, and she wept when she said it, and the man came and did it, and eleven weeks later Detective Vargas asked me who had arranged that and I heard myself say she did and I heard how it sounded.

She said the thing about the fence herself, before anybody else could. She said: we should have fenced the deep end when Emma was born, we all said it, and nobody did it, and it is our fault. Our. She put herself in the sentence.

She brought me a shirt.

I want to say something about the shirt, because it is the smallest thing in this entire account and it is the one that people who have not been through it never believe.

On the Sunday night she went to the house and packed me a bag, and in the bag were dry jeans and my grey shirt with the button missing at the cuff, which is the shirt I wear when things are bad, which I did not know I did until a woman put it in a bag for me.

For four months, every time some small crooked thing surfaced — the fruit on the wrong side, the cook sent home early, my daughter pushing a horse magazine away across a blanket — the shirt came up out of me from underneath and ate it.

She brought me the right shirt. This is a woman who noticed which shirt.

It is not evidence of anything. It is not even kindness, necessarily. A person who is paying that much attention to what you need is a person who is paying attention.

The last thing I want to put down about that week is the thing the nurses said.

I heard it four separate times in five days, from four different women who had no reason to say anything at all, and by the Friday it had become a small warm fact about my life that I carried around: she's very good, your wife.

And she was.

Nobody comes to your bedside for three nights running out of malice. That is not what malice does; malice gets bored. Whatever kept Vanessa Rhodes in that chair from seven in the morning until one the next morning, three nights in a row, on eleven hours' total sleep, was not indifference to my daughter and it was not, I think, remorse either.

I have my own answer. I did not get to it for a long time, and I got to it by watching her at a different bedside, in a different year, in a different chapter of this.

She was not sitting with Emma.

She was sitting where the story was. She was the last adult who had seen it happen. Every account of that evening in the world came out of her mouth, and a story that is only in one person's mouth has to be kept — it has to be there in the room when a nurse asks an idle question at four in the morning, it has to be there when a paediatric liaison comes round with a face chart, it has to be there when a seven-year-old wakes up and starts talking.

Three nights in that chair was not devotion.

It was custody.

We brought Emma home on the Saturday, eleven days before the end of term.

There was a paper banner across the hall that said WELCOME HOME EMMA in Vanessa's handwriting, and a cake, a new one, because the first cake had gone in the bin at the hospital with the mud from my shoes still on the box.

Emma stopped in the doorway and looked at the banner for a while. Then she went in under it and along the hall and up the stairs to her room, and she did not go out the back of the house at all that day, or the next day, or for the remaining eleven weeks of that summer.

She was home for four hours before I understood that she was not going to walk past the kitchen window on the side that shows the pool. She would go the long way round, through the dining room, every time, and if you had not been watching for it you would never in a hundred years have seen it.

I watched her do it eleven times before I said anything.

And when I finally said something, in the August, standing in that dining room doorway, my daughter looked up at me from the floor where she was doing a jigsaw and said:

"Because she can see me from there."
