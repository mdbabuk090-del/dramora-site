She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 03 — The Oranges

Chapter 3 of 25

On the Monday morning I drove up to the house to get Emma's things.

Dr Ilesanmi had said she would be moved out of high dependency by the afternoon and would want her own pyjamas and her own toothbrush and, if there was one, whatever it was she slept with. There was one. A grey rabbit with a chewed ear called Bishop, named after a man who read the weather on a radio station Sarah used to have on in the kitchen. I have never been able to explain to anyone why my daughter named a rabbit after a weatherman, and neither could she.

Vanessa offered to go instead. I said no. I have wondered since whether that was the first thing I ever did right.

It was ten past nine and the light on the deck was completely different, high and white and flat, and everything looked like a photograph of the wrong place.

Nobody had touched anything. There was no reason to touch anything. No police officer had come to the house that night, because no crime had been reported — a child had fallen into a pool at her own home with two adults present and had been taken to hospital, and that is not a thing that generates a scene, it is a thing that generates a phone call to a duty social worker who ticks a box and closes a file.

So it was all still exactly as it had been left when the ambulance took us down the hill.

I stood at the top of the steps for a long time.

I am going to write down what I saw, in the order I saw it, and I am going to be careful, because a barrister spent an hour and ten minutes on this in the February trying to get me to say I had reconstructed it afterward.

I had not. I made a list on my phone that morning, standing there in the sun, and the list has a timestamp on it of 09:14, and it exists, and it was shown to a jury.

One. Emma's lounger, the one nearest the deep end, was lying on its left side with its four legs pointing at the water. Not toppled backward. Not slid. Pitched over forward, toward the pool, so that the whole frame had gone past its own centre.

Two. The cooler was where it had always been, exactly square between the two loungers, with its lid on. Not moved a centimetre. There was a wet ring on the lid the size of a dinner plate where a dish had been.

Three. The dish was in the pool. It had gone in flat and sunk and it was lying in one piece on the bottom at the deep end, a little to the left, about a metre and a half out from the wall.

Four. Nine halves of orange, on the water, and by the morning they had drifted down to the shallow end and gathered in a raft against the steps, and the water around them was faintly cloudy.

Five. Vanessa's lounger, the other one, was upright and square and had a paperback lying face-down on the foot of it, open, spine up, at page one hundred and four.

Six. Her sunglasses were on the arm of it, folded.

That was the list. Then I went inside and got Bishop and the pyjamas and I drove back down the hill.

Nobody looked at that deck for four months except me, and I looked at it every single day, and I want to explain what looking at a thing every day does, because it is the exact opposite of what you would expect.

It stops being evidence and becomes furniture.

If the police had come that night — if one uniformed officer with a phone camera had walked out onto that sandstone at nine in the evening — the case would have taken about nine weeks instead of nine months. There would have been a photograph of a lounger lying on its face. There would have been a note about a dish in the deep end. Somebody whose job it is to think about how objects arrive where they are would have stood there for four minutes and written down one sentence, and the sentence would have been: how did the fruit get so far out.

Nobody came. So the lounger stood back up on the Wednesday, because Marta came in and stood it up, because a lounger lying on its side in a garden is untidy and she had cooked in that house for eleven years and she could not walk past it.

And I let her. I stood in the kitchen window and watched her do it and I thought nothing at all.

The oranges bothered me all summer.

Not as a suspicion. I want to be honest about the register of it. It was not a suspicion, it was an itch, the way a wrong note in a piece of music you don't know very well is an itch — you cannot name the note, you just come away from the room slightly wrong.

Because here is the thing about the dish.

It was a shallow ceramic dish, oval, about thirty centimetres long, and it lived on the lid of the cooler, and the cooler stood between the two loungers. Which means that from where Emma was sitting, the dish was on her right, about sixty centimetres away, at a height of maybe fifty centimetres.

And the pool was on her left.

I did that arithmetic in about the second week of July, sitting on the same lounger with a cup of coffee going cold beside me, and I did it the way you do a crossword you are not really trying to finish. If she leaned right for the fruit, she went away from the water. If she leaned so hard for the fruit that the lounger tipped, the lounger tipped right. Onto the stone. Onto the cooler.

Not into a pool that was on the other side of her.

I sat with that for about forty seconds. And then I put it down.

I want anybody reading this who has been where I was to hear the next part properly, because it is the part I am ashamed of and it is also, I think, the most ordinary thing in this entire account.

I put it down because the alternative was too big to hold.

To pick that thought up and carry it, I would have had to already believe that my wife of fourteen months had put my daughter in that water. And I did not believe that. I did not disbelieve it either. It was not in the range of things I had opinions about, any more than I have an opinion about whether the sun will come up. Vanessa had sat by that hospital bed for three nights. Vanessa had brought me the right shirt.

So the arithmetic became a thing I had done wrong. Obviously the dish had been somewhere else. Obviously Emma had moved it, or Vanessa had passed it to her, or it had been on the ground, or the lounger had been at an angle I was not remembering. Obviously I had it wrong, because I had not been there, because I had been in the utility room getting a cake.

Everything I could not explain, I fed to the ninety seconds. And the ninety seconds ate all of it, all summer, without ever once getting full.

There is one more thing about that morning.

When I came back out through the side door with the rabbit under my arm, I went past the low garden wall, and the camera was still standing there on its little tripod with its lens cap off, aimed west at nothing.

I picked it up.

I have had to say this out loud in a courtroom and I will write it here in the same words. I picked up the camera on the morning of the fifteenth of June. I held it in my hand. It was warm from the sun on one side. I turned it over once and I saw that the screen was black, and I thought, battery's gone, and I thought about Sarah, briefly and badly, the way I did about forty times a day that year.

And I put it back down on the wall.

I did not take it inside. I did not press a single button. There was a card in it with one thousand one hundred and six photographs on it, and one of them was of my wife's leg, and I stood holding the thing in my own hand in the sunshine and I put it back down on the wall because I did not want to spend the next ten minutes looking at photographs of a sunset that my dead wife's camera had taken on the evening my daughter nearly drowned.

Four months.

Barrister asked me why. I said what I have just written. He asked whether it was not, in fact, that I did not want to know.

I said that at the time there was nothing to know.

He said, "There was everything to know, Mr Rhodes. It was in your hand."

I said, "Yes."
