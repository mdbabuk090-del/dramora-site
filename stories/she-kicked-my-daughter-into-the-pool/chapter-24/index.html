She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 24 — Filling It In

Chapter 24 of 25

We filled the pool in on a Thursday in the June, four days after Emma's ninth birthday and two years to the week from the fourteenth.

It cost eleven thousand pounds and took nine days and I want to say, because people assume it is a symbolic gesture that took an afternoon, that filling in a swimming pool properly is a serious civil engineering job. You cannot simply put earth in a hole. You have to punch drainage holes through the base so it does not become a bath under your garden. You have to break the top eighteen inches of the walls down below the finished level. You have to bring in about a hundred and forty tonnes of material and compact it in layers, and it has to be inspected, and it is on the deeds forever.

I know all this because Rhodes & Ivers does it. We have filled in nine of them over the years, mostly for care homes, and I have signed off the method statement for every one.

I did not do this one. I hired a firm from Marbury.

Vargas asked me why, when she came for the tea in the September, and I said because I did not want to stand on that deck with a clipboard being professional, and she said that was the most sensible thing she had ever heard me say.

I did not tell Emma we were going to do it.

I asked her.

That distinction turned out to be the most important thing in this chapter and I nearly got it wrong. I had already had the quotes. I had a start date pencilled. And then, four days before I was going to tell her, the woman I see on Tuesdays asked me one question, which was: "Whose pool is it?"

I said it was mine. It is my house.

And she said: "Michael, two people nearly died in it and one of them did. Neither of them was you."

So I asked.

I sat down at the kitchen table with my nine-year-old and I said, "I want to talk to you about the garden, and I want you to know before we start that there isn't a right answer and I'm not going to be upset with any answer you give me."

I said: we could fill the pool in. Or we could leave it and never use it. Or we could sell the house and go somewhere else entirely, which was also true and which I had had valued.

Emma thought about it for a long time. Longer than a child should be able to sit still for.

Then she said, "If we move, does she know where we've gone?"

That was the first question. Not the pool. Whether an address in a prison file follows you.

I said no. I said that we could go anywhere in the world and nobody would tell her, and that in any case she would be sixty-eight.

Emma said, "Okay." And then: "I don't want to move. Mum lived here."

And then she said the thing that decided it:

"But I don't want to look at it. I don't want to look at it when I'm doing my homework."

They started on the ninth of June.

Emma stayed at school for the first three days, which was her own idea, and Marta came and got her — Marta had been back in our lives for a year and a half by then, twice a week, not as a cook, as Marta — and on the fourth day, Saturday, Emma came out and stood at the top of the steps with her arms folded and watched a machine put six tonnes of stone into the deep end.

She stood there for about forty minutes without saying anything at all.

Then she said, "It's going really slowly."

I said, "It has to. If you dump it all in at once it settles wrong and you get a dip in ten years."

And Emma, aged nine, said, "That's annoying," and went back inside, and that was that.

She chose what went on top.

I had assumed grass, because that is what everybody does — you fill it in, you turf it, and in three summers you cannot see where it was. I had a price for turf.

Emma wanted a vegetable patch.

I said, "Really? Vegetables?"

And she said, "Not for eating. Well — for eating. But mostly because you have to keep going out there."

I did not understand for a second, and then I did, and I had to go into the utility room for a minute.

A lawn is a thing you look at. A vegetable patch is a thing that makes you go and stand in the exact spot, every few days, forever, because something needs water or something has bolted or the netting has come off.

My nine-year-old daughter designed, without any help from anybody, a therapy that a qualified professional later described to me as "actually rather sophisticated."

There are raised beds over the deep end. There are four apple trees on the shallow end where the steps were, because the base is shallower there and you can get roots in. There is a bench.

The bench is where the two loungers were, and it faces west, and the cypresses are still there, and it is the best view in the house.

I sat on it once, on the evening the men finished, on my own, with a beer, and I got about eleven minutes into it before I had to get up.

Emma sits on it all the time.

The last thing.

When they broke out the coping stones at the deep end — the flat sandstone slabs at the edge, the ones I had knelt on — the foreman came and found me in the kitchen and said, "Mr Rhodes, do you want these kept? People sometimes want one for a step or something."

I said no.

Then I went out and looked at them stacked on a pallet, eleven of them, grey-gold, worn smooth, and I stood there for a while.

There is one slab that both of them went off. Sarah in the March, in the dark, and Emma in the June, in the sun, twenty-seven months apart, and I have no idea which one of the eleven it is and I never will, because nobody was measuring in the March.

I had them taken away and broken up for hardcore.

And then I went back into my house, where the kitchen window on that side looks out at four apple trees and a bench, and my daughter was at the table doing her homework with her back to the window, which is how she still sits.

I have never once asked her to turn round.
