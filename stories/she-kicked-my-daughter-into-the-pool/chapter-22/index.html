She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 22 — Frame 251

Chapter 22 of 25

The imaging analyst was called Dr Ewan Traill and he came from a laboratory in Bristol, and he spent three days in the witness box in the February, and for two and a half of them he was extremely boring on purpose.

That is a technique, apparently. You establish, at enormous length, exactly what a camera can and cannot do, so that when you finally put a photograph on the screen the jury has stopped thinking of it as a picture and started thinking of it as a measurement.

He did the physics of frame 214 first. The angle of the lounger. The eleven degrees. The contact point on the rear right leg, low, near the ground, which is not where a hand goes and is not where a person's foot goes when they are trying to stop a chair, because to stop a chair you brace it high, against the seat frame, and every one of the twelve people in that jury box knew that in their bodies without being told.

He was asked, in cross-examination, whether the photograph proved force.

He said: "The photograph shows contact. The subsequent frame shows the chair on its side approximately one metre from where it stood, and the child in the water at the deep end approximately one metre out from the wall. Something did work on that chair. I can tell you the work was done. I cannot photograph the intention."

That was the honest answer and it cost them, and I want it in this account because it is the reason I trusted every other word he said.

They came to frame 251 on the afternoon of the third day.

I had asked, through the officer in the case, not to be told when it was coming. Vargas had said no, absolutely not, and had sat down next to me at lunchtime and said, "It's this afternoon. I'm telling you because if that goes up on a screen and you're not ready, you will make a noise, and the jury will look at you instead of at it."

I said, "What's in it?"

And she said, "Her face."

Here is what frame 251 is.

18:24:50. Three minutes and five seconds after the kick.

Michael Rhodes, aged forty, is on his knees on the sandstone at the deep end with his back to the camera and his daughter across his lap. Her head is toward the water. His head is down. There are nine halves of orange on the surface behind them, drifting, and the shadow of the cypresses is across the whole left side of the frame.

Vanessa Rhodes is behind him, crouched in close, with her right hand flat on his shoulder.

Her face is turned toward the camera, slightly, by accident, because she is looking past his head at the pool.

In the original file her face occupies about a hundred and eleven pixels.

Traill did not enhance it, and he was very careful with the word. He said enhancement implies adding, and nothing was added.

He said what he had was a twenty-four-megapixel photograph taken at f/5.6 at 1/250th on a bright evening, which is an enormous amount of data, and that the face was small in the frame but was not blurred — it was simply small, and correctly exposed, and in focus, because the depth of field at that distance covered it.

He put it up in stages, so that the jury could see there was no trick.

The whole frame. Then a quarter of the frame. Then the deck. Then two figures. Then a woman's head and shoulders at a size where you could see that she had a face and not what was on it.

Then the last one.

Nobody made a sound.

I have been in that courtroom for eleven days by then and there had been coughing and papers and somebody's phone on the Tuesday, and for about four seconds after that image went up there was nothing at all.

She is looking at the water.

Her hand is on my shoulder. Her head is close to the side of my head, tipped in, the way you do when you are holding somebody who is broken.

And she is smiling.

It is not a grin. That is what I need to say, because people who have only read about this case imagine something theatrical, and it was so much worse than that. It is the smallest possible expression. The corners of the mouth are up by a few millimetres. The eyes are slightly narrowed and the skin at the outer corner has creased, which is the part Traill spent forty minutes on, because that crease is the difference between an arrangement of muscles you can produce on purpose and one you cannot.

It is the face of a person watching something go well.

It is exactly, precisely, the face you would make if you were told at a table that a difficult piece of work you had done had come off.

The defence's answer was the only answer available and they were right to make it, and I am going to set it out fairly.

They said: this is a still photograph of one twenty-fifth of a second, taken from a woman's face at a distance of eleven metres, at the end of the most frightening five minutes of her life. They said the human face does extraordinary and unreadable things under extreme stress, that people laugh at funerals, that a rictus of horror and a smile are the same muscles, and that to convict a woman of murder on the basis of the corners of a mouth in one frame out of eleven hundred is exactly what the criminal justice system exists to prevent.

Traill was asked whether he could say it was a smile of pleasure.

He said no. He said: "I can tell you what the muscles are doing. I cannot tell you why."

And the judge said, in the summing up, in terms, to the jury: you may not convict on the photograph of the face. It is not capable of bearing that weight. Consider it, if at all, only alongside everything else.

I sat there and listened to him say it and I thought, he is right, and it does not matter.

Because by that point in the trial the jury had the pill organiser, and the voicemail, and the eleven weeks, and the pronoun, and a company at a storage unit, and a bath in Kendal, and a complaint from an eighty-one-year-old woman that nobody upheld, and a residuary clause with a pencil line beside it, and a scrape and a chair going up at the front.

The photograph did not convict her.

The photograph is simply the only thing in this entire case that ever showed me why.

Everything else in nine months of evidence explained the mechanism. Access, medication, signature, trust deed, opportunity, arithmetic. All of it is the how.

Frame 251 is a woman with her hand on a man's shoulder while he holds his drowned child, and she is pleased.

There is no motive in the world underneath that. There is nothing under it at all. It goes all the way down.

I did not make a noise.

I sat with my hands flat on my knees and I looked at the screen for as long as it was up, which was eleven minutes, and I made myself look at my own back in the bottom of the frame — a man on his knees with his head down, grateful, with a hand on his shoulder.

And afterward, in the corridor, Vargas came and stood next to me without saying anything for a while, and then she said:

"You know what I keep thinking about? She stayed."

I said, "What?"

She said: "Three minutes and five seconds after she did it, with a camera she didn't know was there. She could have been in the house. She could have been on the phone at the other end of the deck. She came and put her hand on you and she stayed, and she'll have thought that was the cleverest thing she did that evening."

She looked at the doors.

"It's the only reason we've got her face at all."
