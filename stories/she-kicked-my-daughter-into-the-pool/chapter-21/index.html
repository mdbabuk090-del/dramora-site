She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 21 — What Emma Remembers

Chapter 21 of 25

The video interview was recorded on the fourteenth of January in a house in a residential street with net curtains and a garden, which is what they use for children, and which does not look like anything at all from the outside, which is the point of it.

I was not in the room. I was in a second room with a television screen and a family liaison officer and a plastic cup of water, and I was told before it started that if I made a sound I would be taken out, and the officer said it apologetically and she meant every word of it.

It lasted fifty-one minutes. Emma was seven years and seven months old.

The woman who interviewed her — Sergeant Aisling Doherty, who does nothing else, who has done four hundred of these — spent the first nineteen minutes talking to my daughter about horses.

I want to describe what a properly conducted child interview is like, because it is the exact opposite of what television has taught everybody, and because watching it was the single most humbling hour of my life.

She never asked a leading question. Not one, in fifty-one minutes.

She did not say did Vanessa kick you. She did not say tell me about the pool. She said, at the twenty-minute mark, "Emma, do you know why you're here today?" — and when Emma said yes, she said, "Tell me what you know about that," and then she stopped talking, and let a seven-year-old fill a silence for forty seconds, which is an eternity, and which I could not have done.

She said tell me more about that eleven times.

She said what happened next nine times.

And four separate times, when Emma said something that mattered, Doherty said: "How do you know that?"

That question is the whole thing. That is the question that took my daughter's account and turned it from a thing a child said into evidence, and it is the question I never once asked her in four months.

Here is what came out of it.

Emma did not see the kick.

She said so at minute twenty-three, unprompted, in the middle of her own account, in exactly the same words she had used to me in June and to Marta in July: "I didn't see it. I was looking at the door."

Doherty said, "How do you know it was a kick, then?"

And Emma said, "Because of the noise it made and because of where the chair went."

Doherty said, "Tell me more about the noise."

And my daughter said: "It scraped. Like when you push a chair in at school with your foot because you're not allowed to but you do it anyway. And then it went up at the front."

Fifty-one minutes, and that is the closest she ever came to describing the act itself, and it is a scrape and a chair going up at the front, and it is worth more than a hundred children saying I saw her do it, and every single lawyer in that case knew it.

What she did see was afterwards.

This is the part I could not sit still for, and the liaison officer put her hand on my arm.

Doherty asked what happened when she came up.

Emma said: "I came up and I couldn't get any air in, and then I went down, and then I came up again and I could see her."

"Where was she?"

"Standing up. At the edge. Where the stone bit is."

"What was she doing?"

And Emma said: "Nothing."

Doherty let that sit for a moment and then said, gently, "Tell me more about nothing."

And my seven-year-old daughter said:

"She was just standing. Her arms were down. She wasn't reaching. When you fall over at school and someone comes, they put their arms out even if they're not going to do anything. She had her arms down."

And then the face.

Doherty said, "You said you could see her. Could you see her face?"

Emma said yes.

Doherty said, "What was her face doing?"

And this is where I have to be careful, because this is the piece that a defence barrister spent an entire afternoon on in the February, and rightly, and I have thought about it a great deal.

Emma said: "Normal."

Doherty said, "Can you help me with normal?"

And Emma said: "Like when she's waiting for the kettle."

Nobody teaches a child that.

I want to put down what the barrister said, because he was doing his job and because he was not, in fact, wrong.

He said: children of seven do not have the vocabulary to describe faces, and so they borrow. He said that the phrase like when she's waiting for the kettle is a construction, and that in six months of a father's distress, in a house where the child knew what the adults suspected, a comparison like that could be assembled without a single person meaning to plant it. He said, and this is the sentence that got closest: "This child was not lying to you. This child was helping you."

And that is a real thing. It happens. It has ruined real cases and it has convicted innocent people.

Here is why it did not work in this one, and it has nothing to do with Emma.

At 18:21:50 — frame 215, five seconds after the kick — a twenty-four-megapixel camera on a garden wall photographed Vanessa Rhodes sitting up on a lounger with her head turned toward the water.

At 18:21:55, 18:22:00, and 18:22:05 it photographed her standing at the pool edge.

In all three of those photographs her arms are down at her sides.

My daughter, who was in two metres ten of water at the time, aged seven, who could not swim, who came up twice — described the position of a woman's arms, in an account given seven months later, and a camera she did not know was running agreed with her.

Doherty had no idea. That is the thing that finished it in front of the jury. Sergeant Doherty had not been shown the photographs, deliberately, and could not have led her toward them if she had wanted to, and it is all on tape, and there is nothing in fifty-one minutes that any barrister on earth could point at.

There is one more thing in that interview and it is not evidence of anything and it never went in front of anybody, and it is the only part I have watched more than twice.

At minute forty-eight, at the very end, when it was all over and Doherty had told her she had done really well and asked whether there was anything else she wanted to say, Emma said:

"Can I say a thing about my dad?"

And Doherty said, "Of course you can."

And my daughter said, to a police officer, on tape, in a house with net curtains:

"He does believe me. He just took ages."
