She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 04 — Bed Nine

Chapter 4 of 25

They moved her to bed nine on the Monday afternoon, on the general children's ward, which is the bed by the window with a view of a car park and, beyond the car park, a strip of sea about the width of your thumb.

She had been off the ventilator since eleven that morning. Her voice had gone — not gone, exactly, but sanded down to about a third of itself, which Dr Ilesanmi said was the tube and not the water and would come back inside a week. Her throat hurt. Her chest hurt when she coughed. There was a bruise coming up on the inside of her left forearm where I had grabbed her, four fingermarks, mine, and a nurse photographed it on the Tuesday as a matter of routine and I had to explain it, and I remember being frightened by how easily I could not prove it.

She slept most of Monday.

At about six in the evening I was sitting on the plastic chair with my phone face-down on my knee, and she opened her eyes and looked at the ceiling for a while, and then she turned her head and looked at me.

I said, "Hello, monkey."

And Emma said, in a voice like paper, "Don't let her in."

I want to slow down here, because this is the hinge of the whole book and it took me four months to walk through it.

I said, "Don't let who in, sweetheart?"

She said, "Vanessa."

And I said — and I can hear myself saying it, in exactly the tone I would have used to tell her there was nothing under the bed — "Vanessa's just gone to get a coffee. She's been here all night, monkey. She's not going to hurt you."

Emma looked at me for a long moment. Seven years old, grey around the mouth, a cannula taped to the back of one hand.

Then she turned her head and looked at the ceiling again and did not say anything else about it that day.

That is it. That is the entire exchange. Nine words from her, twenty-six from me, and then a child deciding something about her father that took the better part of a year to undo.

Vanessa came back in at twenty past six with two coffees and a magazine she had bought in the shop downstairs, a horse magazine, because Emma had been horse-mad since she was five.

She put it on the bed and said, "I got you the one with the plaits on the front."

And I watched my daughter — and this is a thing I saw, on the Monday, with my own eyes, and did not understand until October — I watched my daughter turn her face toward the window and keep it there, and put her right hand flat on the magazine and push it about ten centimetres away from herself across the blanket, and leave it there.

Vanessa's face did something small and hurt, and she said, "That's all right, poppet. It's there when you want it."

And I said, "She's exhausted, love. She's not herself."

I said that. I have to write it down. My daughter did the only thing available to a seven-year-old with no voice and a tube in her hand, and I turned to the woman who had put her in the water and I apologised for her.

Over the next four days there were three visits from professionals, and I want to set out what happened in each of them, because people who have never been near this assume that a child telling the truth is a bomb going off, and it is not. It is a very quiet thing that gets absorbed by a system that is doing exactly what it was designed to do.

The ward sister, Tuesday, on her own, while I was down at the car moving it: asked Emma what she remembered. Emma said she was on the chair and then she was in the water. Sister wrote no recall of event, consistent with hypoxic episode, which was correct, and which was also, unfortunately, true.

The paediatric liaison, Wednesday: a very good woman called Priya Naidu who sat on the end of the bed and drew a face chart. She asked Emma to point at how she felt about coming home. Emma pointed at the flat mouth. Naidu asked if there was anything at home that felt not-safe. Emma said, "The pool." Naidu wrote appropriate water anxiety post-immersion, advise no pressure to swim, which was correct, and which was also true.

The duty social worker, Thursday, twenty minutes, mostly with me: had the household composition, had the accident description, had two adults present and one of them non-swimming, had no prior contacts, had a family known to nobody. Closed it Friday.

Nobody asked Emma the one question. Nobody said: did somebody do that to you.

And I have thought very hard about whether I am being unfair to any of those three women and I do not think I am, because that question does not belong to them. It belonged to me.

She said it to me properly on the Thursday.

She was sitting up. Her voice had come back about halfway. She had eaten some of a yoghurt and she was picking the corner of the foil lid into little pieces, which is a thing she does when she is going to say something.

She said, "Daddy. She kicked me."

I said, "What?"

She said, "Vanessa. She kicked me in. With her leg."

I did not say anything for about four seconds. I want that on the record too: I did not immediately say no. There were four seconds where the whole thing was open.

Then I said, "Monkey — you went in off the chair. You leaned over for an orange and the chair went over. You don't remember it because you were poorly. That's what happens when—"

And Emma said, with the flat certainty of a child who has not yet learned that certainty is a thing adults will punish you for, "I wasn't getting an orange. I don't like the white bits."

She did not like the white bits.

She had never liked the white bits. She would not eat an orange that had pith on it, which is why they were cut in halves in the first place, which is why the dish was there, which is a thing Marta had done for her every day of that summer because otherwise she would not eat fruit at all.

If you had asked me, on any day of Emma's life before that Thursday, whether my daughter would lean out of a chair for a piece of orange with the white bits still on it, I would have laughed.

I sat in a plastic chair in a children's ward with that in my hands and I did not know what to do with it, so I did what four months of my life were made of.

I said, "You were poorly, monkey. It'll come back different."

She looked at me. She had Sarah's flat, patient look, the one that meant I have understood you exactly and I am not going to argue, and she went back to picking the foil.

She did not say it again for six weeks.

I am not going to pretend that I have made peace with this chapter.

I have been told by a woman I pay to sit opposite me on Tuesdays that a man cannot be expected to leap, in one step, from my child fell in the pool to my wife tried to kill her, and that expecting it of myself is a way of not getting better.

I understand the argument. I even think it is true.

But I was in that room. I was the only person in the world in that room. My daughter told me on the Thursday afternoon, in her own words, in a voice she barely had, and she used the correct verb — kicked, not pushed, not threw, kicked, which was a word no adult would have thought to plant in her and which turned out four months later to be anatomically exact —

and I told her she was remembering it wrong.

We took her home on the Saturday. Vanessa had put a banner up in the hall.
