She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 18 — The Ninth of December

Chapter 18 of 25

I had been told not to.

Vargas had said it in about four different ways over eight weeks, and the last time she said it she had put her hand flat on the table, which she had never done before, and said: "Michael. If she finds out we are looking, one of two things happens. Either she runs — which, frankly, I could live with. Or she doesn't run, and she is in a house with your daughter, and she now has nothing whatever to lose."

I understood it. I have a note of it. I did it anyway.

I want to be honest about why, and the honest answer is not noble. It was not about evidence and it was not about Emma. It was that I had spent eight weeks being extremely well-behaved in my own home — eating dinner opposite her, saying goodnight, letting her put her hand on my leg in the car — and on the ninth of December, at about four in the afternoon, having read a statement from a ninety-year-old woman in Rochdale who said I wasn't muddled, love, I was eighty-one, I got in the car.

I did not decide to. I want that understood by anybody who has been under something like this for a long time. There was no decision. There was a man in a car on the coast road with the wipers going.

Gil lives in a converted mill flat on the river at Astley, eleven miles out.

He opened the door with a mug in his hand and said, "Mikey," and then he looked at my face.

And he did not ask me what was wrong.

I have replayed that half-second perhaps a thousand times, and I have had it explained to me by two different professionals that I may be reading far too much into a man's face at a door, and I do not care. My brother is a person who asks. He is the one who rings you when your voice is slightly off on the phone. If I had turned up on his doorstep on any other evening of my life looking like that, the first four words out of him would have been what's happened, what is it.

He stepped back to let me in and said, "Come in out of it."

I did not accuse him of anything.

I sat on his sofa and I said, "Tell me about Calder Bay Rehabilitation Services."

And Gil Rhodes, forty years old, my brother, forty per cent of my grandfather's firm, put his mug down on the arm of the chair, missed the arm of the chair, and dropped it.

He got down on the floor to pick up the pieces and he stayed down there for much longer than picking up four pieces of a mug takes, and I sat on the sofa and watched the top of my brother's head and I felt the last two years arrive in me all at once.

When he came up his face was wet.

He said, "It's a company."

I said, "I know it's a company. I know who owns it and I know what's in it. I want you to tell me about Sarah."

And Gil said, "I didn't know about Sarah."

Not what about Sarah. Not what do you mean.

I didn't know about Sarah.

That is the sentence. That is the whole nine years, right there in a mill flat at ten past five on a wet Tuesday, and it came out of him before he had decided anything, and it is the reason my brother is in prison.

He talked for two hours and eleven minutes and I did not record any of it, which is the single most expensive mistake in this entire story and which Vargas has never once thrown at me.

What he said, in the order he said it:

He owed money. Not to anyone frightening — to a betting company, and then to a second betting company, and then to a bank, and by that autumn to a man in Warrington, and the total by the November of that year was a hundred and nine thousand pounds against a forty per cent share of a company that could not have been sold in a hurry for anything at all without me finding out inside a day.

He met her at the gym, and it was not a chance meeting, and he knew that now, and at the time it was the best month of his life.

She told him about a life-insurance introduction scheme. A commission arrangement. Perfectly legal, she said, and stupid, and slightly grubby, and worth eleven thousand pounds to him for signing his name in a box as a witness on a form he did not read, on a policy for a woman he had known for eleven years.

And he did it, and he told himself it was paperwork, and he took the money in three instalments in the February.

He said — and this is where I got up and walked to the window and stood with my back to him — he said: "It was a life policy, Mikey. Nobody was going to die. She was thirty-eight."

Then he said: "And then she did, and I sat in that church and I thought I was going to be sick, and I told myself, no. Cars. It was a car crash and a pelvis and pills. And I never claimed a penny of it. Ask them. There's no claim."

I said, "There's no claim because a claim would have brought the insurer down on it in a fortnight, and she knew that, and you didn't, and you have spent two and a half years thinking you were lucky."

And then I asked him about the fourteenth of June, and my brother told me the thing that finished it.

The phone call at 3:26 in the afternoon. Sixteen seconds. To my landline, from his mobile, on a Sunday, on my daughter's birthday.

He said she had rung him three days before that and asked him whether, as a trustee, he had ever actually read the Ardwell trust deed. He said he had not. He said she read him the residuary clause down the phone — the part about what happens if the child does not reach twenty-five — and asked him, in a completely ordinary voice, whether he understood what that meant for the two of them.

He said he told her not to talk like that.

He said she laughed and said she was only being practical, and that with a child in the house you have to think about these things, and that people are very strange about money and never plan.

And on the Sunday, he said, he got frightened.

He said: "I rang the house."

I said, "Why?"

He said, "To hear who picked up."

Sixteen seconds. On the fourteenth of June, at twenty-six minutes past three, my brother telephoned my house from a car park because he had a bad feeling, and Vanessa answered, and he asked her what they were all up to, and she said Michael's doing the pool and Emma's got the camera out and we're having a quiet one.

And he said, "Right. Lovely. Say happy birthday to her."

And he put the phone down.

Three hours later my daughter was face-down at the deep end, and my brother heard about it at eleven o'clock that night by text message and drove to the hospital and sat in the corridor with me until two in the morning and put his coat over my legs.

I said, "You knew."

And Gil said, "No. Mikey — no. I wondered. For about four seconds on a Sunday afternoon, and then I told myself I was being disgusting, and I put my phone in my pocket and I went and had lunch with a woman I was seeing."

I drove home.

I want to record that I did not shout at him and I did not hit him, and that when I left he was sitting on his own floor with his back against the sofa, and he said, "What do I do," and I said, "You ring the police tonight," and he said, "Will you come with me," and I said no.

He rang them at ten past nine that night. That is on the record and it counted for something later, and it is the only reason he got what he got instead of what he might have got.

And at twenty past nine, while my brother was on the phone to Fenn Street police station, I was standing in my own kitchen putting my keys in the bowl.

Vanessa was at the table with her laptop and a glass of wine.

She said, "Where've you been? You've been ages."

And I heard myself say, in a completely normal voice: "Gil's. He's in a state about the Marbury job."

She looked at me for slightly longer than you look at a person.

Then she said, "Poor Gil," and went back to her screen.

Vargas came to the house at six the following morning with four officers, and afterwards she told me that when the phone rang at half past eleven the previous night and it was a man saying my name is Gilbert Rhodes and I want to talk about an insurance policy, she had known, before he had finished the sentence, exactly which stupid, dangerous, disobedient thing I had gone and done.

She never mentioned it again.

Not once, in nine months, including on the day in the February when a barrister stood up and used it to try to destroy me.
