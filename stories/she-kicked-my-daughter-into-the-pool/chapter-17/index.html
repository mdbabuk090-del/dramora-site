She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 17 — Before She Was Vanessa

Chapter 17 of 25

My wife changed her name by deed poll on the eleventh of April, four years before I met her, in an office in Preston, for a fee of thirty-six pounds.

I found that out from a police officer.

There is a particular kind of vertigo in learning, at forty-two years old, that the person you have slept beside for two years has a set of documents behind the documents — not a false identity, nothing so dramatic, nothing illegal at all. Thousands of people change their names every year. It is a form and a fee and a solicitor's stamp.

She had told me she had no family and had left home at sixteen and did not want to talk about any of it, and she had told me that in the third month, in a car, crying, and I had held her hand and said she never had to.

I had been so pleased with myself for that. For not making her.

Her name was Nessa Doyle.

She was born in Barrow, and she did have a family — a mother, alive, in a residential home outside Kendal, whom she had not seen in eleven years, and a half-brother in Canada — and she qualified as a physiotherapist properly, at a real university, in 2011, which is a thing I want to say because in the weeks after her arrest people wrote a great deal on the internet about a fake physio and none of it was true. She was good at her job. Every clinical record of her practice is unremarkable. That is part of the horror of it and there is no comfort anywhere in this account, and I am not going to invent any.

Between 2013 and 2018 she worked in Cumbria, latterly in a private community rehabilitation practice in Kendal doing exactly what she did for Sarah: home visits, one to one, long courses, mostly elderly, some post-operative.

In September 2016 she moved in with a man called Alan Kyte.

Alan Kyte was fifty-eight when he met her and she was twenty-nine.

He was a quantity surveyor, twice divorced, no children, and he had a nerve condition in his legs that had come on in his fifties and got worse quickly and had put him into early retirement and then into a house with a stairlift.

She was not his physiotherapist. I want to be precise, because the tabloids were not: she was the physiotherapist of a woman in the same street, and she and Alan Kyte met at a bus stop in the rain in the July, and there are people in that street who remember thinking how nice it was for him.

She moved in fourteen months after they met. They were not married. In the March of 2018 he made a will leaving her the house, which was his and mortgage-free, and eleven months later, on the twelfth of February 2019, Alan Kyte was found dead in his own bath at ten past nine in the evening by the woman he lived with, who had been out at a supermarket, and who came home and rang 999 at 21:14 and was, the call handler noted, screaming.

Drowning, in domestic bathwater, in a man with a documented neurological condition affecting his legs and his grip.

Coroner: accidental death. Nine minutes. The word tragic, once.

There was no suspicion of anything at any time, and I want to say clearly that if you put me on a jury and read me those facts alone I would have voted to acquit in about four minutes, because a disabled man drowning in his own bath is one of the most ordinary deaths in the country.

She sold the house in the October and moved south and changed her name in the April, and eleven months later my brother met a physiotherapist through his gym.

I asked Vargas the question everybody asks, and she gave me an answer I did not expect.

I said: "How did nobody see it? Two drownings."

She said: "Because nobody has ever had both. Cumbria had a bath. We had a pool. Two different forces, two years apart, two different names, and both of them closed as accidents by different coroners inside ten minutes each, because both of them were — Mr Rhodes, listen to me — both of them were entirely credible accidents. There's no computer anywhere in this country that puts those two things on the same screen. The only thing on earth that connects them is a person, and she changed her name."

I said, "So what put them together?"

And Vargas said: "You did. On the tenth of October, with a laptop."

The Kyte case was reopened in the December, and the disclosure that came out of it is the most frightening single document in this entire story, and it is not about Alan Kyte at all.

It is about a woman called Dorothy Sansom, aged eighty-one, of Kendal, who was one of Nessa Doyle's home rehabilitation patients in 2017, and who had a fall on the stairs in the December of that year and broke her hip and did not die.

Mrs Sansom made a complaint about her physiotherapist in the January of 2018 — a written complaint, to the practice, on paper — which was investigated internally over five weeks and not upheld, and which was retained in a file in a cabinet in an office in Kendal for seven years.

The complaint says that the physiotherapist had begun asking her about her will.

The investigation concluded that the patient was confused. There is a note on the file, in the practice manager's handwriting, saying that Mrs Sansom had been "somewhat muddled since her admission" and that the physiotherapist had been "very upset" by the allegation and had offered to withdraw from the case.

Nessa Doyle did not withdraw from the case. She continued to treat Dorothy Sansom for another nine weeks, until Mrs Sansom moved to her daughter's house in Rochdale in the March.

Dorothy Sansom is ninety now. She is alive. She gave a statement.

She said: "I told them. I told them at the time and they wrote it down and they said I was muddled. I wasn't muddled, love. I was eighty-one."

I went to see my brother on the ninth of December.

I want to put down why, because Vargas expressly asked me not to and I did it anyway, and it nearly cost the entire case.

It was not to get anything out of him. It was not some plan.

I went because I had spent two months building a picture of a woman who arrives in a household through a door somebody else is holding open, and I had got all the way to the end of it, and there was only one door left that I had not looked at, and I had known the man standing in it for thirty-seven years.

He opened the door in a jumper with a mug in his hand and said, "Mikey," and I saw his face change as he took in mine, and he did not ask me what was wrong.

That is how I knew.

He did not ask me what was wrong.
