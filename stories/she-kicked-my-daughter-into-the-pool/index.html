She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

I was gone ninety seconds. My wife told the paramedics that Emma had reached for an orange and slipped. Four months later I found my dead wife's camera still standing on the garden wall, and it had been taking one photograph every five seconds.

Chapter 01 — I'll Get the Cake

Chapter 1 of 25

I was gone for ninety seconds.

I have written that sentence first because it is the only part of this story that nobody has ever argued with me about. Everything else has been argued about — in a police station, in two courtrooms, in a hallway outside a hospital room by a man who is my brother — but not that. Ninety seconds is what it takes to walk from the pool deck of that house, through the side door, across the kitchen, to the second refrigerator in the utility room where a cake is kept so that a seven-year-old will not find it, and to come back out again carrying it.

I have walked it since. I made myself walk it, in the March, with a detective standing at the bottom of the garden holding a stopwatch and looking at me the way you look at a man doing something he should not be doing to himself. Eighty-eight seconds the first time. Ninety-four the second, because the second time my hands were not right.

So: ninety seconds. Call it that.

It was the fourteenth of June and it was Emma's seventh birthday, and the party had been the day before.

That is a thing I have had to explain more times than I can count, because people hear birthday and they picture twenty children and a bouncy castle and a woman with a clipboard, and then they cannot understand how nobody saw anything. The party was Saturday. Eleven children, two hours, a magician who was not very good. Sunday was meant to be the quiet one. Sunday was meant to be just us.

Emma had asked for that. She was seven and she had asked for a day with no one in it, which I remember thinking was a strange thing for a child to want and which I have thought about a great deal since.

So it was the three of us. Me, and Emma, and Vanessa.

The house is in the hills above Calder Bay and the pool deck faces west, which means that in June the light comes across it low and long from about six in the evening and everything on that deck gets a shadow four times its own length. Sandstone paving. Two low loungers turned out toward the water. Between them a cooler with a lid, and on the lid a shallow ceramic dish of oranges, cut in halves, because Emma had gone through a phase that summer of only eating fruit if somebody else had cut it.

Beyond the far end of the pool the ground drops away and there is a line of cypress trees, very dark and very still, and past those the sea, which you cannot hear from the deck. That is the thing about that house. It looks out over the water and you cannot hear a single wave. It is the quietest place I have ever lived and I used to say so to people, at dinners, as though it were a thing I had achieved.

The pool is nine metres long. At the near end it is one metre twenty. At the far end, where the two loungers were, it is two metres ten.

Emma could not swim. She could put her face in the water at a lesson, with a teacher's hand under her sternum, and she could hold the edge and kick, and she had a certificate from the previous autumn with a picture of a turtle on it. That is what she could do. Two metres ten is over the head of a grown man.

I want to be accurate about that, because for a while afterward people said she could swim a bit as though a bit were a thing that exists.

At twenty past six I went in to get the cake.

I remember the sentence I said because I have heard it played back to me in a room with the blinds down, and because I have said it to myself perhaps four thousand times since in the doorway of my own kitchen at two in the morning.

I stood up off the lounger and I said, "I'll get the cake."

Emma's face did the thing it did. She had a way of lighting up that started at the eyebrows, and she went up onto her knees on the lounger to watch me go, and she said something about candles that I did not catch and did not turn round for.

That is the piece I would take back if I were allowed one piece. Not the ninety seconds. The not turning round.

Vanessa was on the other lounger with a book she had been on the same page of for an hour, in a wine-coloured swimsuit and a cream robe that she had let fall open, and sunglasses, and she said something pleasant. I don't remember what. Something pleasant.

I remember one more thing and I am going to put it here even though it means nothing yet.

On the low garden wall at the edge of the deck, where the sandstone ends and the planting starts, there was a camera. Not a security camera. A small black bridge camera on a folding tripod about the height of a dinner plate, aimed west along the length of the pool at the sunset, and it had been there since a few minutes past six because Emma had put it there.

I looked straight at it on my way past. I know I did, because to go in the side door you look that way. I thought: she's got Mum's camera out again. And I kept walking.

The kitchen was cool and had the blinds half down.

Marta had gone home at four. Marta Ferreira had cooked in that house for eleven years, since before Emma, since before all of it, and on a normal Sunday she would have stayed until seven and there would have been a woman in that kitchen with a view of the deck through the window over the sink. She had gone at four because she had been told she could.

I did not know that then. I found it out in the August and it is in chapter eight of this, because it belongs there and not here.

I went through to the utility room. The cake was in the second refrigerator behind a tray of something covered in foil. It was a plain vanilla sponge with white icing and seven candles already pushed into it in a ring, and it had a sugar turtle on it, because of the certificate.

I picked it up in both hands. I turned round.

And I heard the water.

Here is what I need you to understand about the sound.

A swimming pool makes noise all day. Somebody gets in, somebody gets out, a lilo slaps, the filter cycles. I had lived beside that pool for six years. There is no version of me that runs at the sound of water.

What I heard was not a splash. It was heavier than a splash and it stopped too fast, and underneath it there was a second sound, a wooden knock, the sound of furniture going over on stone.

I was already moving when I heard Emma.

She got one word out and then half of another.

"Daddy! I can't—"

And then Vanessa started screaming her name.

"Emma! Emma! Emma!"

I do not remember putting the cake down. It was found later on the floor of the utility room, upside down, and that detail was in a newspaper.

I came out of the side door into that low orange light and I could not see her.

That is the part I still dream. Not the water. The half-second of a deck with nobody on it. The two loungers, one of them lying on its side with its legs pointing at the pool. The cooler. The dish gone off the lid. And the whole surface of the pool broken up and moving and covered — actually covered, I could not understand it, my mind refused it — with floating halves of orange.

Nine, they counted afterward. Nine orange halves, cut side up, most of them at the deep end, riding the chop.

Vanessa was standing at the edge with both hands over her mouth.

Then Emma came up. About a metre out from the wall, and she came up the way a child comes up who has never come up before, with her chin high and her mouth open and her arms slapping flat at the surface, and she went under again while I was still running.

I said, "No — no!"

I went in over the top of her. I remember the water being colder than it had any right to be in June and I remember the sting of it in my nose and I remember thrashing about for a second in the wrong place because of the oranges, because they were where my hands went, because they were the right size.

"Emma!"

I got a fistful of her dress. Then her arm. Then all of her, and she was so much heavier than she is, and she was not doing anything.

I got her up over the lip onto the stone and I turned her and there was water coming out of her and no sound coming with it, and I put my mouth over her mouth and I did what a man does when he has done a course once, eleven years ago, in a hotel conference room.

Somewhere behind me Vanessa was on the phone giving the address twice.

Emma coughed at me. Then she was sick, and then she cried, and I have never in my life heard a better sound than my daughter crying.

I had her against my chest with both arms round her and my face in her wet hair, and I was saying it over and over into the side of her head:

"I'm sorry, baby."

And then there was a hand on my shoulder.

Vanessa had come across the deck and gone down into a crouch behind me, close in, and she put one hand flat on my shoulder and left it there, and I let my head down onto Emma and I let her hold on to me, and I was grateful.

I want that written down. I was grateful.

I did not see her face. I was facing the water, and she was behind me, and my eyes were shut.

The camera on the wall had been taking one photograph every five seconds since four minutes past six that evening, and it went on taking them, all the way through it, and for an hour and a quarter after the ambulance left.

It was four months before anybody looked.

Synopsis

Michael Rhodes went inside to fetch a birthday cake and came back to a tipped-over sun lounger, nine halves of orange floating in the deep end, and his seven-year-old daughter face-down in two metres of water. His wife could not swim. His wife had screamed. His wife sat by the hospital bed for three nights running and brought him the right shirt. When Emma came home she said she had been kicked in — and she said it eleven times over four months, and never once changed a word of it, and a psychologist explained gently that children fill the holes that hypoxia leaves. Then in October Emma asked for her dead mother's camera, and Michael found it exactly where a seven-year-old had left it in June: on the low garden wall at the edge of the pool deck, aimed west at the sunset, set to take one frame every five seconds. There were one thousand one hundred and six photographs on the card. Frame 214 is a woman's bare foot against the leg of a child's chair. Frame 251 is what her face was doing three minutes later, while Michael knelt at the water with his daughter in his arms and her hand on his shoulder.

Main characters

Michael RhodesForty, a builder who inherited his grandfather's firm, who was inside fetching a cake for ninety seconds and spent the next four months feeding every crooked thing he noticed to his own guilt.
Emma RhodesSeven, who told her father the truth eleven times without changing a single word of it, taught herself to swim in secret at school so that nobody would tell her stepmother, and was right about all of it.
Vanessa RhodesThirty-four, a rehabilitation physiotherapist who came into the house to help a woman with a fractured pelvis, filled the pill organiser every Saturday, could not swim, and was liked by everybody who ever met her.
Sarah RhodesMichael's first wife, a county swimmer, found face-down at the deep end two years earlier after taking her sleeping tablets — an accident nobody questioned, in a pool she physically could not climb out of alone.
Gil RhodesMichael's younger brother and the trustee of his niece's inheritance, a hundred and nine thousand pounds down, who introduced the physiotherapist to the family and then spent two and a half years not saying anything.
Marta FerreiraEleven years cooking in that house, the only adult who asked the child a follow-up question instead of an apology, and who was let go in August for encouraging her.
Detective Sergeant Paloma VargasThe officer who looked at the photograph, said she believed him, and then told him it was not going to be enough.
