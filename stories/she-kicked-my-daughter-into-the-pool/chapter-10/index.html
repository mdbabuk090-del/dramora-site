She Kicked My Daughter Into the Pool While I Was Getting Her Birthday Cake

Chapter 10 — Sarah's Camera

Chapter 10 of 25

On the ninth of October, a Thursday, Emma had a project to do about the seasons and she needed a photograph of the sky.

She could have used my phone. Any child in the country would have used a phone. She asked for her mother's camera, and I have thought about whether she knew — whether some part of a seven-year-old had kept hold of the black shape on the garden wall all summer and was steering me toward it — and I have decided that she did not.

She wanted the camera because it had been her mother's and because in June she had been allowed to use it and it had been the best day of her year up to the point where it stopped being that.

She said, "Can I do it with Mum's one? The one that does the every-five-seconds."

Sarah bought that camera in the second year of us. It is a bridge camera, black, not a professional thing, with a long fixed lens and a hinged screen, and she used it perhaps four times a year and always for the same subject, which was the light going down over the cypresses at the end of the garden.

She had a theory about it — that the third week of June and the first week of December were the two weeks when that particular view did the thing she liked, and that everything in between was showing off. She had a folder on the computer with about four hundred sunsets in it, sorted by year.

There is an intervalometer built into it. You set it to a number of seconds and it stands there on its tripod and takes a photograph every that-many seconds until you stop it or the battery dies, and then you can run them together into about eleven seconds of film in which two hours of sky moves like weather in a dream.

Emma had watched her mother do it when she was three and four years old, sitting on the sandstone with a beaker.

She had done it herself twice that spring, with me, badly.

And on the fourteenth of June, at four minutes past six in the evening, my seven-year-old daughter carried her dead mother's camera out onto the pool deck, put it on the folding tripod on the low garden wall, aimed it west down the length of the water at a sunset that had not started yet, and set it to five seconds, because five was the number she could remember.

Then she went and knelt up on a sun lounger to wait for her father to come back with a cake.

I could not find it.

That is the part that makes me feel sick even now. On the Thursday evening I went to the shelf in the study where Sarah's things live — the ones I have not been able to move — and the camera was not there.

I looked for eleven minutes. I looked in the cupboard under the stairs and in the box in the garage with the tripods and in Emma's room and in the utility room, and then I stood in the middle of the kitchen with that particular hot feeling of having lost a dead person's possession, which is a very specific and very stupid kind of grief, and Emma said, from the table, without looking up:

"It's outside. On the wall."

I said, "It can't be."

She said, "It is. I put it there and then you were carrying me."

It was on the wall.

Four months. June, July, August, September, and nine days of October. Through eleven weeks of a west-facing summer and four thunderstorms and the whole of the second half of September when it rained for eight days.

It was standing exactly where a seven-year-old had put it, on a folding tripod the height of a dinner plate, on the low sandstone garden wall at the edge of the deck, six metres from the corner of the pool, with its lens pointing west and its cap off and a bloom of green-black on the rubber of the eyecup.

I want to say something about how a thing survives four months in plain sight.

Nobody in that house went out onto that deck. Emma would not go into the garden at all. I went out perhaps six times in four months, at dusk, on my own, and stood at the top of the steps with a drink and came back in. And Vanessa — who had drained the pool and refilled it in June, and who had bought a cover for it in the July, and who had taken over the pool contract and the gardener and everything else about that end of the property — Vanessa never once looked at the low wall behind her, because there was nothing on the low wall except a black object about the size of two fists that had been there so long it had become part of the wall.

I have been asked whether she knew it was there.

I do not think she knew. I think she saw it and did not see it, in exactly the same way I saw a fruit dish on the wrong side of a chair for four months and did not see it. There is no cleverness in it and no luck. It is just the ordinary fact that a human being stops registering anything that has not moved.

The one person in that household who never stopped registering it was the seven-year-old, because it was her mother's, and because she had put it there herself, and because she had spent four months not being allowed into the garden where it was.

I picked it up.

Second time. The first time was the fifteenth of June with a rabbit under my arm and I put it back down.

It was cold and slightly slimy on the underside and there was a woodlouse living in the tripod's folding mechanism, and the screen was black, and there was a fine haze of algae or pollen or four months of weather over the outer element of the lens.

The battery compartment had held. That is not remarkable — it is a sealed door with a rubber gasket, they are built for a rainy afternoon in a field. The card slot had held.

I took it into the kitchen and put it on the table and Emma stood beside me with both hands on the edge of the table and her chin nearly on it.

And here is a thing I have never been able to explain and have stopped trying to.

I did not put the card in the computer.

I put the camera on charge.

There was a card in that camera, and a card reader in the second drawer of the desk in the study eleven metres away, and any sane person, at any point in the next ninety minutes, would have taken the card out and put it in the reader and looked at the photographs on a screen the size of a window.

I plugged the camera into the wall by the bread bin and I said, "Give it an hour, monkey."

I have been asked about this by a barrister, by a detective, by my sister-in-law and by a woman I pay to sit opposite me on Tuesdays.

The honest answer is the one I gave the detective, and she did not laugh at it.

I said: "It was Sarah's. You don't take the card out of somebody's camera. You charge it."

Vanessa came in at twenty to eight with shopping.

The camera was on the counter by the bread bin with an orange light on it and its lens pointing at the ceiling and four months of green weather on the eyecup.

She put the bags down. She looked at it for slightly longer than you look at a thing.

Then she said, "Where's that come from?"

And my daughter, who was seven, and who had spent four months learning exactly what happens to information in that house, said, before I could open my mouth:

"The garage. It was in the garage box."

I looked at her.

She looked back at me with a face like a closed door, and then she said, to Vanessa, in the bright voice she used for that room: "I'm doing the sky for school."

Vanessa said, "Lovely," and started putting the shopping away.

We ate. Emma went up at half past eight. At twenty past nine I unplugged the camera and took it into the study and shut the door and sat down and turned it on, and the screen came up green and showed me the last photograph on the card, which was a photograph of an empty deck at twilight with nine halves of orange floating in the shallow end.

And the counter in the corner said 1106.
