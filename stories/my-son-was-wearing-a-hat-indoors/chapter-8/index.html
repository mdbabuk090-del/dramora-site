I Came Home After Eleven Months and My Son Was Wearing a Hat Indoors

Chapter 08 — What Eli Draws

Chapter 8 of 15

I went to the school on the Thursday and I did not warn them I was coming.

Mrs. Alvarez teaches fourth grade and she is about fifty and she has the eyes of a woman who has been ignored professionally for a long time. When the office told her Eli Vance's father was at the desk she came down the corridor fast, and she shook my hand with both of hers, and the first thing she said to me was:

"You came."

Not hello.

You came.

We sat in her classroom on chairs made for nine-year-olds. She got a folder out of her bottom drawer, and it was the kind of folder a person keeps because they have decided, at some point, that they are going to need it.

"I've called your house four times," she said.

"I've been overseas."

"I know. I called the number on file and I got his stepmother, and she was lovely, and she thanked me, and she said she'd talk to him." Mrs. Alvarez put the folder on the little table. "That was in April. I called again in September, and again in October, and the last time I asked for the father's contact and I was told you were unreachable in a war zone and it would only distress you."

I said, "There's a form. For deployed parents. There's an email that goes to a duty desk and it reaches me in about six hours."

"I didn't know that."

"Nobody does," I said. "Why would you."

She opened the folder.

There were nine drawings in it, in date order, and a photocopy of a page from an exercise book, and a single sheet of lined paper covered in her own handwriting with dates down the left margin.

"He's a lovely artist," she said. "That's the thing nobody says about him. He's the best in this class by a distance."

The first drawing was from October last year, before I left. It was our house, drawn the way kids draw houses, and there were three people outside it and a dog we don't have.

"And this is the same house," she said, "in May."

Same house. Same crayons.

The people were inside it now. Small figures behind windows, one to a room.

And every window had lines across it.

"He does bars," she said quietly. "Not always. Not every time. But when he's drawing home, he does bars. He did it four times between March and June and I've got three of them here."

I looked at those windows for a long time.

"He'd tell you they're blinds," she said. "I asked. He said, 'They're blinds, Miss.' And he's a very good, very careful boy who has learned to have an answer ready."

She turned the page.

The next one was different. It wasn't the house. It was a small dark rectangle with a wobbly line down one side, and in the bottom corner, a shape that might have been a person sitting with their knees up. Nothing else. No sky, no ground, no colour except grey and black.

"He drew that in November," she said. "Free choice, Friday afternoon. Took him the whole session. Wouldn't say what it was."

A cupboard.

A cupboard with a door and a line of light down the edge of it.

I said, "What did you do?"

"I made my third phone call," said Mrs. Alvarez. "And then I did the thing they tell us to do, which is a written concern to the district safeguarding officer, and I did that on the ninth of November, and I have the reference number."

She turned the lined sheet round so I could read it.

Nine entries in her handwriting. Dates, times, who she called, what she was told.

The last one said:

Nov 21 — Contacted DSO for update. Told case closed at intake. Reason given: family already known to services, allegation unsubstantiated, mother has engaged fully. No further action.

I read that line about as many times as I'd read the word stairs.

"Mrs. Alvarez," I said. "Eli's mother died three years ago."

She looked at me.

She looked at me for a long moment, and I watched a woman realise that she had been talking to somebody about a mother for eight months, and had never once said the word out loud in a way that would have made anybody check.

"She always said 'we,'" Mrs. Alvarez said. "She always said 'we'd rather', 'we've decided.' She came to every parents' evening. She brought cakes at Christmas. She signed the forms." She put her hand flat on the folder. "She signs them 'D. Vance.' I never — it never occurred to me to ask. Why would it occur to me to ask?"

"It wouldn't," I said.

"I have been calling a woman I believed was his mother," she said, "and telling her, in detail, every single thing her son had told me."

Neither of us said anything for a while. Somewhere down the corridor a bell went and a hundred kids started moving.

Then she said, "Mr. Vance. Can I show you one more."

She took the photocopy out from underneath.

"It's from his English book. It's a comprehension exercise, they had to finish sentences. Most of them wrote about football."

She put it in front of me.

The prompt was printed on the left. The safest place I know is...

And in a nine-year-old's careful handwriting, in pencil, with the letters going slightly downhill the way his always do:

the truck, becase you can lock it from insid and nobody has the key exsept my dad.

I sat in a chair made for a child, in a room with a fish tank and a number line on the wall, and I put my hand over my mouth, and Mrs. Alvarez got up and went and stood by the window with her back to me so I could have thirty seconds.

Then I asked her for copies of everything.

She said, "I made you a set in April."

She had. It was in the folder, already paper-clipped, with a compliments slip on the front and my name on it, dated the twenty-second of April, waiting for a man who never came.
