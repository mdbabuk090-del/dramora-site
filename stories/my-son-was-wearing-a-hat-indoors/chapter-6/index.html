I Came Home After Eleven Months and My Son Was Wearing a Hat Indoors

Chapter 06 — Mrs. Pike Would Like A Word

Chapter 6 of 15

She came to me. That's the thing about people who are good at this — they never wait to be asked.

Sunday, late morning, Darla out at the gym she pays for from my son's college fund. Eli upstairs. And Roberta Pike came out to the garage where I was doing nothing useful to a lawnmower, with two glasses of iced tea, and she stood in the doorway with the light behind her and said:

"I think you and I should talk about the boy."

I put the wrench down.

"Sure."

She handed me a glass. She sat on the arm of the old sofa I've never got rid of, and she arranged herself, and she took a breath the way people do when they've practised a thing in the car.

"I want to say first that I love that child."

"Okay."

"And I want to say second that your wife is exhausted."

There it is, I thought. Here comes the shape of it.

And then, for about ten minutes, Roberta Pike told me a story that was — and I want to be careful here, because this is the part people don't believe — about eighty percent true.

She said Eli had got difficult after I left. That was true. She said he'd stopped eating properly, that he'd wet the bed twice at seven years old and been mortified, that he'd started lying about small things — homework, teeth, where he'd been. That was true too, or true enough that I couldn't call it.

She said Darla had never had a child and had married into one, and that a nine-year-old boy who has lost his mother and then loses his father to a deployment is not a project, he's a storm, and that Darla had been standing in it alone for eleven months.

She said, "You have no idea what she's carried."

And I said, "Tell me," and I meant it, because I wanted the shape of the story they were going to run.

So she told me. And the more she told me the better she got, and about eight minutes in she made her mistake.

She said: "There was a night in July she called me at two in the morning crying, and I got in the car, and I drove ninety minutes, and I sat with her on that kitchen floor until it got light. That's what she's been dealing with. So when you come home and start —"

She stopped.

She stopped because I had not moved and had not spoken, and I think in that pause she heard the sentence back.

I drove ninety minutes.

"From where?" I said.

"What?"

"Ninety minutes from where, Roberta?"

And there was a small silence in that garage that I could have stood a ladder in.

Because Roberta Pike has been living in my house since March. She told me so herself, at my own stove, with a butter knife in her hand — since I could be useful. Her keys are in the dish by the door. Her car is parked in front of my truck. She knows where the pans live.

In July she was, by her own account, ninety minutes away, in the middle of the night, and had to get in a car.

Both things cannot be true.

"Oh, I still had the condo then," she said smoothly. "I gave it up in August."

"I thought you said March."

"I said on and off, sweetheart."

And she smiled at me and drank her tea, and I smiled back and said something about the lawnmower, and that was that.

Except it wasn't, because she was rattled — and rattled people do the most useful thing in the world. They come back to it.

Twenty minutes later, when I'd carried the glasses in and she was cutting up a melon, she said, apropos of nothing at all:

"You know he does hurt himself, sometimes. Deliberately."

I kept my back to her.

"Does he."

"Bangs his head. When he's in a state." Chop. Chop. "I've seen him do it. Against the door frame. It's a thing they do at that age, apparently, I looked it up. Attention."

"Huh."

"I only say it because —" and here she put the knife down and got a little careful — "because if anybody ever asked, and somebody might, with a boy his age and all these bumps, I'd want you to know it isn't a mystery. He does it to himself."

That is the moment I understood exactly who I was dealing with.

Because I had not accused anybody of anything. Not once. Not in nine days. I had been the most pleasant man in America.

And she had just walked into my kitchen and pre-loaded the defence.

You don't build a defence for a thing nobody has charged you with. You build it because you have already had the conversation — in your own head, at four in the morning, over and over — about the day somebody finally asks.

I said, "That's good to know."

And I went upstairs and wrote it down word for word while I still had it exact, and then I sat on the edge of my bed and looked at the two entries under each other:

Darla: back steps, planter. Hospital: stairs. Roberta: he does it to himself.

Three people. Three mechanisms. One wound.

They weren't covering for each other.

They were each covering for themselves.
