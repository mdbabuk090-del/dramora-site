I Came Home After Eleven Months and My Son Was Wearing a Hat Indoors

Chapter 04 — Four Hundred And Twelve Dollars

Chapter 4 of 15

I hadn't looked at the money in eleven months.

That sounds insane written down. It sounded insane to me, sitting in the truck on the Friday with the app open and my thumb over it. But that is how it works out there, and any man who's been on a long deployment will tell you the same: you set the transfers up before you fly, you check the first two, and then you stop. Not because you don't care. Because there's nothing you can do about it from where you are, and thinking about home is how men get hurt.

I sent it and I didn't watch it land. That was the deal I made with myself in February and I kept it for three hundred and thirty-one days.

There were two accounts.

The first was the household account. My pay went into it, plus deployment allowance, plus the hazard supplement that started in April. Darla had a card on it. She was supposed to have a card on it — she was running a house and a child, and I'd have been a fool to leave her without one.

The second account was not mine.

Nora set it up eight months before she died, when she still had good days and was using them to be practical in a way that I could not stand to be in the room for. The life insurance went into it. It is in Eli's name. I am the custodian, which means I am allowed to spend it on him, for school, for medical, for the things a kid needs — and I am not allowed to spend it on anything else, and there is a form, and there is a bank, and there is a paper trail.

She wrote a note on the day she opened it and put it in the folder with the paperwork, and I have not read it since the week of the funeral because I am a coward about exactly one thing in this world.

For his eighteen. Don't let anybody talk you into anything.

I opened the household account first.

It was fine. That's the thing nobody expects me to say. It was fine. Money in, money out, groceries and utilities and gas and a lot of takeaway. The balance was low, lower than it should have been, but there were four figures in it and nothing screamed.

I want to be fair, even now. If I had only opened that one, I'd have closed the app and gone in and made dinner.

Then I opened Eli's.

Four hundred and twelve dollars.

I looked at it for a long time. Long enough that the screen went dark and I had to bring it back with my thumb, and it still said four hundred and twelve dollars, and it still said the account number I have had memorised since the week we buried her.

I went into the transaction history and I scrolled to the bottom, to February, to the day I flew.

Then I scrolled up, slowly, and read eleven months of my life going out of my son's account four and five thousand dollars at a time.

Cabinetry. Flooring. Two payments to a firm in the city with a name like a law practice that turned out to be a kitchen design studio. An SUV — not the whole thing, the deposit, nine thousand six hundred dollars in June, and then a monthly after that, every month, the same day. A jeweller in April. A jeweller again in August. Something at a dental practice for four and a half thousand that was not any dentist Eli has ever been to.

And in among it, small and regular and somehow worse than all the rest of it put together: a standing transfer of six hundred dollars a month, from my son's account, to an account ending in the same four digits as the one Roberta Pike had written on the back of a Christmas card two years ago, when she sent us a photograph of herself in Florida.

Rent, I suppose. Somebody had decided she was owed rent.

I sat in my truck on my own drive with the phone in my hand.

I'd like to tell you I did something. What I actually did was open the door and be sick on my own gravel, and then sit back down and wipe my mouth on the back of my wrist and stare at the fence.

Because here's what it meant, and it took me about ninety seconds to walk all the way to the end of it.

To move money out of that account you need the custodian. You need my signature or my login, and my login is my thumbprint and a code that goes to a phone that was in a pocket on the other side of the world.

Unless somebody had the folder.

The folder lives in the drawer of the desk in the room at the end of the hall. Nora's room. The room I have not been able to make myself empty in three years, where her handwriting is on things and her cardigan is still over the back of the chair, and which everybody in this family knows I do not open and do not talk about.

I got out of the truck and I went in through the front door and down the hall, past the kitchen where Roberta was telling Darla something about a restaurant, and I went to the end of the hall to the door of my dead wife's room.

There was a new lock on it.

Not a bolt. A proper brass keyed handle, clean and bright, screwed into wood that had been chiselled out to take it, with the little curls of pale sawdust still caught in the carpet against the skirting board where somebody had swept fast and not well.

I stood there with my hand not quite touching it.

Behind me, down the hall, my wife laughed at something her mother said.

And I understood that whatever was on the other side of that door, they had not locked it to keep Eli out.

They had locked it to keep me out — and they had done it recently, and they had done it since Wednesday, because on Wednesday I had walked this hall at two in the afternoon carrying a screwdriver and there had been no lock on that door at all.

Which meant one of them had watched me walk past it.

And decided it was time.
