I Came Home After Eleven Months and My Son Was Wearing a Hat Indoors

Chapter 02 — Good To Be Home

Chapter 2 of 15

I was up at five, because I am always up at five, and because I had not slept.

I made coffee in a machine I did not recognise. It had a screen. There was a little brushed-steel lever for milk foam and a light that pulsed while it warmed up, and I stood in my own kitchen at five in the morning working out how to make a cup of coffee in my own house.

The wine was gone off the floor. Somebody had cleaned it after I went to bed. I got down on one knee with my phone torch and went along the seam where the boards met the island, and I found what I expected to find: two slivers of glass and a thin dark line in the grain where red had gone in and would not come out. Somebody had wiped it fast, in the dark, wanting it gone.

That told me one thing and I wrote it down later in the notebook I started that week: she cleans up at night. Not in the morning, when you'd have the light. At night, when you want a thing not to exist by breakfast.

Darla came down at seven.

I want to describe this properly, because it is the hinge the whole year turns on.

She stopped in the doorway when she saw me. Just for a second. She had done her hair and she was wearing the robe, and she stood there and looked at me and I watched her decide something.

Then she smiled and said, "You made coffee."

And I said, "There was a machine. I fought it. I won."

She laughed.

God help me, I made my wife laugh at seven in the morning, eight hours after I took a hat off my son's head, and the thing that frightens me still — the thing I have not told anybody until now — is how easy it was. Not hard. Easy. I have talked a nineteen-year-old through a tourniquet on his own leg in a voice like I was reading him a weather report. You learn to put your face somewhere else and leave it there.

She poured herself a cup. She stood on the other side of the island, and behind her the sun was coming up over the fence, and she said, "About last night."

Here it is, I thought. Here's where she tells me.

"I should have called you," she said. "It happened so fast and by the time I'd have got hold of you it was already dealt with, and I thought — I honestly thought it would be worse for you, out there, knowing and not being able to do anything."

That is a very good sentence.

I have thought about that sentence more than almost anything else in this story. It is kind. It puts her on my side. It makes her silence into a gift she gave me. And it is built so that if I push on it, I'm the one being unreasonable — because what was she supposed to do, ruin my deployment?

"Yeah," I said. "That's probably right."

Her shoulders came down about an inch.

"Where did he fall?"

I asked it easy, into my coffee, not looking up. This is the part I'd been rehearsing since four in the morning. Not did he fall. Not who did this. Just a bored husband filling in a detail.

"The steps," she said. "Out back."

"Ah."

"He was coming down too fast, you know how he does."

"He does do that."

"And he caught his head on the corner of the planter."

I nodded slowly, like a man being told about weather in a country he doesn't live in, and I drank my coffee, and inside my chest something went very cold and very quiet and lay down.

Because there is no planter by the back steps.

There were three steps and a strip of gravel and a hose reel bolted to the wall, and that is what was there when I left in January, and it is what I had walked past at six that morning with my coffee, because I always walk the perimeter, because eleven months of doing it makes it a thing your legs do without asking.

She could have said the steps. She could have said the corner of the door, the edge of the counter, the bath. Any of it would have held.

She invented an object.

People who are telling the truth about an accident do not invent objects. They say he fell, I don't know, he was just on the floor screaming — because that's what the truth is like, it's got holes in it. Lies are the ones that come with furniture.

"Poor kid," I said.

"He was very brave," she said.

And then I heard the front door.

Roberta Pike came in the way you come into your own house — no knock, keys already going down in the dish by the door, calling out something about the traffic on Hillcrest. She had a coat on that I would later learn cost more than my first car. She was fifty-five if she was a day and she looked well on it.

She saw me and her whole face opened up.

"*Marcus.*" She came round the island with both arms out. "Look at you. Look at this. Darla, why didn't you say —"

"He surprised us," Darla said.

"Well." Roberta got hold of both my shoulders and looked me up and down like a woman inspecting a horse she had already bought. "You're too thin. Isn't he too thin. Sit down, I'll do you eggs."

She knew where the pans were.

That's the detail. That's the one. She reached into the cupboard under the island, second door along, without looking, the way you reach for something in your own kitchen, and she came out with the good skillet — the heavy one, Nora's, the one that lived at the back — and she put it on the burner and turned the gas on and got the butter out of the door of the fridge, and she did all of it while talking about the traffic on Hillcrest.

Nobody visits a house often enough to learn where the back of a cupboard is.

"How long have you been staying, Roberta?" I said.

"Oh —" she waved the butter knife. "On and off."

"On and off since when?"

"Since March," she said warmly, and then heard herself give me a month, and put the month back in its box. "On and off. Since I could be useful. Since this one" — the knife at Darla — "was doing it all on her own with a house this size and a boy that age and you off God knows where. Somebody had to."

Which is a beautiful answer. It is also, for about a second and a half, a date, and I kept it.

I said thank you. I said it about four times. I ate the eggs and they were good, and I said that too. And under the table my hands were flat on my knees and I was counting things.

The coat. The dish by the door with two sets of keys in it. The dark blue SUV on the drive that hadn't been there in January, sitting in front of my truck so that my truck couldn't get out. The gold chain on Roberta's neck, chunky, new, the kind of thing that comes in a box with a ribbon. The way Darla's eyes went to her mother before she answered me about the steps.

Eli came down at ten past eight.

He had the hat on.

He came into the kitchen and there were three adults in it and he went along the wall — not through the middle, along the wall, past the pantry, the long way round to the fridge — and he took out the milk and he stood there holding it and he looked at Roberta.

Waiting.

For permission. To pour milk. In his own house, on a Saturday, at eight in the morning, with his father sitting right there.

"Go on then," Roberta said.

And he poured it, fast, up to about a third of the glass, and put it back, and drank it standing up by the counter in about four seconds flat, and rinsed the glass and put it in the machine, and all of it took less than a minute and he never once sat down.

"Morning," I said.

He looked at me.

There was a thing in that look I couldn't read yet. Not fear exactly. Something further along than fear. Fear is what you have when you don't know what's coming.

"Morning, Dad," he said.

"You want to come to the hardware store with me?"

The room got very slightly still.

"He's got homework," Darla said.

"On a Saturday?"

"He's behind," Roberta said. "Aren't you, sweetheart. He's got that project."

And Eli said, "I've got that project," and he did not look at me while he said it.

He went back upstairs with the hat still on.

I sat in that kitchen for another twenty minutes and I let them talk over me about a bathroom they were thinking of doing, and I said mm, and I said sounds good, and I said whatever you think.

Then I went out to the truck to check the oil, which is what I told them I was doing, and I sat in the driver's seat with the door shut and the engine off, and I got out my phone and I did the first useful thing I had done since I got home.

I opened the notes app, and I typed:

1. No planter by the back steps. 2. R. knows where the pans live. 3. He asked permission to pour milk. 4. He didn't want to come to the hardware store.

And then I sat there for a while looking at number four, because the first three made me angry and the fourth one made me want to be sick.

My son used to hide in the truck bed so I couldn't leave without him.
