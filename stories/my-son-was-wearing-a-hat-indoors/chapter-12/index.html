I Came Home After Eleven Months and My Son Was Wearing a Hat Indoors

Chapter 12 — Two Women, One Story, Two Versions

Chapter 12 of 15

I did not play the recording to anybody in that house.

That is the single most important decision in this story and I want to give it its own line, because every instinct I have was screaming to walk into that kitchen the next morning and put a phone on the counter and press play and watch their faces.

If I had, here is what would have happened: they would have had eleven months and a good lawyer to build one story out of two. By the time anybody official sat them down they'd have agreed it was an accident, both of them, same words, same door frame.

The value of that recording was not in what it proved.

It was that they didn't know it existed.

I emailed it to Priya at 11:40 that night and I went to bed in my own house four feet from a woman who had put my son into a door frame, and I slept for about ninety minutes, and in the morning I made everybody eggs.

Priya listened to it twice and called me at eight the next evening.

She said: "Where were you standing?"

"By the sink."

"Did either of them know it was running?"

"No."

"Did you speak?"

"Once. Four words."

There was a pause and I could hear her writing.

Then she said, "Mr. Vance, I want to be careful with you, because I've had this conversation with a lot of people and it goes wrong when they hear what they want. So: this is not a conviction. This is not custody. This is an eleven-minute domestic argument in which two people say a number of things that are extremely difficult to explain away."

"Which one."

"Two things," she said. "The first is your mother-in-law saying you had his arm and you didn't let go. That's an eyewitness account of the mechanism, given spontaneously, against her own interest, and she said it in front of a third party. That is the single strongest piece of evidence anybody has generated in this case, and your wife's mother generated it for free."

"And the second?"

"The second is your wife saying it's your rent," said Priya. "Because that's not about the boy. That's two people who both know that money moved and who each think the other one is holding the bag. And that's not family court. That's the part that makes a police fraud unit sit up, and it's the part where the two of them stop defending each other permanently."

She let that sit for a second.

"Once they've each got a lawyer telling them the other one is the problem, they will do more damage to each other in six weeks than you could do in six years."

That is exactly what happened.

I filed on the Monday. Emergency ex parte order, temporary sole custody, no contact between Eli and either of them pending a hearing. Priya walked it in herself at nine in the morning with the folder from Mrs. Alvarez and the discharge summary and the audio and a two-page affidavit from me that took us four hours to write because she made me take out every single adjective.

"No feelings," she kept saying. "Feelings are for the trial. Judges grant orders on facts."

We were granted the same day.

Nobody tells you what that afternoon feels like. There's no scene. There's a woman handing you two sheets of paper in a corridor that smells like floor polish, and then you're standing in a car park at three in the afternoon on a Monday holding a piece of paper that says your son sleeps where you sleep.

I picked him up from school myself. He got in the truck and put his bag between his feet and said, "Are we going home?"

And I said, "No, bud. We're going to a hotel with a pool and then we're going to Grandpa Ray's for a while."

And Eli said, "Okay."

And then — a mile down the road, out of nowhere, looking straight ahead:

"Is Darla coming?"

"No."

He didn't say anything for a bit.

Then he said, "For how long?"

And I said, "Ever."

My son put his hand flat on the window and looked out of it for eleven miles and did not say another word, and when we got there he was asleep, and I carried him in, and he was so much lighter than he should have been at nine years old that I sat on the end of that hotel bed and put my hands over my face for a long time.

Here is what the six weeks after that looked like, and it is the least dramatic chapter of this story and the one I'd most want another man in my position to read.

They separated within nine days. Roberta moved to a motel on Route 9 and then to her sister's. She has not, as far as I know, spoken to her daughter since the second week of December.

Roberta got a lawyer first, and her lawyer did exactly what Priya said he would do: he came to the county with a full account, on the record, in exchange for consideration on the financial side. Everything she said in my kitchen, she said again on paper, with dates, and she added eleven months of things I didn't know about and I'm not going to write down.

Darla's story changed three times. First it was the stairs. Then it was that Eli had been out of control and she'd had to restrain him and he'd hurt himself. Then, when her lawyer had seen the bank trail, it was that her mother had pressured her, and that she had been financially controlled, and that she had done what she was told.

Three versions. Same as it had always been. The problem with a family that never agreed on a lie is that it never learns how to keep one.

The money took the longest and was the ugliest. Two hundred and thirty-one thousand dollars had gone out of my son's account in eleven months. The bank's own investigators found that the second page of the custodian agreement — the one with my signature — had been scanned and reused nine times.

That is a felony, and it has a name, and it does not have anything to do with family court at all.

And the safeguarding referral, the one Diane made at one in the morning that went nowhere: it turned out somebody had answered the county's follow-up call in April, and identified herself as the child's mother, and declined an in-person visit, and given a full and reassuring account.

There is a recording of it, because they record everything.

It is Darla's voice.
