I Came Home After Eleven Months and My Son Was Wearing a Hat Indoors

Chapter 03 — The Boy Who Doesn't Flinch Anymore

Chapter 3 of 15

Here is what I did for the next six days: nothing.

I want to be honest about how that felt, because I know how it reads. It reads like a man being careful. It felt like standing in a burning house arguing with yourself about which door to use.

Every hour of it I had the same three sentences going in my head. Put him in the truck. Drive. Don't come back. And every hour I answered them the same way, with the only argument that ever won: if I take him out of here on a Saturday with no evidence and a bag of clothes, then on Monday I am a soldier who abducted a child during a custody dispute, and she is a frightened stepmother who called the police, and she has been at every school pickup for fourteen months and I have been on another continent.

They would give him back to her.

They would give him back to her, and then she would know that I knew.

That's the whole thing. That's the axis everything turned on. She could hurt him for another month while I built a case. Or she could hurt him for the rest of his childhood after I lost one.

So I did nothing, and I watched, and I have never in my life worked so hard at nothing.

You learn a house by walking it at different hours. I had eleven months of leave stacked up and nobody to tell me what to do with it, so I got up at five and I fixed things. That was my cover and it was a good one, because it was also true: the gate was dragging, the tap in the utility room dripped, there was a screen door with a hinge gone. A man home from deployment with a drill in his hand can be anywhere in his own house for eight hours a day and nobody thinks a thing about it.

This is what I found.

He eats standing up. Every meal, unless he's told to sit. At the counter, near the door, with his back not quite to the room. I watched him do it eleven times before I understood I was looking at a man's habit, not a boy's — the way you eat when you might have to move.

He knows the blind spots. There is a place at the end of the upstairs hall, between the linen cupboard and the corner, where you cannot be seen from the top of the stairs or from either bedroom door. He goes there. He took a book there twice while I was on the landing with a screwdriver, and he didn't know I was watching, and he tucked himself into that corner with the ease of somebody going to a place he goes a lot.

Kids have hiding places. I had one. Mine was under the porch and I went there to read comics and be a spy.

You don't sit in yours reading a school library book at four in the afternoon on a Tuesday.

He is silent on the stairs. All of them. There are two that creak, third from the bottom and the one at the turn, and he does not put weight on either of them, ever, going up or down, and he does it without thinking about it, and when I tested it — when I stood in the hall and listened for him coming down — I got nothing at all until he was in the doorway.

He is nine years old. It took me until Thursday to work out what that skill is for.

He doesn't ask for things. Not water, not food, not the television, not to go outside. Six days. He asked for exactly one thing in six days and it was permission to pour milk on the Saturday, and he asked it of Roberta with his eyes and not his mouth.

And he doesn't flinch at Darla.

That one took me longest, because I was looking for the wrong thing. I was watching for him to shrink when she came into a room. He doesn't. He goes smooth. His face does a thing that I can only describe as switching off — everything in it goes level and pleasant and slightly absent, and he answers her in complete sentences, and he says please and thank you, and if you walked in cold you would say what a lovely well-mannered boy.

He flinched at my hand on his head, in the dark, with wool in between.

He does not flinch at her at all.

I sat with that on the Wednesday night out in the truck, and I finally got hold of what it meant, and it was worse than anything I'd worked out so far.

Flinching is what you do when a thing is still a surprise.

He has stopped being surprised.

The other thing I did that week was talk to my son, and I want to put down exactly how badly that went, because I think there are men reading this who are going to be where I was, and I'd like to save them a day or two.

Tuesday afternoon. He was on the back step with a stick, scraping the dirt out of the gravel, in the hat. Eighty-one degrees. I sat down next to him with two cans of soda and I did not give him one, I put both on the step between us so he could take one if he wanted, and he did, after a while.

I said, "That's a mean-looking cut you've got."

Nothing.

I said, "I got one like it when I was about your age. Off a wheelbarrow. Six stitches. My dad was so mad."

He turned the can around in his hands.

I said, "You want to tell me how it happened?"

And my son said, "I fell."

Two words. Flat. Not scared. Not defiant. The way you give a password.

I said, "Where?"

And there it was — the smallest hitch in the world, about a third of a second, his thumb stopping on the tab of the can.

"The steps," he said.

"These steps?"

"Yeah."

We were sitting on them. There was gravel at the bottom and a hose reel on the wall and no planter and no corner of anything, and my son was looking straight at the ground where he had supposedly opened his own head, and he said yeah.

I nearly did it there. I nearly said, Eli, there's nothing here to hit, and I got as far as opening my mouth.

And then I thought: if I break his story on the back step, he tells her tonight that Dad doesn't believe him. Not because he wants to. Because he's nine, and because she has spent fourteen months making telling her things the safest thing he can do.

So I closed my mouth, and I said, "Rough luck," and we drank the soda.

And after a while, without looking at me, he said one more thing.

He said: "Are you staying?"

I said, "Yeah, bud. I'm home for good."

And he said, "Okay," and he got up, and he went inside.

Not good. Not really? Not the thing a kid says when the answer is what he wanted.

Okay. The way you take a piece of information you are going to have to work out how to survive.

I sat on that step for a long time after he went in. The sun went down over the fence I built with his mother. And I understood, finally, that I had been asking the wrong question all week.

I'd been asking what happened to my son's head.

The question was how long it had been going on that a nine-year-old had learned to walk on the two stairs that don't creak.
