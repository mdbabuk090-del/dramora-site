I Came Home After Eleven Months and My Son Was Wearing a Hat Indoors

Chapter 05 — The Locked Room

Chapter 5 of 15

I didn't touch that door for two days.

Everything in me wanted to take it off its hinges. I had a drill in my hand at the time — that's the part that still gets me. Eight minutes and a brass handle in the bin and I'd have been standing in her room.

And then what.

Then Darla comes home and there's a broken door and a man going through drawers, and every single thing I find after that is something I found after breaking in. She cries. She calls somebody. And the first question anybody official asks is not what was in the room, it's why did your husband force the door.

I'd learned that much in Chapter Two of my own life, standing on a back step with a soda in my hand.

So I did it the boring way.

The boring way took four days and cost me eleven dollars.

Day one. I looked at the lock properly. Brass, keyed both sides — which is the thing you notice if you notice anything, because a keyed handle on the inside of a bedroom door is not a privacy lock, it's a storage lock. You don't fit that to keep somebody in. You fit it to keep a room shut against the people who live in the house.

The screws were Phillips, brand new, not a mark on the heads. The chisel work was rough. Somebody had done it themselves with a hand chisel and been in a hurry, and there were three small dents in the paint above the plate where a hammer had missed.

Darla has never held a chisel in her life.

Day two. I went to the hardware store on Route 9 — the one Eli didn't want to come to — and I stood in the aisle and found the lock. Same brand, same finish, thirty-one dollars, and a little sticker on the shelf edge saying the exact model.

I bought a bag of washers I didn't need so I'd have a receipt, and on the way to the register I asked the kid on the desk, easy as anything, whether they'd sold many of those brass ones lately.

He said they sold a few a week.

Then he said, "Somebody bought two of those and a chisel set about a week and a half back, I remember because she couldn't decide about the finish."

I said, "Blonde? Little?"

And he said, "No. Older lady. Blue Nissan."

Roberta drives a blue Nissan.

Two locks.

I stood in that car park for a while thinking about the word two.

Day three. I found the other one.

It took me the whole afternoon, and I found it by doing something stupid — I walked the house with my eyes at the height of a nine-year-old. Down on my haunches, going room to room like a man looking for a dropped screw. And in the utility room, on the door of the tall cupboard where the water heater is, at exactly the height where a small hand would grab it, there was a brass keyed handle.

The utility cupboard. Where the heater is. Where there is no window and no light switch on the inside and about four feet of floor.

I put my hand on it and it was locked, and I stood there in my own utility room for a long, long time, and I am not going to write down what I thought about doing to two women that afternoon, because I did not do it, and because a man should only be judged on the second thing.

Day four. I got in.

Not the utility cupboard — that one I left exactly as it was, because it was evidence and because I already knew what was in it, which was nothing, which was the entire point of it.

Nora's room.

Here is how, and it is so stupid that I'm almost embarrassed: the lock was new but the door was thirty years old, and I hung that door myself in the summer Eli was born, and I hung it badly. It has never sat right in the frame. There's a gap at the top by the hinge side, and if you lift the handle while you push, the whole thing rides up about a quarter inch.

A lock holds a bolt in a plate. It doesn't hold a door that isn't square.

I got a plastic ruler from Eli's homework pile and a wedge of cardboard, and I lifted, and I pushed, and the latch came out of the strike plate on its own, and the door of my dead wife's room swung open.

The smell got me first. Still her. Three years and still her.

Nothing had been taken.

That's what I want you to understand about that afternoon, because I had spent two days building myself up to find the room stripped. Her cardigan was over the back of the chair. Her books were on the shelf in the order she left them. The bed was made the way she made beds, tight, hospital corners, a thing she learned from her father and never stopped doing.

The room was exactly as it had been.

Except the desk drawer was open half an inch, and it had never in three years been open at all.

The folder was still in there. Bank paperwork, the custodian forms, the insurance letter, all of it in her handwriting on the tab: ELI — 18.

I went through it page by page on the floor with my back against her bed.

Two things were gone.

The first was the second page of the custodian agreement — the one with my signature specimen on it, the page a bank will accept a scan of, and only that page, so that the folder would still look full if a man in a hurry flipped it open.

The second was the note.

For his eighteen. Don't let anybody talk you into anything.

Three years I hadn't been able to read it. Somebody else had.

And underneath where it had been, at the bottom of the folder, was something that did not belong to my wife.

A hospital discharge summary, folded in three, dated eleven days before I came home.

I opened it.

PATIENT: Vance, Eli J. — age 9 PRESENTING: Laceration, left frontoparietal scalp. Four sutures. MECHANISM (per accompanying adult): Fell down stairs at home.

I read that line about six times.

Stairs.

Darla had told me the back steps and the corner of a planter. She'd told me that in her own kitchen, at seven in the morning, with the sun coming up, and she had said it easily.

Whoever took my son to that hospital said stairs.

They are not the same lie. And that is the single most useful thing I have ever been handed in my life, because two people who have agreed on a story do not tell two versions of it —

which meant they had never agreed on one.

Which meant they had never once, in eleven days, sat down together and worked out what to say to me.

Which meant that each of them believed the other one had it covered.

I put the folder back. I closed the drawer to exactly half an inch. I lifted the door on the way out and let the latch drop into the plate, and I wiped the handle with my shirt, and I went and made dinner for my wife and her mother and my son, and I was pleasant, and I asked Roberta about Florida.

That night I started a second notebook.

On the first page I wrote:

Two locks bought. One on Nora's door. One on the utility cupboard.

Darla says: back steps, planter. Hospital says: stairs.

They have not talked to each other.

Get them talking.
