I Came Home After Eleven Months and My Son Was Wearing a Hat Indoors

Chapter 10 — Let Them Believe I Know Nothing

Chapter 10 of 15

I had a nurse who couldn't testify, a teacher with a folder, a bank statement, a discharge summary that said stairs, and nineteen seconds of audio through a front door.

My lawyer, a woman called Priya who had been doing this for nineteen years and who did not once in our first meeting tell me it would be all right, laid it out on the table and looked at it and said:

"This is good. This is not enough."

I said, "Why."

She said, "Because every single piece of it has an answer. The bank statements — she'll say you authorised it, and you signed a joint household arrangement, and it'll take eight months to unpick. The teacher — hearsay, and a drawing of a cupboard is a drawing of a cupboard. The audio — that's a woman's voice through a door saying four words, and any lawyer alive will tell a court that she was telling a child not to come out of his room during a tantrum." She tapped the folder. "The hospital record works against you. It's the only contemporaneous document, and it says he fell."

"So what do I need."

"One of two things," said Priya. "Either a physical finding that cannot happen accidentally — and for that you need a paediatrician, not an ER at eleven at night — or you need them to contradict each other in a way that's recorded, with a date on it, in front of somebody who isn't you."

She capped her pen.

"You've told me they've never agreed on a story."

"They haven't."

"Then that's the door," she said. "Don't break the family. Just stop holding it together."

I want to explain what she meant, because it took me a night to see it and it's the smartest thing anybody said to me in that entire year.

A house like that runs on one thing: the two of them never having to compare notes. Roberta thinks the story is he does it to himself. Darla thinks the story is the back steps. The hospital has stairs. Nobody has ever had to line them up, because there has never been anybody in that house who asked the same question twice.

I was that person now.

And the trick — Priya's trick — was that I must never ask either of them a question that made them go and check with the other one.

I had to ask each of them things that made them sure.

So this is what I did for eleven days, and I want to say plainly that it is the coldest thing I have ever done to two human beings and I would do it again tomorrow.

With Roberta, I was a man slowly, reluctantly coming round to her point of view. I let her tell me about the head-banging again. I asked her, sadly, whether she thought he needed to see somebody. I let her be right. And on the fourth day I said, quietly, over the sink: "Don't tell Darla I asked you this. She gets defensive about him and I don't want a fight."

Roberta loved that. She put her hand on my arm.

I had just made her my ally against my wife.

With Darla, I did the exact opposite. I was a husband who found his mother-in-law exhausting. I sighed about the Nissan blocking my truck. I said, on the Sunday, that I thought Roberta talked to Eli in a way I didn't love, and Darla said — fast, before she could stop herself — "I've said that."

I've said that.

I let it go straight past. I didn't grab it. I said, "Anyway," and I changed the subject, and I watched her sit with the fact that she had just criticised her mother out loud to me.

I had just made her my ally against Roberta.

Neither of them told the other. That's the whole of it. Each one thought she had a private understanding with me. Each one had a reason not to compare.

And with Eli, I did the only honest thing in those eleven days.

I did not ask him a single question about his head.

Instead, on the Wednesday, I took him out to the truck, and I gave him the spare key on a bit of string, and I told him it was his.

He turned it over in his hand.

I said, "That's yours now. You don't have to tell anybody you've got it. If you're ever in there and the doors are locked, that's allowed, and nobody's in trouble, and I'll come and sit with you and we don't have to talk."

He didn't say anything. He put it in his pocket.

Then he said, "Is it because of what I wrote at school?"

And I said, "Yeah, bud."

And he said, "Am I in trouble?"

And that — that one, out of everything in this whole story — is the sentence I still hear at four in the morning.

I said no. I said no about six different ways.

He wore that key on the string under his shirt for the next three weeks and he never once mentioned it again, and I know he checked it was still there, because I watched him touch his chest through his sweater about forty times a day like a man checking for a wallet.

On the eleventh day I set the last piece.

Roberta was going to Florida for four days — a thing she announced at dinner with a lot of business about flight times. Darla would be alone in the house with Eli and me.

And at breakfast on the morning Roberta flew out, with her cases by the door and the taxi ten minutes away, I said the sentence I had been building for a week and a half.

I said, easily, into my coffee, not looking up:

"Oh — Darla, the county called yesterday. Some follow-up about the ER thing. They want to come by next week and talk to whoever was there on the night."

Then I drank my coffee.

Nobody had called. There was no appointment. I had invented a date on a calendar out of nothing at all.

And I watched two women, in one kitchen, with a taxi coming, look at each other for the first time in eleven months and discover that they did not know what the other one was going to say.
