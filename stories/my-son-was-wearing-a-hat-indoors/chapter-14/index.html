I Came Home After Eleven Months and My Son Was Wearing a Hat Indoors

Chapter 14 — Everything Nora Left Him

Chapter 14 of 15

The house sold in March.

I didn't want it. I want to be honest about the shape of that, because people assume it was the kitchen, or the utility room, or the door frame — and it wasn't, or not only.

It was that Nora built that house with me and then a stranger renovated it with our son's money, and every good memory in it now had somebody else's quartz on top of it.

So we sold it, and it went for more than it was worth precisely because of the kitchen, which is a joke I have never once found funny.

Before the sale I emptied her room myself.

Eli asked to help. I said yes, and then I lay awake most of the night before deciding whether that had been the right answer, and I still think it was, but only just.

We did it on a Saturday with the window open. Two boxes marked KEEP, one marked ASK LATER, and a stack of flat-packs from the storage place.

Her cardigan went in KEEP without either of us saying anything about it.

Her books took four hours, because he opened every one, because his mother wrote in books — dates, arguments with the author, little notes in the margins — and he had never known that about her, and there she was in about two hundred places in her own handwriting having opinions.

At about three in the afternoon he found the note.

Not the one that was stolen. Another one — folded into the back of a cookbook, the one with the broken spine, in an envelope with his name on it in her writing.

She'd left him seven, we found out eventually. Hidden all over that room in the last four months of her life, in things she knew he'd open one day. Seven letters for a six-year-old to find whenever he got round to it.

He wouldn't let me read that one and I did not ask twice.

He sat on the floor of his mother's room with his back against her bed — the same place I'd sat with the folder — and he read it about six times, and then he folded it up and put it in his pocket and said, "Can I keep the cookbook?"

I said yes.

He said, "Even though it's broken?"

And I said, "*Especially* though it's broken," and that is the first time in five months I heard my son laugh at something I said.

The custodian account was restored in April.

Not by a court — the bank did it, quietly, in the end, because their own investigators had found nine reuses of a scanned signature page and because their exposure was worse than their pride. Two hundred and thirty-one thousand dollars went back into an account in my son's name, and there is a letter about it in a folder that I keep in a fireproof box now, because I have learned some things.

Roberta Pike pleaded to two counts and is repaying at a rate that means she will be finished when she is eighty-one.

Darla's matter took fourteen months. She pleaded to one count of child endangerment and one financial count. The sentence was less than I wanted at the time and about what Priya had told me on day one, and I have made my peace with the number in the way you make your peace with weather.

I did not go to the sentencing. Priya offered to sit with me. I took Eli to the coast instead and we ate terrible fried food and he found a crab and named it Gerald, and I would make the same choice a hundred times out of a hundred.

There is one more thing about that room.

When I lifted the mattress off her bed to break the frame down, there was a sheet of paper underneath it, on the slats.

Not hidden. Just put there, the way you put a thing somewhere while you decide what to do with it, and then die before you decide.

It was a drawing.

Eli did it when he was five, in the year she was ill — a stick family outside a house with a dog we don't have — and she'd kept it, and she'd written across the top in pencil, small, in the corner where nobody was meant to look:

Whatever happens. This.

I sat on the floor of that empty room and held it for a long time.

Then I put it in the KEEP box.

It's on the fridge in the new place. It has been there for two years and it will be there when he is thirty.
