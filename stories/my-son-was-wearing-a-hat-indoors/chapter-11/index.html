I Came Home After Eleven Months and My Son Was Wearing a Hat Indoors

Chapter 11 — The Night The Glass Broke Again

Chapter 11 of 15

Roberta did not get on that plane.

She was back from the airport in fifty minutes with a story about a schedule change that nobody in that kitchen believed, including her, and she wheeled her case back into the hall and left it there for four days like a thing she might still need.

That's when I knew it was working.

They started talking. Not to me — to each other, in the kitchen with the door shut, in the car on the drive with the engine off, in the front room at eleven at night in the voices people use when they think a house is asleep.

And I did the hardest thing on the list, which was: nothing. For three more days. Pleasant. Fixing the gate. Asking about dinner.

Because two people who are frightened of a meeting that is coming will, sooner or later, have the argument about whose fault it is that it's coming.

I just had to be in the room when they did.

I want to say clearly what I did next, because this is the part where somebody always asks whether it was legal, and the answer is yes, in this state, and I checked with Priya twice before I did it, and I have the email.

I put a recorder in the kitchen.

Not a camera. Audio only, and only in the one room, and it was mine, and I was a resident of that house and a participant in the conversation, and in this state that is lawful. Priya wrote me two paragraphs about it and I read them four times.

It sat inside the vent grille under the island, and it ran on the Thursday night from six in the evening.

Here is how it happened.

I did it on purpose and I did it with a wine glass, and if that seems theatrical to you then you have never spent eleven days being pleasant to somebody who put your son in a cupboard.

At ten past nine I got a glass down and poured Darla a drink she hadn't asked for, and I put it in her hand, and I said the county officer's name — a name I had made up, a name that did not exist — and I said, "He wants to know who took him in. That's all. They just want the one who was there."

And I stood back and let it work.

Darla said, "We both went."

And from the other side of the island Roberta said, "I didn't go."

It was very quiet in that kitchen.

Darla turned around. "You were in the car."

"I was not in the car."

"You were in the car, Mom, you sat in the car park because you didn't want to be on the paperwork —"

"I was not on any paperwork," said Roberta, "because I was not there, and you know exactly why I wasn't there, because you told me not to come in."

"Because you'd have said something —"

"Because I would have told them the truth," Roberta said.

And there it was. Nineteen days of work and it took forty seconds.

Darla put the glass down on the quartz, hard.

"Don't," she said. "Don't you dare stand in this kitchen —"

"You threw him," Roberta said.

Nobody said anything at all.

"I didn't throw him."

"You had his arm and you went to put him in there and he pulled and you didn't let go," said Roberta, and her voice had gone flat and tired and about twenty years older. "And he went into the door frame. That is what happened. I was on the stairs. I saw it. And I have been sitting in this house for eleven days with that in my mouth."

Darla said: "You told me to stop him screaming."

"I told you to stop him screaming, I did not tell you to —"

"You said, whatever it takes, before the neighbours —"

"Don't you put that on me. Don't you dare put that on me."

And Darla, who had never in fourteen months raised her voice in front of me, said, very clearly:

"It's your rent."

And that was the sentence that ended it. Not the injury. The money.

Because after that they were not two women arguing about a child any more. They were two people working out, out loud, at speed, in my kitchen, over a recorder in a vent, which one of them had signed what.

I stood by the sink and I did not move, and I let it run for eleven minutes.

And at some point in the middle of it, the second glass went over — Roberta's, backhanded off the counter, not thrown, just knocked — and it hit that four-thousand-dollar floor and broke on the first bounce, in almost exactly the same place, and the wine went out flat across the boards.

Nobody cleaned it up this time.

I looked at it, and I thought about the first night, and my son on the step stool with his back to the room going smaller.

Then I said the only thing I said in the whole eleven minutes.

I said: "Which door frame?"

They both stopped.

And Darla — my wife, who is very quick, and who had been quick for fourteen months, and who I had spent nineteen days making absolutely certain she was safe with me —

Darla looked at the door frame of the utility room.

She didn't point. She didn't say it.

Her eyes went to it for less than half a second, because that is what happens to human beings when they are frightened and somebody asks them which one.

Upstairs, a door opened very quietly, and I heard two stairs not creak.
