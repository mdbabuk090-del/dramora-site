I Came Home After Eleven Months and My Son Was Wearing a Hat Indoors

Chapter 07 — The Nurse Remembers

Chapter 7 of 15

The hospital would not talk to me, and they were right not to.

I went in on the Tuesday with the discharge summary folded in my pocket and my ID and my custodian paperwork, and I stood at the desk in Records and asked for the full file, and a very calm woman explained to me about consent and about the fact that the parent of record on the admission was not me.

I said, "I'm his father."

She said, "I know. I'm sorry."

And I want to say — because it matters, because there is a version of this where I put my hand on that counter and raised my voice and everything after it goes differently — I said thank you, and I sat down in the plastic chairs, and I thought about it for twenty minutes.

Then I went and did the thing that actually worked, which was to go back to the ER waiting room and read the noticeboard.

There was a photograph on it. Staff fundraiser, a line of people in matching T-shirts holding a cheque. Twenty-odd faces. Names underneath.

I took a picture of it.

That evening I sat in the truck and went through the local Facebook groups — the ones about the school district and the ones about the road works and the one where people post pictures of found dogs — and I matched four of those faces to four names to four profiles, and one of them, a triage nurse called Diane, had posted eleven days before I came home, at 1:14 in the morning:

Some nights this job takes it out of you. Hug your kids.

I did not message her. I want to be clear about that, because I nearly did, and if I had, everything would have ended right there.

What I did instead was work out where she got her coffee.

It took two mornings. The little place by the roundabout, 6:40, before shift.

On the second morning I was in the line behind her, in a T-shirt, with paint on my hands from a fence I was genuinely painting, and I did not say one word to her about my son. I let her get her coffee and go.

On the third morning she was behind me, and I paid for hers, and she said you didn't have to do that, and I said my wife's a nurse — which was a lie, and it is the only lie I told anybody in the whole of this — and she laughed and said which unit, and I said ER, and she said oh God, and we talked for four minutes about how nobody thanks them.

On the fourth morning she said, "Are you not working?"

And I said, "I just got back. Eleven months. I'm home with my boy."

And she went very slightly still, the way people do when a shape falls into place.

She said, "What's your name?"

I said, "Marcus Vance."

Diane looked at me for about three seconds.

Then she said: "Is he all right?"

Not who? Not should I know you?

Is he all right.

Eleven days, a hundred patients, and a triage nurse remembered a nine-year-old's surname on the strength of a first name and a deployment.

I said, "I don't know. I'm trying to find out."

She looked at her coffee for a while.

She said, "I can't tell you anything about a patient. You understand that. I'd lose my licence and I'd deserve to."

"I know."

"So I'm not going to."

"Okay."

And then Diane, who I have never seen since and who I hope has a long and easy life, said the following sentence to the air in front of her, as though I were not standing there at all:

"It's just a strange thing, isn't it. When a woman brings a child in at eleven at night with a scalp lac, and she's very calm and very well dressed, and she says he fell down the stairs. And you write it down, because you write down what they tell you. And then three hours later, when the boy's coming round from the sutures and he's on his own with you for two minutes, you ask him what happened — because we always ask them on their own, that's the whole job — and he says he doesn't remember."

She turned her cup around.

"And you look at the notes and you think: there's no reason a nine-year-old shouldn't remember falling down stairs at nine o'clock at night. Concussion, maybe. But he wasn't concussed. He was bright as a button. Knew the day, knew his teacher, knew every single thing I asked him."

"Except that."

"Except that," she said. "And you flag it. Because that's what you're supposed to do, you flag it, you tick the box, it goes to the safeguarding lead, and somebody makes a call within seventy-two hours."

She picked up her coffee.

"And then you go home and you don't hear another word about it, because you never do."

She stood up.

"It's the not hearing that gets you," she said. "You do the right thing at one in the morning and then it just — goes somewhere. And you never find out if it landed."

Then she said, "Good luck with the fence," and she left.

I sat in that coffee shop until they started stacking chairs around me.

A referral had been made.

Eleven days before I came home, a nurse in a bright room had ticked a box about my son, and it had gone into a system, and something had happened to it.

And nobody had ever called me. Not the school, not the hospital, not the county. Nobody had emailed the man whose name is on the boy's birth certificate and whose military address had been on file at that school for eleven months.

Somebody had answered that call.

Somebody in my house had picked up the phone to a safeguarding officer and said the words that made it go away, and I had eaten dinner with both of them the night before.
