I Came Home After Eleven Months and My Son Was Wearing a Hat Indoors

Chapter 09 — The Camera Darla Forgot

Chapter 9 of 15

The doorbell camera was mine.

I bought it in the January before I flew, and I fitted it myself on a cold Saturday with Eli holding the drill bits, and the account is in my name and the subscription comes off my card and Darla has never once had to think about it, because it is the sort of thing that just works and then becomes invisible.

That is the entire reason it survived.

People remember to delete the things they think about. Nobody deletes the thing that has been beeping quietly by the front door for a year like a smoke alarm.

I logged in on the Friday night, out in the truck, on my phone, with my hands not quite steady.

Thirty days of history.

That's what the plan gives you. Thirty days, rolling, and everything older than that is gone forever and there is nothing anybody can do about it. Eleven months of that house had already fallen off the back end while I was six thousand miles away not thinking about it.

I had thirty days.

I started at the oldest one and I worked forward, and it took me until nearly four in the morning, and I am not going to pretend it was detective work. It was a man sitting in a cold truck watching his own front door, over and over, for six hours.

Here is what a doorbell camera sees: everybody who comes and goes. It does not see inside the house. It does not see the kitchen, or the utility room, or the upstairs hall.

Which turned out not to matter at all, because of what it does record, which is sound.

Not much. Fifteen, twenty feet through a front door. Muffled. But when a house is quiet at night and somebody is shouting in the hallway, the camera by the door picks it up, and it timestamps it, and it keeps it for thirty days.

I found eleven clips.

Eleven, in twenty-nine days, where something in that hallway was loud enough to trigger the sound detection on a device pointed at the street.

I listened to all of them twice.

Nine were nothing — a television, a door slamming in wind, Roberta on the phone in the front room laughing at something.

Two were not nothing.

The first was fourteen days before I came home. 21:52. Forty-one seconds. You can hear a woman's voice, not shouting exactly, going at a pitch and a speed that has nothing to do with talking to a child, and you cannot make out the words except for four of them near the end, which are very clear because she says them slowly:

"— then don't come out."

And then a door closing. And then nothing.

Twenty-two minutes later, the camera picks up the front door opening, and Roberta Pike walks out to the blue Nissan and drives away, and at 22:31 she comes back with a plastic bag from the pharmacy on Route 9.

The second was eleven days before I came home.

22:47.

Nineteen seconds.

There is a bang — one, flat, wooden, not glass. Then a boy's voice, very short, and it isn't a word.

Then quiet.

Then, at 22:51, four minutes later, the front door opens and my son comes out with Darla's hand on the back of his neck, and she is holding a folded tea towel to the top of his head, and she is completely calm, and she puts him in the passenger seat of her car, and she goes back and locks the front door, and she checks it.

That's the bit I keep coming back to. She went back and checked the lock.

At 22:53 they pull off the drive, and the camera goes back to sleep.

Eleven days before I came home. Twenty-two forty-seven.

Twenty-two forty-seven and a wooden bang and my son making a sound that isn't a word, four minutes before he leaves that house bleeding.

Not stairs.

There isn't a set of stairs in this world that says bang once and stops.

I sat in the truck with the phone against my forehead for a long time.

Then I did four things, in this order, and I did them before I let myself feel anything, because I had learned by then that feeling it came later and cost about two hours.

One. I downloaded all eleven clips to the phone. Then to a laptop. Then to a drive that went into the toolbox in the truck bed under a tray of sockets.

Two. I emailed all eleven to myself at an address nobody in that house knows exists, and then to the lawyer whose name I'd been carrying folded in my wallet since the Tuesday.

Three. I upgraded the subscription — sixty days instead of thirty — because the one thing I could still buy was time.

Four. I did not change the password.

That last one was the hardest thing I did in the whole of that autumn, and it is the reason any of the rest of it worked.

Because if I'd changed it, and she'd gone to look, and found herself locked out —

she'd have known.
