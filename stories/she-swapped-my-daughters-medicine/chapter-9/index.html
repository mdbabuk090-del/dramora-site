My Mother-in-Law Swapped My Daughter's Medicine

Chapter 09 — The Visit

Chapter 9 of 10

They took Curtis Pike off the jet bridge.

He was carrying two passports, twenty-two thousand dollars in cash, and an external drive sewn into the lining of a suitcase. The arrest led the evening news, and by nine o'clock there were three vans parked outside our house.

They wanted a photograph of my daughter. They wanted to know whether she had cracked the case. They wanted the brave little girl from the hospital bed, five years older, standing in a doorway.

I said no, in the shortest sentence available.

Nell watched them through a gap in the curtain.

"They're telling people I found him."

"You found a password in an envelope. Investigators found him."

"They still know my name."

"Yes."

"I don't like it."

"We can go out through the garage."

"I'm not running out of my own house."

She let the curtain fall and went to do her homework.

The Initiative put out a statement asking the media to leave minors connected to the case alone. Most of the papers complied. A number of online channels did not — they re-cut the footage of her hospital speech and captioned it the child who brought down a medical empire.

She hated that phrase more than anything else that was written about her.

"I didn't bring down anything," she said. "Grandma did it. Elliot helped her. Curtis helped them. Then a lot of adults did their jobs."

"People like stories with one hero and one villain."

"That's not what happened."

"No."

"So why do they say it?"

"Because the truth takes longer, and it doesn't fit in a headline."

Pike refused to cooperate at first. He said my brother had invented everything for attention. He said someone else had created the archive.

Then the technicians finished with the drive from his suitcase.

It held copies of the private ledgers. It also held recordings. Curtis had been taping Rosalind for years — insurance, in case she ever decided he was the loose end.

On one of them she says:

The trust will be mine eventually. Until then, keep the transfers small.

On another they discuss the charity accounts.

Nobody queries anything labeled emergency assistance.

And if a family asks for receipts?

And Rosalind laughs.

Families with sick children are far too tired to audit paperwork.

Nell heard that sentence in Joanna's office, because she had asked to be there and I had decided she was old enough to be told no by somebody other than me.

Her face went white.

"Turn it off."

Joanna stopped it.

"Do you want to leave?" I asked.

"No."

"You don't have to hear any more of it."

"I knew what she did to me." Nell's voice was flat. "I didn't know she did it to other kids."

Neither had I.

The six families were contacted through the Initiative, on conditions we set and the district attorney accepted. No press. No fundraising campaign built out of their photographs. Each family got an independent attorney and an independent financial adviser, paid for out of the recovered funds, and nobody had to tell their story in public to get their money back.

One of the mothers asked to meet me.

Melissa Grant's son Caleb had been treated for leukemia at eleven. The foundation had opened an account to cover the four-hour drive to the hospital and a room near it.

Seventy-nine thousand dollars had gone out of that account.

She sat across the table from me in Joanna's conference room and turned her coffee around without drinking it.

"For three years I thought I'd made mistakes," she said.

"What kind?"

"When the reimbursements were refused, the foundation told me I'd submitted the wrong forms. That I'd missed the deadlines. Every time."

"You hadn't."

"I stopped arguing. Caleb was sick and I didn't have anything left over for arguing." Her voice broke. "I apologized to my son for not being organized enough."

I sat there and thought about a woman in a plum suit announcing to a ballroom that I had forgotten my daughter's medication.

The method never changed.

Create confusion. Blame the exhausted parent. Protect the institution.

"Tell your girl she helped my family," Melissa said.

I told her that night.

Nell looked uncomfortable. "I didn't do much."

"You made a hard decision."

"Elliot gave me the password."

"He did."

"So he helped."

"He handed over evidence he'd been sitting on for four years, when he thought it might buy him something. That's not the same as helping."

"Can bad people do good things?"

"Yes."

"Can good people do bad things?"

"Yes."

"Then how do you know which one someone is?"

"You watch the pattern. You watch whether they take responsibility when nobody's making them. You watch what they do when telling the truth costs them."

She thought about it.

"He only tells the truth when it costs someone else."

"That's been his pattern for a long time."

Pike's attorney filed a motion arguing the password had been improperly obtained from a child. He wanted the archive thrown out. The police had a warrant before they opened a single folder, so the motion was weak — but a defense is allowed to test how evidence was found, and testing it might have meant putting my daughter on a stand.

Elliot's attorney contacted the prosecutor the next morning. Elliot would testify about Pike and the archive.

In exchange, he wanted to be considered for a transfer to a lower-security facility.

Nell was furious when Joanna told us.

"He's selling it again."

"Yes."

"Do they have to say yes?"

"No."

"Will they?"

"They might, if his evidence keeps six families' money safe."

"So he gets something."

"He may."

"That's not fair."

"No. It isn't."

"Then why do it?"

"Because the system sometimes has to make deals with guilty people to prove what other guilty people did. It's ugly and it works more often than it should."

She crossed her arms.

"Does that mean he wins?"

"No. He's still in prison. Your mother is still dead. He doesn't get any of it back."

In the end it didn't matter. Pike's attorney heard the recordings and folded within a week. Elliot's testimony was never needed. His transfer request was denied.

He had waited too long, one final time, to use the only thing he had.

Two years later, Nell turned fourteen and got a school assignment about accountability. Every student had to interview someone who had done something seriously wrong and accepted responsibility for it.

She came home and put the sheet on the table without saying anything.

"You could interview your grandfather about the birthday he forgot," I said.

She didn't laugh.

"You're thinking about Elliot."

"Yes."

"You can pick someone else."

"I know."

"Why him?"

"Because I don't actually know if he ever did accept it."

"Visiting him might not answer that."

"I know."

"Are you trying to forgive him?"

"No."

"Then what?"

She looked at the assignment sheet for a while.

"I want to know if people can change when changing doesn't get them anything."

That was the question underneath all three letters.

Dr. Brooks met with her four times before we went. Together they wrote the rules down on an index card. Elliot could not ask for forgiveness. He could not describe Rebecca's death in unnecessary detail. He could not blame Rosalind. He could not make his feelings Nell's problem. Nell could end it at any second, for any reason or none. I would sit beside her and say nothing unless she asked.

On the morning of the visit she checked the injector in her bag.

"You're not going to need that because of Elliot," I said.

"I know."

"Then why check?"

"Because being ready reminds me I can leave."

Prison had thinned my brother's face and gone gray at his temples. He was forty and looked older than our father had at sixty.

When Nell came in, he stood up.

A guard told him to sit down.

We took the chairs opposite. Nobody spoke for a while.

Then Elliot said, "You look like your mother."

Nell's face closed.

"I didn't come here to make you feel better."

He looked down. "I understand."

"I have questions."

"I'll answer them."

"Why did you put the password in a letter to a child?"

"Because I thought your father would destroy anything with my name on it."

I leaned forward before I could stop myself. "You thought I'd destroy evidence that could help sick children?"

He met my eyes. "I thought you hated me enough to burn them unopened."

"I did hate you."

"I know."

"I wouldn't have destroyed evidence."

"I understand that now."

Nell was watching him the whole time.

"That's not the whole answer," she said.

Elliot stopped.

"No," he said. "It isn't."

"So what is?"

"I wanted to keep it until it was worth something to me."

"You used me as storage."

"Yes."

"You made me carry something you were too scared to carry."

"Yes."

"Did you know Curtis was still stealing?"

"I suspected it."

"Did you know other children lost money?"

"Not all of them. Some."

"And you didn't tell anyone."

"No."

"Why?"

"Because I thought it might get me moved somewhere better later on."

Nell opened her notebook and wrote one line.

"Did you love my mom?"

"Yes."

"Did you love me?"

"Yes."

"Then why were we never worth more to you than protecting yourself?"

His hands closed on the edge of the table.

"Because I wanted to be loved without having to be responsible for loving anyone back."

"That sounds like something a counselor taught you to say."

"It is."

"Do you believe it?"

"I do now."

"How would I know?"

"You wouldn't," he said. "You shouldn't believe I've changed because I tell you I have. You'd have to watch what I do for a long time, and you have no reason to spend your life doing that."

That answer surprised all three of us.

"What have you done?" she asked.

"I work in the hospice unit here."

"What does that mean?"

"I sit with men who are dying and have nobody coming. I read to them. I write their letters when their hands stop working."

"Why?"

"My counselor suggested it."

"And now?"

He was quiet for a moment.

"Because your mother asked me to call for help while she was dying, and I didn't. I can't undo that. But when somebody asks me for something now, I don't walk out of the room."

My chest went tight.

Nell put her pen down.

"What did she say?"

Elliot looked at me for permission.

I did not give it. I did not stop him either.

"She said Adrian will help you. Just call the ambulance."

I closed my eyes.

"She said, 'He'll forgive you. Just call.'"

For eight years I had imagined my wife's last words a hundred different ways.

It had never once occurred to me that they had faith in me in them.

Even going under, she was trying to give my brother a door.

Nell started to cry.

"Why didn't you call?"

Elliot's face came apart.

"Because I was afraid they'd find the money."

"She was dying."

"I know."

"You had a phone in your hand."

"I know."

"You could have saved her."

"I might have."

"Then why didn't you?"

He made himself look at her.

"Because in that minute, protecting myself was worth more to me than her life."

There was no softer version available. He didn't try to find one.

Nell closed her notebook.

"I'm not forgiving you."

"I understand."

"I might never forgive you."

"I understand."

"You don't get to keep saying you understand and then wait for me to change my mind."

He nodded slowly. "I won't wait for anything from you."

"I don't want more letters."

"There won't be any."

"If you ever know something that could protect someone, tell the police. Don't put it in an envelope and mail it to a kid."

"I won't."

She stood up, and the visit was over.

In the parking lot she was sick behind the car. Dr. Brooks had warned us that was possible. I got water out of the boot and sat down on the curb next to her.

"I thought hearing why would make it make sense," she said.

"Did it?"

"No."

"Some choices never turn into sense. That's what makes them choices."

"He picked himself."

"Yes."

"And Mom still thought you'd forgive him."

"Your mother thought calling an ambulance mattered more than what happened afterwards."

"Would you have forgiven him?"

"If he'd called, and she'd lived — I'd have tried."

"Even after he stole from her?"

"Money comes back. Trust can be rebuilt or it can be ended, and either is survivable." I looked at the gate. "Your mother couldn't be replaced."

Nell leaned her head on my shoulder.

"I don't want to come back here."

"You never have to."

"What if he really has changed?"

"Then he can change without being given access to you."

That was the lesson I wished somebody had taught Rebecca fifteen years earlier: a person's growth does not create an obligation in the people they hurt to open the door again.
