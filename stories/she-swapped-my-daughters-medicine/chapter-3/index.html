My Mother-in-Law Swapped My Daughter's Medicine

Chapter 03 — What Was Supposed to Happen

Chapter 3 of 10

They arrested Elliot in my daughter's hospital room.

He did not resist. He held out his hands and kept looking at the two auto-injectors on the floor as though they had done something to him.

Reed had the syringe couriered to the state laboratory that night, and the two returned injectors bagged separately. Then he sat down across from me in a family room with bad coffee and explained what my brother had been sent to the luncheon to do.

The plan had never been for Nell to die.

The plan was for me to inject her with the wrong thing.

Elliot's job was to lift both real auto-injectors out of my jacket and leave the prefilled syringe in their place. When Nell went into anaphylaxis in front of four hundred donors, I would reach into my pocket in a panic and find one dose of something, and I would use it, because a father with a child turning blue does not stop to read a dispensing label.

Then Nell would get worse instead of better.

And in front of the entire board of the hospital, Rosalind would explain that her son-in-law had been carrying his dead wife's old sedative around in the same pocket as his daughter's emergency medicine, for two years, because grief had made him careless.

That was the story. That was all of it. A negligent widower, a public medical emergency, four hundred witnesses, and a guardianship petition already drafted and waiting on a lawyer's desk.

"He didn't leave it," Reed said. "That's the part that saved her."

"Why not?"

"He says a server came into the coat area while he had his hand in your jacket. He took both injectors and walked out with the syringe still in his bag. Then he couldn't get back to the table."

So I found nothing at all.

And an ambulance came, and my daughter lived, and Rosalind had to improvise the rest of the afternoon out loud in a ballroom.

"Where does the syringe come in?"

"He drove to her house afterward and gave it to her. She put it in her car." Reed turned his cup around. "Then he spent three hours working out that a syringe sitting in her glove box with a label on it was the only physical object connecting either of them to anything. So he came here to trade. Put the two real injectors back into the evidence, take the syringe out, and let the whole thing be a catering error."

"He came to a hospital full of police."

"He believed she was still free. He believed there was still time."

I thought about the phone call. Did anyone scan it yet.

"He was asking if the barcode had already been read."

"He was."

I have thought about that question more than almost anything else in this story. My brother's first instinct, on being told his niece had nearly suffocated on a ballroom floor, was to find out whether a label had been read out loud.

He knew what was on it.

He had known for two years.

Nell cried after they took him out. Not loudly. She turned her face into the pillow and tried to keep it off her face where I could see it.

"Sweetheart."

"Uncle Elliot said he loved me."

I pulled the chair closer. "I know."

"Was that a lie?"

I did not have a clean answer, and I did not want to hand her one that would fall apart later. Elliot had carried her on his shoulders through the zoo. He taught her to ride a bike in our street. He sat on the floor of her room for two nights when she had the flu and I had a deadline.

"He may have meant it," I said. "But he made choices that put you in danger. Caring about someone doesn't cancel out hurting them."

"That's what you said about Grandma."

"It is."

She thought about that for a while.

"Was Mom scared of them?"

"I don't know."

It was the truth, and it was the first time it had ever occurred to me that my wife might have spent the last month of her life inside a house full of people she had stopped trusting.

Reed came back near midnight with a consent form for the guest room, and another for anything connected to Rebecca's trust. I signed both without reading them. Then we went out into the corridor so my daughter could sleep.

"There's something else," he said. "Elliot's lawyer has shut him up, but before she got here, he said Rosalind gave your wife a sedative because Rebecca was having panic attacks."

"Rebecca didn't have panic attacks."

"Was she ever prescribed anything to sleep?"

"Not that I knew of."

"Heart condition?"

"No. The medical examiner called it a sudden arrhythmia."

"Full autopsy?"

"Limited. Rosalind objected. She said Rebecca wouldn't have wanted it."

"And you agreed."

I leaned against the wall.

"I had come home from an airport and found my wife on the bedroom floor. Rosalind got to the house before the ambulance had finished loading her. She told me Rebecca had been complaining of chest pressure for weeks."

"Had she?"

"No."

"Then why didn't you push?"

"Because she was her mother," I said, and heard how thin it sounded out loud.

Grief does something to your judgment that is very hard to describe afterward. Rebecca was thirty-five. One week she was arguing with me about a kitchen extension and the next week there were fourteen people in my living room speaking with total confidence about what had happened, and I could not remember how to answer a phone. Rosalind arranged the funeral. Elliot moved into the guest room to help. A physician connected to Rosalind's hospital signed off on the heart explanation.

I accepted it because the alternative was unthinkable, and because everyone around me had already agreed.

"Who signed the death certificate?" Reed asked.

"Dr. Nathan Vale."

He wrote it down. "Did he treat her?"

"He saw her once. Rosalind arranged it."

Reed looked up.

"Nathan Vale is listed as a paid clinical adviser to Vancroft Medical Consulting."

My brother's company.

"How long have you known that?"

"About four hours. It's in the trust statement."

"What do you need from me?"

"Rebecca's medical records. Her laptop. Her phone if you still have it. Her email. Anything she kept."

"Take all of it."

That night the hospital moved Nell to a private pediatric room and put an officer outside the door. A social worker brought me a clean shirt. At two in the morning, sitting in a plastic chair beside my daughter, I called the one person left who had known Rebecca before she knew me.

Joanna Pierce is an estate attorney. She had drafted Nell's trust, and she had been Rebecca's friend since law school.

I told her what had happened.

She was quiet for a long time.

"Adrian," she said. "There's something I should have told you two years ago."
