My Mother-in-Law Swapped My Daughter's Medicine

Chapter 02 — The Name on the Label

Chapter 2 of 10

Rebecca never took my name.

Her father had left her the money, and she had told me once, laughing, that she was keeping his surname because it was the only thing of his that Rosalind had never managed to get her hands on. So she stayed Rebecca Hale, and Nell was a Vance, and for nine years that was a joke we made at family dinners.

Standing in that emergency room, it stopped being a joke.

The nurse looked at the screen again. "The dispense date is two years ago."

"How many years?" Reed asked.

"Two. May." She checked the label a third time, because by then she could feel the room. "It was filled two years ago."

I did the arithmetic before I could stop myself.

Rebecca died on a Thursday night in May.

The syringe in that bag had been filled two days before it.

"What is it?" I said. "What's in it?"

"The label says it's a sedative. Injectable." The nurse set the scanner down carefully, the way you put down something you have decided not to touch again. "The label tells you what the pharmacy dispensed. It doesn't tell you what's actually inside the barrel. That needs a lab."

Reed was already on his radio.

Rosalind said, "Call my attorney."

Then, because she could not help herself:

"And before you make a mistake, Detective, you might remember who paid for the children's wing you're standing in."

Reed put his radio away.

"Rosalind Hale, you're under arrest in connection with the deliberate exposure of a child to a known allergen, the removal of emergency medication, and tampering with evidence."

She did not struggle. She did not raise her voice. She let the officer take her wrists, and while he was doing it she looked past him at my daughter.

"This is your father's fault."

I stepped in front of the bed again.

"No. It's yours."

They took her out through the corridor and the door swung shut, and Nell reached over and put her fingers around two of mine.

"Is it over, Dad?"

I wanted to say yes.

Reed set a sealed envelope on the tray table.

"No," he said. "It isn't."

The envelope had come out of Rosalind's briefcase. Inside were three documents.

The first was a petition asking the court to remove me as trustee of my daughter's inheritance — just under three million dollars, left to Nell by her mother.

The second proposed Rosalind as the replacement trustee, with control of the money until Nell turned eighteen.

The third asked the court to name a permanent guardian for Nell if I lost custody.

I looked at the preparation date on the first page.

Two weeks before the luncheon.

Before the dessert. Before the missing injectors. Before Rosalind stood up in front of four hundred people and told them I had forgotten my daughter's medicine.

Then I read the incident summary attached to the petition. It described a father who failed to carry his daughter's emergency medication to a charity event and put her life at risk through negligence.

It had been written a fortnight before it happened.

"This was planned," I said.

Reed turned to the last page.

Rosalind's signature was at the bottom. Beside it was a second signature, from the person who had sworn under oath that I was depressed, unreliable, and unfit to raise my own child.

My brother.

Elliot had been living in my guest room since the week Rebecca died. He picked Nell up from school when I worked late. He ate at my table four nights a week. He knew which pocket I kept the injectors in, because he was the one who used to remind me to check them.

He had not signed as a witness.

He had petitioned the court to make himself Nell's permanent guardian.

My phone started ringing on the tray table.

ELLIOT.

Reed looked at me. "Answer it. Don't tell him she's been arrested."

I put it on speaker.

"Adrian." My brother's voice was calm. "I heard something happened to Nell."

"She had a reaction."

"Is she alive?"

Not is she all right. Not what happened.

Is she alive.

"Yes."

I heard him breathe out.

Then he asked the question that ended him.

"Did anyone scan it yet?"

Reed's head came up slowly.

I had not told my brother that anything had been recovered. I had not told him there was a syringe, or a bag, or a label. Nobody outside that room knew there was a barcode to scan.

"Scan what, Elliot?"

The line went dead.

Reed had hospital security on every entrance inside of two minutes. Then he went back to the papers from Rosalind's briefcase, and behind the custody petition he found a private account statement from Nell's trust.

Six hundred and eleven thousand dollars had gone out of it over eighteen months, in amounts small enough that no one reviewing a quarterly summary would stop on any single line.

Every payment had gone to the same recipient.

Vancroft Medical Consulting.

My brother's company.

Nell tugged my hand.

"Dad."

"Yes, sweetheart."

"Uncle Elliot came in my room last night."

I turned around. "What did he want?"

"He asked where Mom's watch was."

The smartwatch had been Rebecca's. After she died I gave it to Nell because it still had her mother's old voice memos on it, and Nell liked to fall asleep listening to them.

Elliot had known what was on it. He had also known Nell had been wearing it at the luncheon.

"He looked in my dresser," Nell said. "I told him I didn't know where it was."

"Where was it?"

"Under my pillow."

I kissed the top of her head. "You did exactly the right thing."

Security called Reed nine minutes later.

Elliot's car had just come up the ramp into the visitor garage.

"He still thinks she's running this," Reed said. "He doesn't know she's in a cell."

The door opened a few minutes after that.

My brother came in carrying a small black medical bag, and stopped dead when he saw the uniform by the window. The bag slipped out of his hand and landed on its side, and two epinephrine auto-injectors rolled across the floor and stopped against the leg of Nell's bed.

Both of them. Sealed. Unused.

The ones that had been in my jacket.

"Those aren't mine," Elliot said.

Reed looked down at them.

"Then why did you bring them back?"

My brother did not answer.

He was staring at the sealed bag on the tray table, and at the syringe inside it, and at the little printed label with his sister-in-law's name on it, and I watched him understand that he was about four hours too late.

"She was only supposed to sleep," he said.

The room went very quiet.

"Supposed to?" Reed said.

Elliot heard what had just come out of his own mouth.

For two years I had believed my wife's heart had simply stopped.
