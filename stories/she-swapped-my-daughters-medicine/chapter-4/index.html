My Mother-in-Law Swapped My Daughter's Medicine

Chapter 04 — If Something Happens to Me

Chapter 4 of 10

"Rebecca called me four days before she died," Joanna said.

I stood up and walked to the window so I would not wake my daughter.

"Why didn't you tell me?"

"She asked me not to. She said she was looking into withdrawals she couldn't explain, and she wasn't going to accuse anyone in her family until she had something she could put in front of a judge."

"Withdrawals from Nell's trust?"

"It wasn't Nell's yet. It became Nell's when Rebecca died. Before that it was Rebecca's — her father set it up for her, separately, specifically so that Rosalind could never touch it."

"What did she find?"

"Payments to a consulting company. Your brother's."

I put my hand flat on the cold glass.

"Why was Elliot anywhere near my wife's money?"

"Rosalind introduced him as an investment adviser."

"Elliot has never been an investment adviser in his life."

"Rebecca worked that out too," Joanna said. "That's why she called me."

She told me the rest of it in pieces, the way people tell you things they have been carrying.

Rebecca's father had died when she was twenty-six and left her the money in a structure Rosalind had spent years trying to unpick. When Rebecca married me she named me successor trustee. After Nell was born she amended it again so that everything would pass directly to Nell if anything happened to her.

Rosalind was named nowhere in any version of that document.

"She was going to remove Elliot's access and order a full audit," Joanna said.

"When?"

"Friday morning."

I sat down on the arm of the chair.

"She died Thursday night."

"Yes."

Neither of us said anything for a while.

"Did she leave anything with you?"

"A sealed letter. She told me that if she didn't turn up on Friday, I should get it to you."

"I never got a letter."

"I sent it certified. To your house."

"When?"

"The Monday after the funeral."

I already knew what she was going to say next, and I still had to hear it.

"Who signed for it?"

"Elliot," she said. "Elliot signed for it."

He had been living in my house for six days by then. He had been the one collecting the mail because I could not walk to the end of the drive without stopping.

"What was in it?"

"I don't know. It was sealed."

"Do you have a copy?"

"No."

I ended the call before I said something to a woman who had lost her friend too. But I was angry with her for a long time after that, and some of it was fair.

Reed came back the next evening with a list.

The search of Elliot's room had produced a second phone, printouts of my electronic signature, my daughter's school and medical schedules going back a year, a draft guardianship petition, eighteen months of trust statements, and one empty certified-mail envelope with Joanna's firm printed in the corner.

The letter was not in it.

But there was something else in the drawer with the envelope.

A receipt for a storage unit, rented under Vancroft Medical Consulting, six days after my wife's funeral.

Reed got a warrant.

The unit held nine boxes of financial records, two laptops, and a plastic crate of things taken out of Rebecca's home office — her desk calendar, a paperweight Nell had made, a box file of my father-in-law's original papers.

One of the laptops was hers.

The drive had been wiped, but badly, by someone in a hurry who had used the operating system's own delete function and believed that was the end of it. The forensic technician had most of it back inside a day.

In the recovered material was a folder.

IF SOMETHING HAPPENS TO ME.

There was one file in it.

They played it for me the following morning, in a conference room on the fourth floor, with a box of tissues already on the table because Reed had watched it first.

Rebecca appeared on the screen in a gray sweater, in our old kitchen, with the window behind her. Her hair was tied back. She looked tired and completely lucid.

The timestamp was three days before she died.

"Adrian," she said. "I hope you never have to watch this."

I put my hand over my mouth.

I had not heard my wife's voice in two years. It went through me like cold water.

"I found withdrawals from my trust that I did not authorize," she said. "The payments went to Elliot's company. But I think my mother arranged them."

She held up a fan of paper toward the camera.

"I asked Elliot about it yesterday. He told me my mother said the money was going to end up hers anyway."

She looked straight down the lens.

"She's also been asking people what happens to the trust if you lose custody of Nell."

Reed had already seen it. He was watching me instead of the screen.

"I'm meeting Joanna on Friday," Rebecca said. "I'm removing every member of both our families from access and putting an independent bank in as co-trustee. I haven't told my mother."

She glanced off-camera, toward the kitchen door.

Then she leaned in and lowered her voice, and I have never in my life wanted so badly to reach into a screen.

"Elliot's been in the house a lot. He says he's helping you with the extension. I found him in my office at eleven o'clock last night."

The frame shook slightly as she moved the laptop.

"If I don't make the meeting — there's a blue folder in the cedar chest at Mom's lake house. My father put the original trust papers in there before he died. She doesn't know it's there. I'm fairly sure she doesn't know it exists."

The file ended.

I stared at the frozen last frame for a long time.

"Does Rosalind have a lake house?" Reed asked.

"Yes."

"Does Elliot have access to it?"

"He has keys to everything she owns."

Reed stood up and put his jacket on.

For two years I had believed I was a man whose wife's heart had stopped for no reason. I had built an entire personality around that. I had gone to a grief group and said the words sudden cardiac event out loud, more than once, to strangers.

I was not that man.

I was a man whose wife had known she was in danger, had written it down, filmed it, hidden it, and mailed it — and had been robbed of every single copy by the two people I let sleep under my roof.

They had missed one.
