My Mother-in-Law Swapped My Daughter's Medicine

Chapter 06 — The Trial

Chapter 6 of 10

Rosalind's trial ran five weeks.

The gallery was full every morning. She came in each day in a conservative suit, wearing the same composed expression she had worn standing over my daughter's hospital bed. She never once looked at me.

She looked at the reporters.

She had spent forty years building a face for exactly that camera. She funded a hospital wing. She hosted the dinners. She appeared in magazines with her hand on the shoulder of a child in a treatment chair.

Her attorneys tried very hard to make the trial about that face.

They called her a philanthropist. They called my brother unreliable because he had taken a plea. They said Nathan Vale was saving himself. They suggested — carefully, in the passive voice — that Rebecca might have taken the sedative on her own.

Then the prosecutor played my wife's video.

The whole room watched a thirty-five-year-old woman in a gray sweater explain, in her own kitchen, exactly who was stealing from her and exactly what she intended to do about it on Friday.

Rosalind's expression did not move.

But when Rebecca said I think my mother arranged them, her mother looked down at the table.

Vale testified for a day and a half.

He admitted writing the prescription in Rebecca's name. He admitted handing the syringe to Rosalind. He admitted attending the house instead of calling for an ambulance, and drafting a death summary before anyone was pronounced.

"Did Rebecca Hale consent to receive that sedative?"

"No."

"Did she have a diagnosed cardiac condition?"

"No."

"Did you tell Adrian Vance that his wife had been complaining of chest pain?"

"Yes."

"Was that true?"

"No."

Elliot testified for two days.

He walked them through the invoices, the transfers, the letter he signed for and opened, the camera he unplugged, the tea, the forty-one minutes, the storage unit, and the coat in the ballroom.

On cross, Rosalind's attorney asked why a jury should believe a man who had stolen from his own niece.

"They shouldn't believe me because I'm good," Elliot said. "I'm not. They should believe the invoices, the messages, the video, and the lab report. I'm guilty. So is she."

The attorney turned. "She?"

My brother looked across the room at the defendant.

For most of his life he had called her Mrs. Hale, because she was my wife's mother and not his. After Rebecca died she started calling him dear, and he started calling her Rosalind, and then he started doing what she said.

"I called her family when she was paying me," he said. "Adrian was my family. I sold him."

I felt nothing that I would call sympathy.

But it was the first time in twenty months that my brother had said a true thing in a room where it cost him something.

The jury saw the ballroom footage twice. They saw the dessert move. They saw the jacket come off the chair. They saw the syringe go halfway into the pocket and come back out.

They heard forty seconds of smartwatch audio in which an eight-year-old practices two lines about being nervous, and then two adults, four seconds, nine words. Both? Yes. And the plate? Where it needs to be.

On its own it is nothing. A defense attorney could stand a jury in front of that recording all afternoon and ask them what a plate is.

So the prosecutor did not leave it on its own.

He put the audio on one side of the screen and the ballroom footage on the other, and ran them at the same time, and the jury watched a hand lift my jacket off a chair at the exact second a woman's voice said yes.

Then he cleared the screen and put up the guardianship petition and its incident summary, side by side with a calendar, and left them there for a long time.

Fourteen days.

The description of my failure had been drafted fourteen days before the failure occurred.

Nobody at the defense table ever offered an explanation for that.

I testified last.

I described the ballroom, and the empty pocket, and the phone call from my brother. Rosalind's attorney tried the only door he had left.

"Mr. Vance, you hate my client."

"I hate what she did."

"So this is revenge."

"This is my daughter being alive."

"You also control a trust worth almost three million dollars."

"An independent bank shares control of it now."

"Was that always the arrangement?"

"No. My wife named me because she trusted me."

"And you failed to notice six hundred thousand dollars leaving it."

That one landed. It was meant to.

"I did," I said. "I failed to notice a great many things."

"Perhaps you were never competent to manage it."

"Perhaps I trusted the people who were stealing it."

He stepped closer to the rail.

"Isn't it true that Rosalind Hale warned others, repeatedly, that you were unstable after your wife's death?"

"Yes."

"Were you depressed?"

"I was grieving."

"Were you medicated?"

"For seven months."

"Did you ever forget your daughter's school schedule?"

"Yes."

"Did you ever miss an appointment?"

"Yes."

"Then Mrs. Hale had grounds for concern."

I looked at her while I answered, and she finally looked back.

"She didn't collect my mistakes because she was worried about Nell," I said. "She collected them because she needed a file. And when my ordinary mistakes weren't enough for a judge, she manufactured a medical emergency in a room full of witnesses and put my daughter in it."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

The civil custody petition — which she had filed from a cell, three weeks before trial, claiming I had coached my daughter and mismanaged the trust — was dismissed before the criminal case even finished. The judge found no evidence that I was any danger to my own child, and ordered Rosalind to have no direct contact with her.

The jury was out for less than two days.

They convicted her on every substantive count: the trust fraud, the deliberate endangerment of a child, and her part in the death of her own daughter.

At sentencing I was allowed to speak.

I brought my wife's letter with me, folded in my jacket pocket, in the same place I used to carry the injectors.

"You believed being family gave you ownership of Rebecca," I said. "You believed her father's money was always going to come back to you, and when she refused, you took her life instead."

She stared straight ahead.

"Then you looked at your granddaughter and saw another account. You exposed an eight-year-old to something that could kill her because you wanted a judge to watch me fail."

Nothing moved in her face.

"I used to think your weapon was money. It wasn't. Your weapon was our belief that family problems stay inside the family."

She looked up.

"You were counting on our silence. Rebecca broke it three days before you killed her. Nell broke it from a hospital bed. Elliot broke it eleven days too late. It's finished."

I folded the letter and put it back in my pocket.

"You will never be near my daughter again."

Rosalind Hale received a sentence long enough that she is unlikely to be released in her lifetime.

Vale got a shorter one for cooperating, and lost his license permanently.

The hospital took her name off the children's wing within a month, issued a public apology, and wrote a new policy preventing donors from having any access to medical records or influence over internal investigations.

Elliot pleaded guilty to conspiracy, theft, fraud, obstruction, and his role in my wife's death. The cooperation shortened his sentence. It did not shorten it by much.

Before they transferred him, he asked to see me.

We met in a secure room with an officer standing by the door.

"I don't expect you to forgive me," he said.

"Good."

"I loved her."

"You watched her die."

His eyes filled. "I was frightened of Rosalind."

"So was Rebecca. She still tried to stop you."

He put his head down.

We sat there for a while.

"I keep thinking about the moment I could have called," he said.

"So do I."

"I had the phone in my hand."

I stood up.

"Don't ask me to make you feel better about that."

"I won't."

"If you write to Nell, her therapist decides whether she sees it. She decides whether she reads it. Not you, and not me."

"Will you ever visit?"

"I don't know," I said, and it was the honest answer.

Forgiveness was not something I owed him.

Neither was hatred.

Outside the courthouse, Reed was waiting at the bottom of the steps.

"It's over," he said.

"The trial is."

"That's what I meant."

Across the street, my daughter was standing beside Joanna's car with her coat unbuttoned. She had grown two inches since the luncheon. She saw me and waved with her whole arm, the way children do.

For the first time in a year, the future did not look like another room with somebody standing behind the curtain.
