My Mother-in-Law Swapped My Daughter's Medicine

Chapter 08 — The Letters

Chapter 8 of 10

Nell was twelve when she brought the envelopes home.

She was taller, louder, and starting middle school. She still carried three injectors, but she no longer checked a dessert with her hands shaking. We still did Sundays at the kitchen table. The routine had stopped being fear and turned into something closer to housekeeping.

The Rebecca Hale Initiative had grown into a small organization working with schools, clinics and family courts across the state. Joanna ran the legal side. Mason Reed had retired from the department by then but stayed on as an adviser, mostly so he could disagree with people in meetings. I sat on the board.

Nell attended events only when she wanted to. Nobody was allowed to put her photograph on a brochure or repeat her story without asking her first. She had already spent enough of her childhood being used by adults.

That Saturday morning I came into the kitchen and found her at the table with three sealed envelopes lined up in front of her.

"Dr. Brooks says I'm old enough to decide."

"Deciding not to read them is also deciding."

"I know." She touched the corner of the first one. "Do you think he's sorry?"

"I think he regrets it."

"That's not the same thing."

"No. It isn't."

"What if reading them makes everything bad again?"

"It might hurt."

"What if I never read them and I wonder for the rest of my life?"

"That might hurt too."

She looked up. "What would you do?"

"I'd burn them."

Her eyebrows went up.

"But that's my feeling," I said. "It doesn't have to become your decision."

She pushed all three into the drawer. "Not today."

"That's fine."

The phone rang about forty minutes later. It was Joanna, and she did not open with hello.

"Adrian, one of the old Vancroft accounts was accessed yesterday."

Elliot's shell company. Almost everything connected to it had been frozen after the trial.

"What kind of account?"

"A secure document archive. Somebody logged in and tried to delete four folders before the session was cut."

"Who?"

"We don't know. The login came from a machine inside the old accounting office of the Hale Family Foundation."

The foundation had dissolved after the conviction. Its board was gone. Its donations had all been audited.

"What's in the folders?"

"We can't see them. We don't have the original password."

"Does Elliot have it?"

"Almost certainly."

I looked at the kitchen drawer.

Nell had heard enough of it to get there before I did.

"The letters," she said.

I covered the phone. "You don't have to open anything."

"What if there's a password in them?"

"Then investigators can ask him."

"They already did, didn't they."

Joanna answered through the speaker. "Yes. He won't discuss the archive without his attorney present."

Nell's face changed. "He still wants something for it."

"Probably."

Elliot had cooperated at trial, but only after the evidence made cooperation the cheaper option. Even then he had kept things back. He treated the truth like currency. He spent exactly as much of it as he needed and no more.

"Let the police handle it," I said.

She kept looking at the drawer. "Someone's deleting evidence."

"That's still not your responsibility."

"What if it's Mom's evidence?"

I did not have a good answer for that, and she knew it.

Dr. Brooks came to the house that afternoon and sat with Nell while the three envelopes lay on the table between them.

"You are not opening these to rescue anyone," she said. "You open them only if you think knowing will help you."

"What if it helps somebody else too?"

"Then that's a good outcome. You're still allowed to stop halfway."

Nell opened the first one.

Elliot's handwriting had got worse.

Nell,

I know sorry is too small a word for this. I helped Rosalind hurt your mother. I helped her hurt you. I'm not going to ask you to forgive me.

She stopped there.

"Do you want me to read it?" I asked.

"No."

She read the rest of it silently. It was mostly memories — my wife bringing him groceries after he lost his job, my wife filling in his college application at our kitchen table, my wife telling me to give him the guest room when he had nowhere to go.

At the bottom he had written one paragraph that Nell turned around and slid across the table so I could see it.

Every meal I ate in your house after Rebecca died was a lie. Your father thought I was helping him survive it. I was making sure he never found out what it was.

Nell folded the page. "He knew Dad trusted him."

"Yes."

"He liked it."

"Yes."

She opened the second envelope.

That one was about money. Elliot listed transfers, most of which investigators had found years earlier.

Then he named a man.

Curtis Pike had been the Hale Family Foundation's accountant. He gave evidence during the original investigation that he had processed routine payments and known nothing. Nobody charged him.

Elliot wrote:

Curtis built the invoices. Rosalind paid him to keep two sets of books. One set for the auditors. One for us.

Underneath that paragraph was an account name.

And a password.

Joanna tried it from her office, on a machine the district attorney's technicians had already prepared.

The archive opened.

Folders came up across the screen.

HALE TRUST. VALE PAYMENTS. FOUNDATION. PRIVATE LEDGERS.

She closed it immediately without opening a single one, and called the financial crimes unit.

Nell was still holding the letter.

"Why did he send it to me?"

The answer was in the last paragraph.

I thought your father would destroy anything I sent. I thought you'd keep my letters even if you never opened them. I put it in your hands because I was still too frightened to carry it in mine.

She pushed the page away from herself.

"He used me again."

"Yes."

"He could have told the police any day for four years."

"Yes."

"He made me hold his secret and he never even asked me."

"Yes."

Her eyes filled. "I helped open it and I still hate that he did that."

"You're allowed both."

She looked at the third envelope. "I don't want to open that one."

"Then we won't."

I put it back in the drawer.

The archive came apart under a warrant inside a week. It proved Curtis Pike had built the false invoices, exactly as Elliot said.

It also proved something nobody had gone looking for.

Nell's trust had not been the only source.

Money had been taken out of hospital charity funds — accounts opened for children in long-term treatment, to cover travel, accommodation and lost wages while their families lived at a bedside.

Rosalind Hale had built a public reputation raising money for sick children.

She had also been taking it back out.

Investigators identified six families.

Some of those children were teenagers by then.

One of them had died before anyone noticed the money was missing.

The financial crimes unit froze Pike's accounts on a Tuesday.

By Thursday his office was empty, his house was cleared, and the airline had him on a one-way ticket leaving the following morning.

Reed called me that night.

"We pulled a message Pike sent to your brother three months ago. Came in through another inmate's family."

"What did it say?"

He read it out.

If the girl ever opens those letters, all of it collapses. Make sure Adrian never lets her.

I stood in my kitchen and looked down the hall at a yellow bedroom door.

Curtis Pike had known about the letters.

He had known my twelve-year-old daughter was holding the password.

And by then, he knew we had opened the account.
