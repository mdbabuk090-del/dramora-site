My Daughter Was Feeding Someone Through the Wall. The Child Who Crawled Out Called Me Dad.

Chapter 11 — Outside

Chapter 11 of 15

We did not go back to Ardmore.

I could not have done it and neither could either of them, and in any case the house was a crime scene for eleven days and after that it was evidence and after that it was a thing I sold in the spring for less than it was worth to a couple who asked no questions and who I assume found out within a week.

We went to my brother Deshaun's, which was ninety minutes away and had a garden.

Two girls, one father, one aunt, one uncle, four bedrooms, and a set of problems that no book covers.

The first thing was the sun.

Marlowe did not like being outdoors, and I had expected that, and I had it backwards. I thought it would be fear. Open space, agoraphobia, the standard picture.

It was not fear. It was pain. Actual physical pain — her eyes streamed, she got headaches, she said the sky was "loud". After thirty months in a box room with a fence-facing window, an ordinary overcast afternoon in a garden was a physical assault.

We did it in minutes. Four minutes on the back step on day one, with a hat. Six on day two. She kept the count herself, out loud, and got extremely irritated if anybody rounded up.

It took eleven weeks to get to an hour.

The paediatric ophthalmologist said she would be entirely normal within a year and she was.

The second thing was that she could not be alone in a room, and she could not be watched in a room, and there was a narrow band in between where she was all right.

The band was: someone else present, doing something else, not looking at her.

Deshaun worked that out before any professional did. He started doing his accounts at the kitchen table in the evenings instead of in the study, and Marlowe would come and sit at the other end with a colouring book, and neither of them would say anything for an hour and a half.

My brother did that four nights a week for eight months and never once mentioned it to me as a thing he was doing.

The third thing was the talking, and this is the one that surprised everybody.

Marlowe's vocabulary was enormous.

She had had books — Camille brought books, hundreds over three years, and Marlowe read all of them many times — and she had had three years of listening to a family through a wall. She had the vocabulary of a bright ten-year-old and the pronunciation of somebody who has read every word and heard almost none of them.

She said ep-it-ome. She said mis-led as two hard syllables. She said hyper-bowl.

She had strong opinions about books and had never had a conversation about one.

The speech therapist said it was the most straightforward case she'd had in a decade — there was nothing wrong with the machinery, the child simply needed to be talked to, in volume, by anybody, about anything, for about a year.

So we talked to her.

The whole house. Constantly. Deshaun's wife Ijeoma is a person who narrates her own cooking and it turned out to be a medical intervention.

The fourth thing was June, and the fourth thing was the hard one.

They had been introduced through a wall.

June had been coming to that vent for eleven months with crisps and whispering. Marlowe had known June's voice her whole life in that house. In a certain sense they were the only two people in the world who understood the situation from the inside.

And they could not be in a room together.

Not violence. Nothing dramatic. They would simply go silent and stiff, both of them, and drift to opposite walls, and stay there.

Fenwick said it was shame, running in both directions, and that it was the most predictable thing in the file and the slowest to fix.

June's version: I knew and I didn't tell. She had worked this out on her own by then, without anybody teaching it to her, and it had gone into her like a nail.

Marlowe's version was worse and took nine months to surface.

Marlowe believed she had ruined June's family.

She had been told, for three years, that if the secret came out everything would break. Then the secret came out and everything broke — the house, the mother, the family — exactly as promised. Camille's prediction had come true in every particular.

So Marlowe, at seven, concluded that she had done it. That she had said one word through a hole in a wall and destroyed a household, and that the girl who used to bring her crisps had lost her mother because of it.

The first time they were all right together was in March.

Ijeoma had the radio on and June was doing something at the counter and Marlowe came in and stood in the doorway, and June — without turning round, without making it a moment, and I am fairly sure without thinking about it at all —

said, "There's crisps in the top cupboard now. You can just have them."

And Marlowe said, "Okay."

And that was it. That was the whole thing. That was eleven months of therapy delivered in fourteen words by a seven-year-old at a kitchen counter.

I was in the hall and I did not go in, because I knew that if I went in it would stop.
