Chapter 15 — The Room at the End

Chapter 15 of 15

Marlowe is eleven now.

She is tall, which nobody predicted. She swims, badly and with total commitment, at a pool eight minutes from the house. She has a best friend called Wren who has been told a shortened version and who has never once asked a follow-up question, and I would do anything for that child.

She still reads constantly and she still says two or three words wrong, and she will not be corrected, and at some point in the last year the wrongness stopped being a symptom and became a joke the family has. She says ep-it-ome deliberately now. It is the funniest thing in our house.

June is twelve. She is louder than her sister and better at maths and worse at being alone. She writes to her mother on the first and the fifteenth. Two years ago she started signing them June H. instead of June, and I have never asked about it.

They share a bathroom and argue about it constantly, which the professionals tell me is the single healthiest fact in the file.

We moved out of Deshaun's after twenty months, into a house four streets away, which is a bungalow, which was not an accident.

Marlowe chose her room.

There were two available and I had assumed she would take the big one at the front with the bay window, because it has the most light and because for three years she had none.

She took the small one at the back.

I said, carefully, that the front one was bigger.

She said she knew.

I said the back one only has the one window and it faces the fence.

And she looked at me with an expression I recognised, because it is my mother's expression, and she said:

"Yeah. I like it."

I did not say anything else about it, and I did not raise it with Fenwick for a month, and when I did Fenwick laughed at me — actually laughed, out loud, for the only time in six years — and said, "Wes. She's allowed to like a small room. Some people like small rooms. She's had four hundred hours of therapy, she'll tell you if it's the other thing."

She did tell me, eventually. About a year later, in a car, because everything important in our family happens in a car.

She said the front room was too far from everybody.

She said in the back room she can hear the kitchen.

She said, "I like knowing where everyone is."

And I said that made sense, and we drove for a while, and then she said, without any change in her voice:

"That's the wall thing, isn't it."

I said probably, yes.

She said, "Is it bad?"

I said no. I said there are things that happened to you that we had to fix, and there are things that happened to you that just became part of how you're built, and telling the difference is most of the work, and this one is the second kind, and she gets to keep it.

She thought about that for about a mile.

Then she said, "Good, because I'm not moving."

I kept the photograph.

Not the one of me — that one is Marlowe's, it always was, and it lives in her room with the picture of Nadia in the hospital chair.

I kept the piece of paper that came out of the disclosure bundle. The forensic photograph of the crawl space floor. Wide boards, grey dust, and one patch by the vent worn down to clean pale wood.

I do not look at it often. It is in an envelope in a drawer with the deed to the bungalow and a copy of the letter from Portland.

I keep it because it is the only physical record of the thing that nobody else in the world would find remarkable and that I need to remain able to see: that for three years, in a house I paid for and walked through twice a day, a small person heard my voice in the morning and got up and crossed a dark space and sat down as close to it as she could get.

Every time.

Eleven hundred mornings.

And the wall was fourteen millimetres of plaster, and I could have gone through it with my hand.

I get asked, at the end, whether I forgive myself.

The honest answer is that the question doesn't fit. Forgiveness is for things you did. I didn't do anything — that's the whole problem, that's exactly the crime, and there is no ritual for it.

What I have instead is a habit.

When I come in the front door, I say, "I'm home," out loud, into the house, every single time, even when I can see that both of them are sitting right there in the kitchen looking at me.

June thinks it's ridiculous and tells me so.

Marlowe has never said one word about it in four years.

She just answers.
