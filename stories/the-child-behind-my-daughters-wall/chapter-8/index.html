My Daughter Was Feeding Someone Through the Wall. The Child Who Crawled Out Called Me Dad.

Chapter 08 — The Rule Was Not a Threat

Chapter 8 of 15

June was interviewed four times.

Not by police, after the first one. By a specialist, in a room with a two-way mirror and a box of Duplo that she was too old for and used anyway, and I watched two of those interviews from the other side of the glass with Raghavan standing next to me not saying anything.

The first thing that had to be established was how long June had known.

Everybody assumed three years. That is what the neighbours assumed, and the internet, later. The sister knew the whole time. It made a better story and it also made June a monster, which is a thing people are surprisingly willing to do to a seven-year-old.

June had known for about fourteen months.

She had found out the way children find out anything, which is by being where she wasn't supposed to be. She had come out of her room at night for water and heard her mother talking through a door. She had asked. And Camille — and this is the part I still cannot metabolise — Camille did not deny it.

Camille sat her four-year-old down. No: sat her six-year-old down, June was six by then. And explained that there was a girl in the house, and that she was a secret, and that the rule of the house was that Daddy must never be told.

She did not say why.

June was asked, on the recording, four separate times, in four different ways, whether she had ever been told why.

June said no. And then June said the thing that I have quoted in my head at every difficult moment since:

"You don't get told why. It's a rule."

The interviewer said, "Do you have other rules?"

And June listed them. No screens before homework. Shoes off in the hall. Don't touch the thermostat. Don't tell Daddy about Marlowe. Bed at eight, half eight on Fridays.

She said it in one breath. In the middle of a list.

I want to explain something about that, because I got it wrong for a long time and it made me cruel to my own child for about six weeks.

I thought June had chosen. I thought that somewhere in there was a moment where a little girl weighed up her father against her mother and chose her mother, and I could not stop thinking about it, and the way it came out of me was that I could not look at her.

The psychologist — her name was Dr Abiola Fenwick and I owe her a great deal — sat me down and drew a line on a piece of paper.

She said: a seven-year-old does not have a category called crime. She has a category called rules, and rules come from parents, and rules do not require justification, and rules are how a child knows the world is being managed by somebody competent. That is the whole point of them.

She said: you are angry that June didn't recognise this rule as different from the others. But there was nothing about it, from inside June's life, that marked it as different. It was delivered in the same voice, by the same person, in the same kitchen, as the rule about shoes.

She said: your wife did not ask your daughter to keep a secret. She installed it. Those are different operations.

Then she said the sentence that ended the six weeks.

"June didn't choose her mother over you. June has never once, in her entire life, been in a situation where those two things were on the table at the same time."

Here is what June did do, on her own, without being told, starting about eleven months before the wall came open.

She started taking food.

Not from meals. From the cupboard, after everyone was asleep. Crisps, mostly, because they were in a low cupboard and they didn't need a plate. Sometimes a sandwich if she could make one without the fridge light waking anybody.

And she started pushing it through the vent, because the vent was the only part of Marlowe she could reach without going down the hallway past our door.

Nobody asked her to do that. Camille didn't know. It came out in the third interview and I watched Camille's face when it was put to her at the hearing and it was the only time in the whole process she looked genuinely surprised.

Because Camille was feeding Marlowe. Properly. Three meals.

June's food was not necessary.

The interviewer asked June why she did it, if her mother was already giving Marlowe dinner.

And June — seven years old, in a room with a mirror, with a stranger writing things down — said:

"Because she was on her own."

That is the sentence, out of everything, that I would take out of the whole three years and keep.

My daughter, who had been handed a monstrous rule at six years old and had filed it next to shoes off in the hall, had also worked out entirely by herself that a person in a room with no other people in it needs someone to come at night.

She could not name what was wrong. She had no framework for it. She was not rebelling — she never once considered telling me, and she said so, and she was baffled by the question.

But she knew, at some level below rules, that a girl behind a wall should get something that wasn't dinner.

So she brought crisps.

The crisp packets in the crawl space were all recent. All bright, none faded. Forensics logged forty-one of them and dated the oldest to about thirteen months before.

Forty-one nights, out of eleven hundred.

That is what a seven-year-old could do against her mother, and she did it, and nobody has ever thanked her for it except me.
