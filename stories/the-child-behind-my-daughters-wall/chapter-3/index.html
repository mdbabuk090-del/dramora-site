Chapter 03 — What the Flashlight Found

Chapter 3 of 15

The beam went in low and hit a floor.

Not joists. Not insulation batts and dead air. A floor — rough boards laid across the joists, swept, with a folded blanket on it.

Somebody had made that space usable. Somebody had gone in there with boards.

The beam went right, across the boards, and picked up things in a sequence I could not make sense of quickly enough. A plastic cup. A child's shoe, one, on its side. Three crisp packets, bright and new-looking, the foil still shiny. A book with a soft cover, face down, open.

Then the beam came back left and found a leg.

A bare leg, small, folded, with a dirty knee.

I said, "Oh my God."

I did not decide to say it. It came out of me the way air comes out of a bag you sit on.

The leg moved. Pulled back an inch. And I moved the flashlight up.

She was sitting cross-legged with her back against a stud, about four feet in, and she was holding a teddy bear against her chest with both arms, and on top of the teddy bear she was holding the sandwich.

She had not eaten all of it. She had eaten about half. She had kept the rest.

She was seven, or near it. Her hair was strawberry blonde and it curled tight, and it had not been brushed in a long time — not days, longer, the kind of tangle that has become a structure. Her dress was off-white cotton and it was grey with dirt at the hem and at the knees. Her cheeks were smudged and there were clean lines down through the smudge on both sides, old ones and one new one.

Her eyes were pale grey-green.

They were my eyes. I did not think that then. I want to be honest about the sequence: I did not look at that child and think she has my eyes. That is the version people expect and it is not what happened. What happened is that I looked at a filthy, terrified seven-year-old in a hole in my wall and I thought, with total clarity and no context whatsoever, she is not afraid of me.

She was afraid. Her chin was going. Her shoulders were up around her ears. But she was not looking at me the way a child looks at a stranger who has just come through a wall with a hammer.

She was looking at me the way you look at something you have been waiting for and are not sure you're allowed to have.

Behind me, Camille had stopped talking.

That was the loudest thing in the house.

I got down onto both knees so my face was level with the opening. I put the flashlight on the floor and angled it up at the ceiling of the space so it wasn't in her eyes — I don't know where I learned to do that, it just seemed obviously right — and the whole little room went soft and orange, and I could see all of her.

Her feet were bare. Her toenails were long.

"Hey," I said. "Hey. It's okay."

She didn't move.

"Nobody's angry. Nobody's in trouble."

Her arms tightened on the bear.

I have replayed my own voice here too. I sounded, on the recording that exists in my head, like a man talking to a dog on a road. Careful and too bright and useless.

And then she looked past my shoulder.

She looked at Camille.

And every part of her that had been almost — almost leaning forward, almost unfolding — went back. Her spine straightened against the stud. Her eyes dropped to the boards. She became very, very still, in a practised way, the way June had closed her bedroom door with both hands so it wouldn't bang.

She was not doing it because she was frightened of Camille hurting her.

She was doing it because she had been told. Because there was a rule, and the rule was live in that hallway right now, and she was obeying it in front of me while I watched and did not yet know it was a rule.

"Marlowe," Camille said.

Quiet. Not a shout. A correction.

She used the child's name.

My wife stood in our hallway, looking through a hole she had told me to leave shut, and she used the child's name, and she said it the way you say a name you have said several thousand times.

I turned around and looked at her.

She had both hands over her mouth. Her eyes were shining and enormous and she was shaking her head, very slightly, on a loop.

She was not shocked.

She was caught. Those are different faces. I had seen her shocked before — her father, the phone call, 2019. This was not that face. This was the other one.

I turned back to the hole.

The little girl had lifted her head.

She was looking straight at me. Her mouth opened and it took her two goes to get any sound out at all, and when it came it was so quiet I felt it more than heard it.

"Daddy?"
