Chapter 10 — What the Statute Says

Chapter 10 of 15

Detective Raghavan came to the hotel on day six with a folder and a look on her face that I had not seen before, and she said, "I need to manage your expectations, and you are going to be angry with me."

I said fine.

She said: "You want kidnapping."

I said yes.

She said, "You're not going to get kidnapping. And I want you to hear why from me, sitting down, rather than from a headline."

The problem is that almost every serious charge in the book is built around a taking.

Kidnapping requires a seizure or a carrying away. Camille did not take Marlowe. Marlowe was delivered to her front door by an adult acting on the instructions of the child's own mother, and handed over, voluntarily, on the doorstep. There is no abduction anywhere in the timeline. The child arrived.

Unlawful imprisonment requires confinement against the person's will, and the defence was going to argue — and did argue, for four days — that a four-year-old who is told she is waiting to meet her father and who chooses to wait is not confined in the sense the statute means. There was no lock on the box room door. There has never been a lock on that door. Marlowe could have walked out of it at any hour of any day for three years.

That is the fact the defence built its entire case on, and I need to be honest and say that it is a real fact.

Raghavan said: "The lock was in her head. I know that. You know that. A jury may know it. But there are eleven other things I can charge that don't require me to prove a thing that happened inside a child's mind, and every one of them will stick."

What Camille was charged with, in the end:

Child endangerment, first degree. Multiple counts, because each period is chargeable and the prosecution stacked them by year.

Criminal neglect of a minor — this was the heavy one, and it did not require a taking or a locking. It required only that she assumed care of a child and then failed to provide medical care, education, and the basic conditions of a child's development. Three years without a doctor. Three years without a school. Thirty months without direct sunlight. That is not an argument, that is a chart.

Interference with custody.

Two counts of falsifying records, because she had twice signed forms about the household that required her to state the number of persons resident.

And, later, after the second search: concealment of a child from a parent, which is a statute that exists in eleven states and almost never gets used, and which Raghavan told me was written for exactly this and had been waiting for thirty years for a case to fit it.

The word wall does not appear in any of it.

I said the thing that everybody says, which was: three years.

Three years of a child's life, and the charge sheet reads like a licensing violation.

And Raghavan said something to me that I did not want at the time and that I have since repeated to two other people in similar rooms.

She said: "The law is not a scoreboard for how bad a thing was. It's a list of the things we've worked out how to prove. Those are different documents and it is going to feel, for about two years, like nobody understands what happened to your daughter. They do. They just have to say it in a language that survives an appeal."

There was a second thing in the folder and she put it on the table face down.

She said: "The other reason I'm here is your other daughter."

Because Camille was going to be charged, and Camille was June's mother, and June had, in the eyes of the state, been used. A minor had been enlisted into the concealment. That is its own offence, and it is charged against the adult, but it means the child sits in the middle of the file.

Raghavan said: "There will be an assessment of June. There has to be. And I want to tell you now, because a lot of fathers in your position say something in the first week that they can't take back — nobody in this building thinks June did anything wrong."

I said I knew that.

She said, "You will stop knowing it around week three. Everybody does. Call me when it happens."

I did call her, in week four, from a supermarket car park, having just heard myself say something to my own child that I am not going to write down.

She picked up on the second ring and she said, "There it is," and she stayed on the phone with me for fifty minutes, and it is the single greatest professional kindness anybody has ever done me.
