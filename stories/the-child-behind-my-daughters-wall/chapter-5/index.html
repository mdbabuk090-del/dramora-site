Chapter 05 — The Photograph

Chapter 5 of 15

It was a print. An actual photograph, on paper, the kind nobody has made in twenty years.

It had been carried. That was the first thing. The corners were rounded off from handling, one was creased right through, and the surface had that dull patchy gloss that photographs get when they have been held by hands a great many times.

She held it out toward me at arm's length, into the flashlight beam, and her hand was shaking, and she did not let go of it.

I had to lean into the hole to see it.

It was a boy. Seven, maybe eight. Standing against the mottled blue-grey backdrop that every school in America used in the early nineties. Collared shirt buttoned to the top by an adult who cared more than the child did. Tight strawberry-blonde curls. Pale grey-green eyes.

Me.

I have that photograph. My mother had it framed in the hallway of the house I grew up in, and when she died in 2016 I put four boxes of her things in my garage and did not open them, and one of those boxes had a brown envelope in it with about sixty loose prints.

It is not a subtle photograph. It is not a maybe. It is a school portrait of me at seven years old, and a seven-year-old girl was holding it in a hole in my wall, and she had the same hair and the same eyes and the same jaw and she had been carrying it long enough to wear the corners off.

I did not take it from her.

I want that in here. I reached out and my fingers got about halfway and I stopped, because she had both hands on it, and I understood — without being told, without anything — that this was hers.

"Can I look?"

She thought about it.

Then she let go of one side.

I turned it over. On the back, in blue ballpoint, in my mother's handwriting, which I would know anywhere on earth:

Wesley, second grade, 1995.

And underneath, in different ink, in handwriting I did not know:

This is your dad. His name is Wesley Halloran. He lives at 14 Ardmore.

I did not stand up. My legs were not available.

I sat down on the hallway floor with my back to the broken wall and the photograph in my hand and I looked at my wife.

Camille had not moved from where she was standing. She had her arms wrapped around herself.

"Whose child is she," I said.

"Wes—"

"Whose child is she."

And Camille said — and I will hear this in the specific tone she used until the day I die, because it was tired, and it was almost relieved, the way you sound when the thing you have been holding up finally comes down —

"She's yours."

There was a woman. Her name was Nadia Okonkwo-Reyes and I knew her for fourteen months, between 2017 and 2018, before Camille and I got serious. She was a paramedic. She worked nights. She wanted to move to Oregon and I did not.

It ended in a normal way. Badly, but normally. She did not tell me she was pregnant. I found out eight years later, in a conference room, that she had gone to Oregon after all, and that she had had a daughter in 2019, and that four years later she had been diagnosed with something that moves fast, and that she had spent the last nine months of her life trying to work out what to do with a child who had no other living relative.

She landed on me.

She had my mother's address, because it was the only address of mine she had ever had, and my mother was dead, and the house had been sold, and there is a version of this where the trail just ends there and Marlowe goes into the Oregon system with a photograph and a name.

But Nadia was thorough, or desperate, and she found 14 Ardmore.

In August 2023 a woman she had paid drove a four-year-old girl two thousand miles and put her on my front step at nine o'clock in the morning with a rucksack, a teddy bear, and a brown envelope with a photograph in it.

And rang the bell.

And I was at work.

I have gone over this more times than is good for a person, so let me just put it down plainly.

If I had been home, everything would have been fine.

Not fine. It would have been an earthquake. It would have been a terrible year. Camille and I might not have survived it and June's life would have been rearranged around a sister she didn't ask for.

But there would have been no wall.

The entire thing — three years, all of it — turns on the fact that on a Tuesday morning in August a doorbell rang at 14 Ardmore and the person who went to answer it was my wife.

She opened the door and there was a small girl standing on her step with strawberry-blonde curls and grey-green eyes, holding a photograph of my husband as a child, and she said — Camille told me this herself, later, in a room with a recording light on —

she said, "Is my dad here?"

And Camille said she looked at that child for what she thinks was about four seconds.

And then she said, "Come inside."
