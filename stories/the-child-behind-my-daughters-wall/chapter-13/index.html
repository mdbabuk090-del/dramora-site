Chapter 13 — The Fourth Day

Chapter 13 of 15

The trial ran eleven days and I attended nine of them, and I have very little memory of eight.

The fourth day I remember completely.

The fourth day was Camille.

Her attorney was a careful man called Ellsworth who did not do theatrics, and his strategy across the first three days had been consistent and, I have to say, effective. He never denied a fact. Not one. He agreed the child was in the house. He agreed the child had no doctor and no school. He agreed with the sunlight figure. He agreed with all of it.

What he did was keep saying one sentence, in different clothes, for three days:

This was not a cage. This was a woman who could not find the exit from a decision she made in four seconds.

And then he put her on the stand, which everybody told me he would not do.

The prosecutor was a woman called Mrs Adeyemi-Stark and she did not raise her voice once in eleven days.

She let Camille talk for a long time. Ellsworth walked her through the three years and Camille answered, and she cried twice, and neither time was performed — I was married to her for eleven years and I know what her performed crying looks like, and this was not it.

She said she had intended it to last a week.

She said she had spent the first month trying to find a way to tell me that did not end her marriage, and had not found one, and that after about six weeks the problem had changed shape: it was no longer how do I tell him about the child, it was how do I tell him about the six weeks.

She said, and this is verbatim from the transcript because I have read it more times than is healthy:

"Every day I didn't tell him, telling him got worse. It's a debt. It compounds. By the second year the interest was more than the loan."

Ellsworth asked her whether she had ever considered simply opening the door and walking out of the house and letting it happen.

And Camille said, "Every single day."

He asked her why she didn't.

And she said, "Because every single day it was still possible that I wouldn't have to."

Then Mrs Adeyemi-Stark stood up and she did not ask about any of that.

She asked about a Tuesday in March 2025.

Marlowe had had a fever of 39.8 for two days. Camille had treated it with children's paracetamol from the bathroom cupboard and cold flannels, and on the second night the child had been delirious and had not recognised her.

This was in the file because Camille herself had told police about it in her second interview, before she had a lawyer, and had never retracted it.

Mrs Adeyemi-Stark said: "On that night, did you consider taking her to a hospital?"

Camille said yes.

"What stopped you?"

And Camille said, "I didn't know what I would say."

Mrs Adeyemi-Stark let that sit.

Then she said, "Mrs Halloran, you have told this court that every day you did not act, action became harder. That the debt compounded. Do you agree that on that night, a six-year-old child with a temperature of thirty-nine point eight was, in your own words, the interest on that debt?"

Ellsworth objected. It was sustained.

It didn't matter. Everybody in the room had already heard it, including the jury, including me, including Camille, who had gone the colour of the wall behind her.

There was one more thing on the fourth day and it was not about Marlowe.

Mrs Adeyemi-Stark asked her about June.

She asked her to describe the conversation in which she told her six-year-old daughter that there was a girl in the house and that her father must never know.

Camille described it. Kitchen. After school. She had made toast.

Mrs Adeyemi-Stark said: "Did you tell her why?"

Camille said no.

"Why not?"

And Camille said — and this is the only moment in eleven days when I had to leave the room —

"Because if I'd told her why, she'd have known it was wrong. She only kept it because it didn't have a reason."

She said it as an explanation. She thought it was mitigation. She genuinely, sitting in that chair, believed she was describing an act of protection toward her daughter — keeping the moral weight off a six-year-old by giving her a bare instruction instead.

And she was, in a way. That's what makes it unbearable.

She had thought about it carefully.

She had worked out that a rule without a reason is easier for a child to carry than a rule with one.

And she had used that.
