My Daughter Was Feeding Someone Through the Wall. The Child Who Crawled Out Called Me Dad.

Chapter 06 — Three Years

Chapter 6 of 15

I did not say any of the things a man is supposed to say.

I did not shout. I did not throw the hammer. There is a version of this in people's heads where I stood up and put my hand through a door, and I understand why that version is more satisfying, but the truth is I stayed on the floor.

I looked at the photograph. Then I looked at the hole. Then I looked at my wife.

And I said one sentence.

"You kept my daughter in this house for three years."

Not a question. There was nothing left to ask. Every piece had arrived: the child, the name, the photograph, the handwriting, she's yours, and the word tonight, and leave it shut, and the way June had looked down when her mother came up the stairs.

Camille said, "It wasn't like that."

I said, "There is a hole in my wall and there is a child in it."

"She had a room, Wes."

That was the first crack in my understanding of the shape of it, and I did not have room for it that night, but it turned out to be the axis the whole case turned on.

There is a door at the end of the upstairs hall that goes into what the previous owners used as a box room. It has a sloped ceiling and one small window that faces the neighbour's fence. We put a chest of drawers in it in 2015 and then we put the door on a hook and stopped going in, the way you do with a room you don't need.

Camille had cleared it out in the autumn of 2023.

There was a bed in there. A proper bed, with sheets that were changed. There were clothes in the chest of drawers, and they were the right size, and they had been replaced twice as the child grew. There were books. There was a night light. There was a plastic potty for the first year and after that Camille had worked out a schedule for the upstairs bathroom that ran on the hours I was at work.

Marlowe did not live in a wall.

Marlowe lived in a room, at the end of the hallway I walked down twice a day, and the wall was just the part of it I could hear.

I need to say this carefully, because for about six months I told the story the other way — my wife kept a child in a wall — and every time I told it that way I was, without meaning to, making it better. A monster in a fairy tale keeps a child in a wall. A monster in a fairy tale can be understood at a glance and stopped.

What Camille did was worse and much harder to look at.

She raised her.

Badly, secretly, criminally, without a doctor or a school or a single other human being knowing that child was alive — but she got up every day for three years and fed a little girl and washed her clothes and cut her hair and put her to bed, and then came downstairs and had dinner with me.

The wall was not the prison. The wall was where a child who was not allowed to be seen went to be near the sound of her father.

I asked her why.

Not that night — that night I asked nothing else, because I got up off the floor and I went and got a blanket out of the airing cupboard and I put it around Marlowe and I carried her down the stairs and out to the car, and Camille followed me down the drive saying my name, and I did not answer her, and I drove to St Eligius with a seven-year-old in the back seat holding a teddy bear and half a sandwich.

I asked her four months later, through a lawyer, in a room with a table.

And Camille said: "Because I opened the door."

I said that wasn't an answer.

She said, "You weren't there. You've never once had to be the person who opens the door."

She said she looked at that child on the step for four seconds and in those four seconds she understood the entire rest of her life. That the child was mine. That the child was proof of a woman before her. That I would take her in, because of course I would, and that from that day forward Camille would be the second wife raising the first woman's daughter, and every parent-teacher evening and every Christmas and every time that child's face did something my face did, everybody in the room would know.

She said: "I wasn't going to be that. I told myself I'd find somewhere for her. And then it was Thursday. And then it was October."

I said, "It was three years."

And she said the only thing she has ever said about it that I believe completely:

"I know exactly how long it was."

But that is four months ahead.

On the night itself I came out of the front door with a child in my arms and the first thing that happened was that June was standing at the top of the stairs in her pyjamas.

She was not crying.

She looked at Marlowe in the blanket. Then she looked at me. And she said, in a voice of pure, cold, seven-year-old dread:

"Am I in trouble?"

And that is when I understood that the wall was going to be the easy part.
