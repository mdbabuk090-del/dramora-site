Chapter 14 — Six to Fourteen

Chapter 14 of 15

The jury was out for two days and returned guilty on every count except one of the falsification charges.

The sentence was six to fourteen years.

I have been asked, more than once, whether I was satisfied. It is a question people ask because they want to be told a number that closes the account, and there isn't one. Six is not enough and forty would not be enough and the failure is not in the arithmetic, it's in the idea that there is an arithmetic.

What I felt, sitting in that room when it was read, was almost nothing.

And then, about four seconds later, an enormous and shameful wave of relief — not that she had been punished, but that it was over, that no more days of my life would be spent in that building.

I have made peace with that. Fenwick says everybody gets it and almost nobody admits it.

June was in the car park with Ijeoma. She was eight by then.

She asked me what happened and I told her, in the words we had agreed with Fenwick in advance, which were: your mum is going to be away for a long time, at least six years, and you're going to be able to write to her, and none of it is because of anything you did.

June said, "Six years is when I'm fourteen."

I said yes.

She said, "That's not that long."

And then she cried for about forty minutes in the back of a car in a car park in a way I had not seen her cry once in two years, and I sat in the back with her, and afterwards she fell asleep against the door and Ijeoma drove us home at fifty miles an hour the whole way with the radio off.

June writes to her mother every two weeks. This was the hardest decision I have ever made and I made it in favour, on Fenwick's advice, against my own entire instinct.

Fenwick's argument was simple and I could not beat it. She said: June has one mother. That mother committed a serious crime against June's sister and used June to do it. All of that is true, and none of it stops being true if you cut off contact — you just add abandonment to it, and you make the mother into a myth, and at fourteen June will go and find the myth on her own with no adult in the room.

She said: let her have the real one. The real one is worse than the myth and that is the point.

So June writes. I do not read the letters going out. I do read the ones coming in, and June knows I do, and that was her idea, not mine.

The letters back are — I have to be fair — good letters. Camille asks about school. She does not ask about Marlowe. She has never once, in the whole correspondence, asked about Marlowe, and June noticed that entirely on her own at age ten and was extremely angry about it for a month, and that anger has done more work than four years of therapy.

Marlowe has never written and has never been asked to.

She was interviewed, on video, once, and it was played to the court, and that was her total involvement. She did not attend a single day. She had turned eight six weeks before it started and I was not going to put her in that building.

She has asked about Camille exactly twice.

The first time, at eight: "Is she in prison because of me?"

We had eleven months of work ready for that question and it still went off like a grenade.

The second time, at ten, and this one nobody had prepared me for:

"Was she nice to me?"

Because that is the thing that will not resolve for her, and I don't think it ever fully will. Every adult in Marlowe's life for three years was Camille. Every meal, every book, every clean set of clothes, every time she was ill. There is no version of her childhood before seven that does not have that woman in the middle of it being kind to her.

And it was all a wall.

I said: she was kind to you, and she was doing something terrible to you, and both of those are true at once, and you are allowed to miss the first one. You do not owe anybody the tidy version.

Marlowe said, "Okay," and went back to her book, and three days later at dinner said, out of nothing, "I don't miss her. I miss the room."

Which I think is the most sophisticated thing anybody has said about the whole affair.

There is one more piece of the legal file and then I'm done with it.

In the second year, Camille's parents petitioned for grandparent visitation with June. It was granted, and it works, and they have June for a fortnight every summer.

They also, in the same filing, made an application in respect of Marlowe.

They wanted contact with her. On the basis that she had lived in their daughter's household for three years and that they had, in that period — this is the phrase in the document — "an established familial connection."

They had never met her.

They did not know she existed until it was on the news.

The judge dismissed it in under a minute, and Raghavan, who had no professional reason to be in that room, was in that room, and she caught my eye on the way out and did not say anything at all.
