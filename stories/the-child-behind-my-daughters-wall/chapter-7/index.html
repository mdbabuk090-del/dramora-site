My Daughter Was Feeding Someone Through the Wall. The Child Who Crawled Out Called Me Dad.

Chapter 07 — St Eligius, 12:40 a.m.

Chapter 7 of 15

The triage nurse was called Bettina and she had been doing the job for nineteen years and I watched her face while I explained, and her face did nothing at all, which I have never forgotten and have always been grateful for.

I said: this is my daughter. I found her tonight. I believe she has been in my house for three years. I did not know she existed.

Bettina said, "Okay," and picked up the phone.

She did not ask me a single question. Later I asked her why and she said, "Because you had her in a blanket and she had her hand in your shirt. I could ask questions after."

They took Marlowe into a room and I was not allowed to go in, and I stood in a corridor at one in the morning holding a teddy bear and a photograph of myself.

A police officer arrived at 1:20. Then a second one. Then, at about three, a woman in a grey cardigan who introduced herself as Detective Priya Raghavan and who did not shake my hand, which I understood.

She took me into the family room with the bad sofa and the wall clock and she said, "Before anything else. Where is your wife right now?"

I said I didn't know. I hadn't looked.

She said, "And your other daughter?"

And my stomach went out from under me, because in the four hours since the hammer I had not thought about June once.

June was in the house. With Camille. Where I had left her.

They found them both at the kitchen table at 3:40 in the morning. Camille had made June a hot chocolate. She had not called anybody, she had not packed a bag, she had not run. She had cleaned up the plaster in the hallway. There is a photograph in the file of the upstairs hall taken at 4 a.m. and the floor is swept and the broken lath is stacked in a neat pile against the skirting board and the hole is just — there, in a clean hallway, like something being renovated.

Detective Raghavan told me that. She told me because she wanted to watch me hear it.

I have never been able to decide what the sweeping means. I've had three professionals give me three different answers. The one I believe is the least dramatic: that Camille has kept that house immaculate for eleven years, and at four o'clock in the morning with her life ending she did the thing her hands knew how to do.

The medical part took two days and I will keep it short, because the details belong to Marlowe and she is going to be able to read this one day.

She was small for seven. Not starved — Camille had fed her, genuinely fed her, three meals — but the wrong shape, because she had not run. Nobody who has read the paediatric report ever forgets the sentence about her legs.

She had a vitamin D deficiency that they described as profound. She had not been in direct sunlight, they estimated, for something in the region of thirty months. Her hearing was excellent. Her eyesight was fine at distance, which surprised them, and I have thought about it: the box room window faces a fence, but past the fence there is sky.

She had never been to a dentist. She had one adult tooth coming in crooked.

She had no immunisation record and no medical record and no birth certificate in this state, and for about nine days there was a genuine administrative question about whether Marlowe legally existed. The Oregon record was found on day nine. Marlowe Okonkwo-Reyes, born March 2019, Portland. Mother: Nadia Okonkwo-Reyes. Father: unknown.

Nadia had left the father field blank.

Raghavan told me that gently, which I appreciated, and I said, "Why would she do that?" and Raghavan said, "Sometimes people leave it blank because they don't know. And sometimes they leave it blank because they want the child to be able to find him without the state getting there first."

The DNA came back on a Friday.

99.99 percent. The letter says "the alleged father cannot be excluded." which is the most cowardly sentence ever written by a laboratory.

I read it in the car park.

Then I went upstairs to the ward and sat down next to the bed and Marlowe was awake, and I did not know how to say it, and in the end I said:

"I got a letter today that says I'm your dad."

And she said, without any surprise at all, "I know."

And then, after a moment, with real concern, because she was seven and this had clearly been bothering her more than any of the rest of it:

"Am I allowed to say it now?"
