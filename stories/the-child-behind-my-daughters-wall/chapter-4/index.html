My Daughter Was Feeding Someone Through the Wall. The Child Who Crawled Out Called Me Dad.

Chapter 04 — Why Did She Say That

Chapter 4 of 15

Here is what I understood in that moment: nothing.

I want that on the record, because of what came later, because of what was said in the hearing and what was said to my face on a driveway by a woman I had never met. People need the father to have known. It makes the story survivable. If he knew, then the world still works; there was a lie and now there's a truth and the machine turns over.

I did not know.

A seven-year-old girl in a hole in my wall said Daddy and my entire mind produced one flat, stupid thought, which was: she has me confused with someone.

That is genuinely what I thought. That she was lost, that she was ill, that she had been in the dark long enough to be confused, that some other man had put her there and she was mistaking me for him in bad light.

I did not turn around. I want to be exact. My face stayed pointed at the hole, at her, because some animal part of me had worked out that if I looked away she would go back to being still, and I said it out into the hallway to my left, where my wife was.

"Why did she say that?"

She. Not you.

I have been asked about that too. A lawyer asked me, in a small room with a whiteboard, why my first sentence after a hidden child called me her father was a question about grammar rather than an accusation.

Because I had not got there yet. Because you did this requires knowing what this is. I had a child, a hole, a hammer, my wife eight feet behind me, and a word.

The word was doing something to my chest that I had no name for.

Camille didn't answer.

I heard her breathe in to start a sentence and then not start it. Twice.

"Camille. Why did that child call me Daddy."

"Wes, please—"

That is the entire content of what my wife said in the first ninety seconds after the wall came open. Wes, no. Leave it shut. Marlowe. Wes, please.

Nine words. Four of them my name.

Not one of them was I don't know.

If she had said I don't know I would have believed her. That is the thing that gets me at four in the morning, still, years later. She had the option. It was right there. She could have said Wes, I have no idea who that is, and I would have believed her, because I had believed her about everything for eleven years, and the police would have come and it would have been a long strange night and she would have had a fighting chance.

She didn't take it.

She said please.

You only say please to someone who has power over an outcome you are dreading. Please is a confession with the content removed.

The little girl was watching my face.

That is the thing about the whole night that I did not appreciate until much later, when a child psychologist explained it to me in language I hated.

Marlowe was not waiting to be rescued.

Marlowe had made a calculation. She had broken a rule — she had made a sound, she had said the forbidden thing, in front of the person she had been told must never know — and she had done it, and now she was watching my face to find out what it had cost her.

That's why she didn't move toward me. That's why she didn't cry out or reach. She had spent the only chip she had, and she was reading the table.

"Sweetheart," I said. "Can you tell me your name?"

Her lips moved.

"Marlowe."

"Marlowe." I said it back. "That's a good name. I'm Wes."

Something went across her face that I did not understand for a week.

Disappointment.

Small, and quickly hidden, and completely unmistakable.

I found out later what it was. She had known me as a voice for three years — through the wall, through the floor, through the vent, my voice in that hallway every morning and every night, June saying Daddy to it. She had built a person around that voice.

She had never once heard the voice say its own name.

So I'm Wes was new information, and it was the wrong kind. It made me a stranger with a name instead of the thing she had been assembling in the dark. For one second she thought she had got it wrong.

Then she looked down at her lap, at the teddy bear, and she did the thing that ended my marriage and everything else.

She lifted the bear an inch, and from underneath it she took out a photograph, and she held it up.
