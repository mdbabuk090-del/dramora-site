My Daughter Was Feeding Someone Through the Wall. The Child Who Crawled Out Called Me Dad.

Chapter 02 — Leave It Shut

Chapter 2 of 15

Camille came up the stairs fast and stopped about eight feet short of me.

That distance matters. I have thought about that distance.

A woman who does not know what is happening runs to her husband. A woman who does not know what is happening says what's going on. Camille stopped at a range where she could see both me and the wall, and she put her hands out, palms down, the way you gentle a dog.

"Wes. Come downstairs."

"June says there's someone in the wall."

"June is seven."

"June is seven and she made a sandwich."

Camille's eyes went to June for less than a second, and June — this is the part I cannot get out of my head — June looked down.

Seven years old. Caught. Not by me. By her mother.

"Go to your room, sweetheart," Camille said.

And June got up and went. No argument, no but Mom, no dragging. She walked past me and she did not look at the vent again and she closed her bedroom door behind her with both hands so it wouldn't bang.

That is not a child obeying a request. That is a child executing a drill.

"There's an animal in there," Camille said. "There's been a raccoon in the roof space since the spring. I've called someone twice."

"You've never mentioned it."

"I've mentioned it. You don't listen when I talk about the house."

That was true, and she knew it was true, and it was the smartest thing she said all night. It landed on the exact bruise. For a full second I stood there in my own hallway and considered whether I was a man who did not listen.

Then I heard it again.

Not chewing this time. A shift. Weight moving on a wooden floor, a small body adjusting, and then a breath — an indrawn breath that caught in the middle, the way a person breathes in when they have been crying for a long time and have got as far as quiet.

Raccoons do not do that.

I stood up.

"Wes—"

"What's back there?"

"Nothing's back there."

"What's back there, Camille."

And she said, "Nothing you need tonight."

I have been asked, by police, by lawyers, by a therapist I saw for eight months, why I went and got the hammer instead of calling someone.

It is a reasonable question and it has a stupid answer.

I went and got the hammer because it was in the utility room, forty feet away, and the police were twenty minutes away, and there was a sound in my wall that had breathed in like a person.

I also went and got the hammer because of the word tonight.

Nothing you need tonight.

That is not a denial. A denial is there's nothing there. What she said was a scheduling proposal. She was asking me to postpone. Somewhere between the bottom of the stairs and the top, my wife had done the arithmetic and concluded that her best available move was to buy one more night.

Which meant there was something to buy a night for.

I went down. I got the hammer and the long flashlight out of the utility drawer. Camille followed me the whole way, talking, and I could not tell you one word of it. I remember the shape of it. Reasonable, then faster, then not reasonable.

At the bottom of the stairs she got in front of me and put a hand flat on my chest.

"If you go up there," she said, "you don't get to undo it."

I want to record that accurately, because everyone assumes she said don't out of panic. She didn't. That sentence was the truest thing anybody said in that house in eleven years, and she said it deliberately, and she was right.

I went up.

I knelt down at the knee wall, about a foot to the left of the vent, and I put the flashlight on the floor pointing at the skirting so I could see what I was doing.

Camille was behind me and to the left. She was saying my name.

I hit the wall.

Plaster is nothing. People think a wall is a serious object. A hammer goes through a plasterboard-and-lath knee wall like it's going through a cake. The first hit made a hole the size of a fist and a puff of grey dust and a sound much louder than I expected.

And from inside — instantly, before the dust had settled — a sound came out.

A small, high, indrawn gasp. And then nothing. A very deliberate nothing. The sound of something in there deciding, at speed, to be silent.

That was when I stopped being frightened of what was in the wall.

I hit it again.

Camille screamed, "Leave it shut!"

Four words. Present tense. Leave it shut — not there's nothing in there, not stop, you'll hurt yourself, not Wes, please, it's just a raccoon.

Leave it shut means it is currently shut and I would like it to remain that way.

I pulled the broken pieces out with my hand. Lath splintered. Plaster came away in sheets. The hole went from a fist to a bucket to something a person could get their shoulders through, and cold air came out of it — colder than the hallway, and old, and dusty, and underneath the dust there was a smell that was not a house smell.

It was the smell of somewhere someone had been living.

I picked up the flashlight.
