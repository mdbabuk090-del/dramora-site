My Daughter Was Feeding Someone Through the Wall. The Child Who Crawled Out Called Me Dad.

Chapter 09 — The Promise

Chapter 9 of 15

Marlowe's silence had a different engine, and it took a specialist eight sessions to get it out of her, and when I finally understood it I had to leave the building.

June had been given a rule.

Marlowe had been given a promise.

Camille never threatened her. Not once. There is no evidence of it anywhere in three years, and Marlowe, questioned by people who are very good at asking, never produced any. No hitting. No shouting. No dark, no cold, no going without.

What Camille told her, in the first week, when Marlowe was four years old and had just been driven two thousand miles and left on a step, was this:

Your daddy is in this house. He doesn't know about you yet. He isn't ready. If he finds out before he's ready, they'll come and take you away and you'll never get to meet him. So we have to be quiet. And one day, when he's ready, I'll tell him, and then you can come out.

That is it. That is the whole mechanism.

It is not a threat. A threat says if you speak I will hurt you, and a threat can be resisted, and a child who is threatened knows, at some level, that they are being wronged.

This did not say that. This said: the thing you want most in the world is on the other side of your silence, and every day you stay quiet is a day closer to getting it.

She was not being punished for making noise.

She was being asked to earn her father.

Dr Fenwick explained to me why this is worse, and she was careful about it, because she could see what it was doing to me.

She said: a threatened child builds resistance. It costs them enormously, but the wrongness is legible. They know the adult is the problem.

She said: a child in Marlowe's position cannot locate the adult as the problem, because the adult is the helper. Camille wasn't the wall. Camille was the person managing the process of getting her out. Every day Camille came upstairs, Marlowe was seeing her ally.

She said: and the worst of it is the deferral. There is no failure state. It never resolves into this isn't working. It's always not yet. A child can hold not yet for a very long time.

I said, "For three years?"

She said, "For three years, and she'd have held it for six."

I asked, eventually, the question I had been carrying since the first night.

I asked how Marlowe knew my voice.

Because that had never made sense. The photograph explained the face, or the boy's face — but she had not recognised me from it, and she said so herself, and of course she hadn't: I am thirty-eight and the photograph is of a child.

She recognised the voice.

The box room shares a wall with the top of the stairs. Every morning at 7:10 for three years I stood at the bottom of those stairs and shouted up that June was going to be late. Every night I read to June through a door twelve feet from where Marlowe was lying.

She had three years of me. Just no face.

And whenever June said Daddy to that voice, Marlowe learned which voice was the one she was waiting for.

Fenwick asked her, in the sixth session, what she used to do when she heard it.

Marlowe said she used to go and sit by the vent.

Not to listen through it. The vent is at floor level in the hallway and the hallway is where the voice was. She went and sat next to it because it was the nearest she could get.

Fenwick asked how often.

And Marlowe, who was seven, and who did not know that this was a remarkable answer, said:

"Every time."

There is a detail from the physical search that I did not learn for six months, and I learned it by accident, from a photograph in a disclosure bundle.

The rough boards in the crawl space were dusty everywhere except one place.

There was a patch by the vent, about the size of a seat cushion, that was worn clean. Bare wood. Polished, almost, the way a step in an old church is polished.

Three years of a small person sitting in the same place.

The prosecutor put that photograph in front of the jury and did not say anything about it for eleven seconds, and I have never seen a room do what that room did.

The last thing.

At the hospital, on the second night, I asked Marlowe if she had ever thought about just shouting.

Just once. Just yelling through the vent, at that voice, in the hallway.

She said yes.

I asked her why she didn't.

And she said, "Because then I'd have made him find out before he was ready. And it would have been my fault."

She was seven years old and she had spent three years protecting me from herself.
