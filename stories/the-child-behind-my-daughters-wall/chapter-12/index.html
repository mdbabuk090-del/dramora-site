My Daughter Was Feeding Someone Through the Wall. The Child Who Crawled Out Called Me Dad.

Chapter 12 — Nadia

Chapter 12 of 15

The envelope came from an attorney's office in Portland in November, forwarded three times, and it had been sitting in a file since 2023 with instructions to release it to Wesley Halloran on proof of paternity.

It had been waiting three years for a letter that I only generated because I put a hammer through a wall.

Inside was a single sheet, handwritten, and a second photograph.

I am not going to reproduce all of it. Some of it is Nadia's and stays hers.

But the relevant part reads:

I'm not going to pretend this is fair to you. I thought about it for a long time and there's no version where it's fair to you.

I didn't tell you in 2018 because you'd have done the right thing and I didn't want the right thing, I wanted Oregon. That was mine and I own it.

I'm telling you now because I'm out of options and she has nobody. Not a nobody like a hard time. A nobody like a form with no next of kin on it.

She knows about you. I've told her since she could understand it. I want you to know she's not arriving frightened of you, she's arriving expecting you, and if you turn her away that's the thing that will do the damage, not the wall of it.

Her name is Marlowe. She reads early. She's stubborn like your mother.

Don't tell her I was sorry. Tell her I was late.

The second photograph was Nadia, taken in a hospital chair, thin, in a headscarf, holding a four-year-old on her lap.

Marlowe has this photograph now. It is in a frame beside her bed. It is the only picture she has of her mother's face, because everything else went into a rucksack that was in Camille's loft for three years, and the loft got damp in 2024.

That is the thing about this that nobody puts in the summary. Camille kept the rucksack. She didn't destroy it, which is what a person covering up a crime does. She put it in the loft.

And then she let it get wet.

Not out of malice. Out of the ordinary, domestic, unbearable fact that it was in the loft and the loft leaked and she did not go up and check, because going up and checking would have meant thinking about it.

Sixteen photographs, a birth bracelet, and a child's drawing, destroyed by a roof.

The photograph Marlowe has now survived only because it was in a lawyer's file in Oregon.

There is a line in the letter I have not been able to leave alone.

She's arriving expecting you.

Nadia's whole plan rested on one assumption: that the person who opened the door would be me.

She had no reason to think otherwise. She had my mother's address, and she had traced it forward to Ardmore, and as far as she knew she was sending a child to her father's house.

She did not know there was a second wife.

She did not know that at nine o'clock on a Tuesday morning in August I would be forty minutes away on a job in Barrow Creek, and that the doorbell would be answered by a woman who had never heard the name Nadia Okonkwo-Reyes and who would understand the entire situation in four seconds without a single word being said.

The whole thing is a coin.

I have stopped saying that out loud, because when I said it to Fenwick she stopped me and said, "No. Ninety-nine women out of a hundred open that door and the child sleeps in the spare room that night and everybody's life is hard for a year. It isn't a coin. It's a coin and a person."

I read the letter twice in the car and then I went in and sat on the end of Marlowe's bed, and I said I'd had a letter from her mum.

She went completely still, which is what she does.

I read her the parts she was allowed to have. I skipped two lines. I will give her those when she is older and she knows they exist because I told her they exist, which Fenwick says is the correct way to do it.

When I got to the end she said, "Read the last bit again."

Don't tell her I was sorry. Tell her I was late.

She thought about it for a while.

Then she said, "That's not the same thing."

I said no, it isn't.

She said, "Which one is better?"

And I said I didn't know, and she said, "I think late is better," and she took the photograph out of my hand and looked at it for a long time and then she asked if she could keep it in her room, as though it were mine to lend.
