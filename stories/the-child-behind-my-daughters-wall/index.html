Chapter 01 — She Eats After Dark

Chapter 1 of 15

My daughter was kneeling on the hallway floor at eleven o'clock at night, pushing a sandwich into the wall.

I want to be careful about the order of things, because the order is the whole story.

I did not walk in on something monstrous. I walked in on my seven-year-old in pink pyjamas, sitting back on her heels in the upstairs hallway, feeding an air vent.

There is a low vent at the base of that wall. White metal, horizontal slats, about the size of a dinner plate. It has been there since we bought the house. I could not have told you, that night, whether it was connected to anything.

June had a sandwich in both hands. She had folded it lengthways to make it narrower and she was working it through the slats with her thumbs, patiently, the way a child works a coin into a slot.

"June."

She didn't jump.

I keep coming back to that. A child caught doing something strange jumps. A child caught doing something forbidden freezes and then produces a story. June did neither. She turned her head and looked up at me and her face was completely calm.

"June, who's that for?"

"She eats after dark."

She said it the way you'd say the bins go out on Tuesday. Flat. Slightly impatient, even, as if I were asking about a thing that had been true for a long time and that I should already know.

She.

Not it. Not the mouse. Not nobody.

I remember the specific sensation of my own body getting the information before I did. My arms went cold from the elbow down. I heard the fridge cycle on downstairs. I heard my wife's foot on the bottom stair.

"Who eats after dark?"

June looked back at the vent.

Then she did something I have thought about every day since. She glanced past me, down the hallway, toward the top of the stairs. Checking.

Not checking if she was in trouble.

Checking if her mother was coming.

We had lived in that house eleven years. It is a timber-frame two-storey on a street where every third house is the same house. Dark hardwood on the second floor because the previous owners refinished it and we could never afford to redo it. Cream walls. One ceiling light in the hallway on a dimmer that never went all the way up.

Along one side of the upstairs hall the roof comes down, so there's a knee wall — a short wall, chest height on me, built where the slope meets the floor. Behind a knee wall there is always something. Usually nothing worth having. Insulation, joists, a long triangular dead space under the roof.

Ours was big. I knew that in the way you know a fact about your own house without ever having tested it. When we moved in, the inspector had said there was a decent crawl area back there and that it was dry.

I had never been in it. Not once in eleven years. There was no reason to.

I put my hand on the vent cover.

"Daddy, don't."

That was the first time June's voice changed.

Not scared for herself. Scared for something. She put her hand on my forearm — a seven-year-old's hand, nothing, no weight at all — and she said it again, quieter.

"Don't. You'll get her in trouble."

I have replayed the next ninety seconds more times than I have replayed any other ninety seconds of my life, including the ones in the hospital, including the ones in the courtroom.

I crouched down. I put my ear near the slats.

At first there was nothing. The house noise. The furnace. My own blood.

Then, from somewhere back and to the left, deeper in than the wall had any right to be — a small, careful sound.

Chewing.

Someone in there was eating the sandwich.

I said, out loud, in my own house, in a voice I did not recognise:

"Who's in the wall?"

And from the bottom of the stairs, before I had finished the sentence, before I had even stood up, my wife said my name.

Not what?

Not what are you talking about?

She said, "Wes, no."

She knew what I was asking. She knew what the answer was. She had known for three years, and she had eleven seconds of stairs to decide what to do about it, and what she decided to do was tell me to stop.

Synopsis

Wes Halloran breaks open a knee wall in his own upstairs hallway and finds a seven-year-old girl sitting in the dark with a teddy bear and a sandwich. She has been in the house for three years. She calls him Daddy before anyone has told her his name, and the only thing she owns is a yellowed photograph of a curly-haired boy — a photograph Wes recognises, because the boy in it is him. What follows is not a search for who put her there. Everyone in the hallway already knows. It is the slower, worse question of how a woman kept a child inside a house for three years, and how two little girls were taught that it was normal.
