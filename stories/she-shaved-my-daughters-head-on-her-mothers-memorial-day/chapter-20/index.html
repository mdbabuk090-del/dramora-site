She Shaved My Daughter's Head an Hour Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 20 — The Memorial That Happened

Chapter 20 of 25

We held it on the fourteenth of March, six months and three days late.

Not because of the anniversary of anything. Because Ella said, in the last week of February, at the table, with a fork in her hand:

"When are we doing Mum's thing?"

That was the first time she had mentioned it in five months and two weeks. I had been told by Ruth not to raise it and had followed that instruction with the discipline of a man who has been told the one thing he is allowed to do is nothing.

I said: "Do you want to?"

She said yes.

I said: "It doesn't have to be big."

And my daughter, eight years old, said: "It does though."

Ninety-one people, near enough. Some had moved, one had died — Iris's cousin's husband, of a heart attack in the January, which is the kind of thing that happens while you are busy.

I sent the same list a second letter. I have kept a copy and it is four lines long and it does not apologise.

Every single person came except four.

The ones who did not come did not come for ordinary reasons. Nobody stayed away because of the story. I had expected — I had prepared myself, in some detail, at two in the morning on several occasions — for a room half full of people who had spent six months hearing versions.

It was full.

We did it at the house, in the long room, with the doors open onto the lawn, and it stayed dry, which the family has now decided is a thing Iris does.

Different florist. Same order of service — the same ninety-one folded cards, out of the workshop safe, with her photograph on the front and Reading — Ella Ashworth on the second page, which I had had reprinted and then, at about eleven o'clock at night three days before, changed my mind and took the old ones back out of the safe.

I asked Ella which.

She looked at both. She said: "The old ones. They've got the right date on."

I said the old ones have the wrong date on, love, that was September.

And she said: "Yeah. That's the right one. That's when it was supposed to be."

So there are ninety-one orders of service in existence for a memorial that says the eleventh of September on it, and it was held on the fourteenth of March, and every person who was there has one, and about a third of them have no idea why.

She read the poem.

I am going to write this part carefully because it is the middle of the whole thing and I have had two years to work out how to say it without decorating it.

She stood on a low box that a man at the works made for her on the Thursday, at the end of the long room, in front of ninety-odd people, with her hair at the length it had got to by March, which was somewhere between the stripe and a bob and did not look like a hairstyle so much as like a stage of something.

She had the paper. That was the deal we had made, and Ruth had helped us make it: she takes the paper up with her whether she wants it or not, because the point is that the paper is allowed.

She did not use it.

She said all fourteen lines, and she got the rhyme that does not work slightly wrong in the same place her mother's handwriting gets it wrong, and at the end of the eleventh line, where it says the thing about the kitchen door, she stopped for a bit.

Nobody moved. Ninety people in a room and I have never heard anything like the quality of that silence; it was not embarrassment, it was the entire room holding still for a child.

Then she finished it.

Then she said, which was not in the plan and which nobody knew was coming:

"That's my mum's. She wrote it in March."

And got off the box.

Iris's mother watched it in Kingston, Ontario, on a laptop in a kitchen.

Different film company. I could not, in the end — that is not fair on Gareth, who behaved impeccably throughout and who never sent a second invoice and to whom I owe more than money, but I could not have those particular people in the house, and I told him so on the phone, badly, and he said "Daniel, I'd have done the same" and meant it.

So it was two young women from Sheffield with one camera and a stand.

The stream had four addresses on it and the password was filey, because of the photograph.

Her mother wrote to me eleven days later, on paper, and the letter is in the box with the bow, and it is four sides, and the only part of it I am going to put here is the last paragraph:

You will think you have to keep her mother alive for her. You do not. She is doing it. All you have to do is not be frightened of it, which is much harder, and which you were not able to do for the first year, and which you appear to have started doing since the awful business, and I am sorry that it took the awful business, and I am not sorry it happened, and I have not told anybody here that.

Afterwards, there was food, and it went on much longer than these things do, and at about four o'clock in the afternoon there were about thirty people left and the children had all gone out onto the lawn and it had turned into something that was very nearly a party.

Maya was there. She was still working for us then; she left in the May. She stood at the back during the reading with her arms folded and her chin up and I did not look at her because I would not have got through it.

Beth was there. She did the food, and the tables, and the parking, and she was faultless again, and we did not speak about anything for longer than four minutes at a time, and that was the arrangement then and it more or less still is.

Mark Vance was not there. It had not occurred to me to invite him and it would have been grotesque, and in the summer he said, apropos of nothing, in the yard: "Was it a good day? The service." And I said yes. And he said "Good," and got in his van.

At about half past five, when everybody had gone, I went round the house turning things off.

That is the poem. That is what it is about — a woman going round a house at night turning things off, and the last two lines are about how the house will not notice when it is somebody else doing it.

I had not planned to do it. I did not do it as a ceremony. I did it because it was half past five and there were forty lights on and a caterer's urn still plugged in in the boot room.

And I got to the kitchen and reached for the switch by the door and my daughter was sitting on the stairs in the dark in her coat, watching me.

She said: "You're doing it."

I said: "Doing what?"

She said: "The poem."

I stood there in my own hall with my hand on a light switch.

Then she said: "It's all right. Somebody has to."

That is the sentence. Out of everything in this — two years, a court, a recording, four hundred and ten words read out in front of a magistrate — that is the sentence I would keep if I were allowed to keep one.

She was eight, and she had had her mother's hair taken off her head in her mother's room by a woman who wanted the day to be normal, and six months later she stood on a box and gave the whole room her mother's poem without the paper, and then she sat on the stairs in the dark and gave her father permission to be the one who turns the lights off.

The house did not notice.

That is the point of the poem. The house never notices. It is not supposed to.
