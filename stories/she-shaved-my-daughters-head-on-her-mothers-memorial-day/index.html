She Shaved My Daughter's Head Two Hours Before Her Mother's Memorial

My fiancée said she lost control for one second. Then I found the receipt for the clippers, dated nine days before, and a seven-year-old in the next village who had already learned my dead wife's poem by heart.

Chapter 01 — Eleven O'Clock

Chapter 1 of 25

The service was at eleven and I walked into that room at four minutes past nine.

I know the time because the film company had a call sheet and the call sheet is a document, and documents survive things that people do not. Two cameras in the garden by half past eight. Sound test in the house at nine. Guests from ten fifteen. Service at eleven, in the long room, with the doors open onto the lawn if it stayed dry.

It stayed dry. That is one of the small stupid details my head has kept. It was a clear morning in the second week of September and it stayed dry all day, and by five o'clock there were ninety place settings in a marquee that nobody sat down in.

My wife had been dead two years and one month. Her name was Iris. This was to be the memorial we had not been able to hold at the time, because at the time I could not stand up in a room and say anything at all, and my sister had told me that nobody would think less of me for waiting, and she had been right about that if about nothing else.

Ella was eight. She was going to read her mother's poem.

The house is on the ridge road above Calderwell and it faces south, and the dressing room is at the end of the upstairs corridor where the roof drops. It had been Iris's room. It had a low window seat and a long mirror on a stand and a dressing table with the veneer lifting at one corner, which I had made in my father's workshop in the second year I ran it and never got right.

Claire had wanted to replace that table for nine months. She had said so pleasantly, twice, in the tone people use when they intend to ask a third time.

I was downstairs in the kitchen with a sound engineer clipping a radio microphone to my collar when I heard it. Not a scream. A sound in the throat of a child that stops before it becomes anything, and then a woman's voice saying a word I could not make out, and then the same child's sound again, higher.

The engineer said something. I do not know what.

I have been asked, formally, twice, how long it took me to get from the kitchen to the top of the stairs, and I have said I do not know, and both times the person asking wrote something down.

Maya had both arms round her.

That is the first thing. Not the hair, though the hair was on the carpet in one long piece and it did not look like hair, it looked like something spilled. Not the clippers. Maya Delgado, twenty-four, three days a week, standing in the middle of that room with my daughter pulled backwards into her body and one hand flat on the top of Ella's head, over the part that was gone, holding it there like you would hold a wound.

Ella's dress was rucked at the shoulder where she had twisted.

Her head was shaved in one bare stripe from the hairline at her forehead straight back over the crown. Not neatly. Not all the way to the scalp — the blade had lifted twice and there were two ridges of stubble left standing, and I remember being unable to look away from those two ridges, because they were evidence of a hand that was not steady, and even in that first second some part of my mind was already doing the work it has not stopped doing since, which is deciding what kind of thing had happened.

Claire was by the dressing table.

She was in the dress she had chosen for the memorial, a deep red one, which I had not said anything about when she showed me and which people said things about later.

She was still holding the clippers.

They were still running. That is a detail nobody believes and it is true. That flat buzzing noise going on in the room while nobody moved.

Ella's ivory bow was on the dressing table where it had come off. It had been in her hair forty minutes earlier because I had put it there myself, badly, and Maya had fixed it without saying anything about the way I had done it.

Behind the bow, at the back of the table, was the photograph of Iris.

The hair on the carpet had come down against the foot of the frame. Nobody arranged that. I have thought about it a great deal and there is no arrangement in it, it is simply where a thing falls when it falls, but I have never been able to see that photograph since without seeing what was lying against it.

Then Claire raised the clippers again.

I want to be careful here, because this is the part that got argued about, and because I have had nine weeks and then a year and then longer to decide what I actually saw, and what I actually saw was a woman lift her hand about as high as her own shoulder with a running blade in it while a child was two metres away with her back turned.

She said, later, that she was lifting them to switch them off.

That may be true. I have allowed for it. I have sat in a room with a solicitor and allowed for it out loud, and the honest position is that I cannot tell you what she intended in that half second, and neither can she, and neither can the two people who were in the room.

But that was when I took them off her.

I took her wrist with my left hand and the body of the clippers with my right and I turned them out of her fingers, and I found the switch, and the noise stopped.

The silence in a room after a noise like that is a physical thing. It comes down.

Ella said, into Maya's shoulder, not to me:

"Daddy."

Twice. The second time worse than the first.

Claire said:

"Defend her again and the wedding is off."

I have repeated that sentence to four different people in an official capacity and I have never once managed to say it in the voice she used, because the voice she used was not angry.

It was the voice you use with a supplier who has let you down.

I looked at her. I looked at the ring on her hand, which had been my grandmother's and then my mother's and which I had given her in April on a hill in the rain like a man in a film. I looked at the red dress, and at the two hard little ridges of stubble on my daughter's head, and at the hair against the foot of the photograph.

I said:

"Wedding?"

Then I took her hand — I have to be exact, I took her hand, she did not give it — and I took the ring off her finger, and I put it on the dressing table on top of the hair.

I said:

"What wedding?"

Then something happened that I did not understand for nine weeks.

She went white.

Not furious. Claire Vance was a woman who could hold a room of thirty at a supplier dinner and make a joke land at the far end of the table. If I had simply broken her heart at four minutes past nine in the morning she would have hit me, or she would have said something in front of Maya designed to be repeated downstairs, or she would have walked out of that house and let ninety people arrive to a locked gate.

Instead she went white and put a hand out and found the edge of the dressing table with it.

At the time I read that as shock. I read it as a person understanding, in real time, that she had destroyed her own life over a child's hair, and I remember thinking — in the middle of everything, standing there with the clippers in my hand — that I was watching somebody's face while they lost everything, and that I did not feel anything about it at all.

I was wrong about what I was watching.

It was not the wedding.

It took me until the fourteenth of November to find out what it was, and by then a great deal had been said in rooms I was not in, and my sister had had three weeks and then nine more to tell me a thing she had known since August, and had not.

Then I did the part that belongs to me.

I put the clippers on the dressing table, next to the ring, next to the hair. I put my hand out behind me and Ella came round Maya and took two of my fingers, which is what she did instead of taking a hand, and I moved her behind my legs.

The danger, at that point, was over. I want that written down plainly. I had the clippers. The child was behind me. There were three adults in the room and one of them was holding the only object in it that could hurt anybody.

Claire took a step towards me and said my name.

And I put my hand flat on her upper arm and I pushed her.

Once. Hard enough that she went back off her heels and her legs caught the front edge of the armchair by the window and she went down into it sideways, with her arm over the arm of the chair, and the chair moved on the boards.

She was not injured. That was established later, in detail, by people whose job it is to establish it.

It does not matter that she was not injured.

I was forty years old and I had spent two years telling a room of employees that we do things properly here, and I put my hand on a woman and pushed her into a chair when there was no longer anything to stop.

Nobody made me do that. Not her, not what she had done, not the hair on the floor.

That part is mine. It is the only part of that morning I have never been able to give to anybody else, and I have tried.

I got Ella out.

Not into the corridor, where the sound engineer was standing at the top of the stairs with his mouth open and a roll of tape in his hand. Down the back stairs, through the boot room, out to the yard where the flower van was reversing, and into the workshop office, which is the room in my life that smells the most like my father and the least like anything that had just happened.

Maya came with us. Nobody told her to.

I sat Ella on the desk. I put my jacket round her because she was shaking, and eight-year-olds shake in a way that looks like cold and is not.

She had one hand up flat on the top of her head, over the bare stripe, exactly where Maya's hand had been.

She said:

"I ugly."

I have been hit twice in my life, properly, both times in a yard behind a pub when I was nineteen and stupid, and neither of them was anything.

"You're not," I said.

"I ugly," she said. "It off."

Maya crouched down at the side of the desk so that she was lower than Ella, which is a thing I had watched her do a hundred times without ever once noticing it was a technique.

"It's not off," she said. "It's short. Short on one side. That's all it is. It's short."

Ella looked at her.

Then Ella said, to Maya and not to me:

"She said I look like Mummy."

I said, "What?"

And Maya looked up at me from the floor, and I watched a twenty-four-year-old woman decide whether to say the thing she was about to say to the man who paid her.

"Not in front of her," I said. "Not now."

"No," Maya said. "Not now."

Outside, in the yard, the flower van reversed all the way up to the doors and a man got out and opened the back of it, and it was full of white and cream and green, forty-one arrangements for a service that was going to start in an hour and fifty-one minutes, and he looked at the three of us through the office window and lifted a hand, cheerfully, because he did not know anything yet.

Nobody did.

Synopsis

Daniel Ashworth walked into the dressing room of his own house at four minutes past nine on the morning of his wife's memorial and found his eight-year-old daughter's hair on the carpet in one long strip, the nanny holding her, and the woman he was going to marry in October still holding the clippers. Claire said it happened in a second. She said Ella had pulled away, that there had been shouting, that she had lost control the way anybody might. For nine weeks the only person who said otherwise was a twenty-four-year-old employee whom Claire had already arranged to have moved out of the house. Then a paper receipt turned up for a set of cordless clippers bought nine days before the memorial, and a child in the next village turned out to have been learning Iris Ashworth's poem for a fortnight, and a film crew hired so that two grandparents in Canada could watch the service they were too ill to fly to had left a radio microphone switched on in that dressing room for twenty-six minutes. Four of those minutes were before Daniel walked in. This is what was on them, what his own sister had heard three weeks earlier and not repeated, and what it cost him to be the man who pushed a woman into a chair after the danger had already passed.

Main characters

Daniel AshworthForty, a furniture manufacturer who inherited his father's workshop, two years a widower, who told everyone he was ready to remarry because saying it often enough had begun to feel like being it.
Ella AshworthEight, who had her mother's hair and was going to read her mother's poem at eleven o'clock, and who said one word — the same word, twice — and was believed by nobody who mattered for nine weeks.
Claire VanceThirty-one, an interior designer hired to take a dead woman's house apart room by room, who was very good at it, and who said afterwards that she had lost control for one second.
Maya DelgadoTwenty-four, employed to help with a child three days a week, who put her arms around somebody else's daughter in front of the woman who was about to become her employer's wife, and had already been told her room was needed.
Iris AshworthDead two years at thirty-nine. A glioma. She left a poem, an ivory silk bow sewn out of the offcuts of her grandmother's wedding dress, and a daughter who looked exactly like her.
Beth AshworthForty-six, Daniel's older sister, who introduced Claire to the family, heard one sentence in a car park three weeks before the memorial, and decided it was not the kind of thing you repeat.
Owen PryceSixty, the family solicitor, who explained the difference between a second and nine days and then made his client sit with the part of the morning that belonged to him.
