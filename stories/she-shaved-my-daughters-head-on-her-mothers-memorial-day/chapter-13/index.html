She Shaved My Daughter's Head Two Hours Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 13 — Ella Stops Going Upstairs

Chapter 13 of 25

For eleven days after the eleventh of September my daughter did not go up the main staircase in her own house.

She went up the back stairs from the boot room, which are steep and have a turn in them, and she went up them at a speed that I did not comment on because Ruth Ellery had told me not to.

The main staircase goes past the door of the dressing room. That is all it is. There is no other reason.

I offered to swap her room. She said no. I offered to shut the dressing room and lock it, and she said no to that too, and I did not understand why until Ruth explained it to me, which is a sentence I could put at the top of most chapters of this.

Ruth said: "If you lock it, you've agreed with her that there is something in it."

Ruth Ellery is a clinical psychologist who works with children and she is about fifty-five and she has an office above a dentist's in Wakefield with a bowl of very poor toys in it that she says are deliberately poor.

We got to her in the fourth week, which is later than I would like and is apparently quite fast.

She saw Ella fourteen times over five months. She saw me nine times, separately, which I did not expect and did not want and which turned out to be the more useful half.

The first thing she said to me, in the first session, before I had said anything except my name, was:

"The hair is not the injury."

I said I knew that.

She said: "You don't yet, but you will."

The list of what actually happened to Ella, as against what people in the town thought happened to Ella, took months to assemble and I have it in a document because Ruth made me write it, and I have never shown it to anybody until now.

She would not have her hair washed for six weeks. Not by me, not by Maya, not by anybody. She would stand in the shower and do it herself, badly, with the door open.

She wore a hat indoors from the twenty-third of September to the eleventh of December.

She stopped saying the word memorial and never said it again, and would say that day instead, and still does at fourteen.

She would not sit with her back to a room. That one took me the longest to notice and I only noticed it because a teacher mentioned it at a parents' evening in the November — that Ella had asked to move desks twice and always ended up at the back wall — and I sat there in a small chair with my knees up round my ears and understood.

She asked me eleven times over four months whether Claire was coming back. Eleven. I counted after the fourth, because Ruth told me to count.

And she did the thing that broke me, which was that she got very good.

Very good. Helpful. Quick to say sorry. Quick to say it was fine. In the October half term she took her own plate to the sink at every meal for nine days running, which she had never once done in her life, and I said something cheerful about it, and Maya looked at me across the kitchen with an expression I did not decode until that night.

Ruth's phrase for it, when I described it to her: "She is making herself cheap to keep."

The buzzing thing came in the November.

I had gone out to the workshop on a Saturday to do something with a jointer and Ella had come with me, which she often did, and I switched on a small hand router at a bench about three metres from where she was sitting.

She was under the bench before I got my hand off the switch.

Not crying. Under the bench with both hands flat over the top of her head, in exactly the posture Maya had held her in on the eleventh, which she cannot have remembered consciously and had reproduced perfectly.

I turned it off and sat down on the floor about a metre and a half away, which is the distance Ruth had taught me, close enough to be there and far enough not to be looming, and I did not touch her, which was the single hardest physical act of my adult life.

After a while she said, from under the bench: "It's the same sound."

I said: "It is. It's exactly the same sound. I should have thought."

She said: "You didn't know."

Eight years old, under a bench, with her hands on her head, reassuring me.

We did a thing after that which was Ruth's idea and which I would not have thought of in a hundred years.

We did not remove every buzzing object from the house, which was my instinct and which Ruth vetoed flatly: fear grows in the shape of whatever adults treat as radioactive.

Instead we made a list of every machine in that house and workshop that made that noise, and there were nine, and we went round over three Saturdays and Ella switched each one on herself, with her own hand, for as long as she wanted, which the first time was about a second and a half.

The electric toothbrush. The beard trimmer in the bathroom cupboard, which I threw away afterwards anyway, and Ruth said that was allowed as long as Ella had switched it on first. The hand router. The orbital sander. The kitchen blender. The tile cutter. The garden strimmer. The electric carving knife that had belonged to my mother and had never been used. The clippers I bought.

Yes. I bought a set of clippers.

Ruth's idea, and I fought her about it for two sessions, and she was right and I was wrong and I have never been happier to be wrong about anything.

They live in a drawer in the kitchen where Ella can see them. She switched them on herself in the January, at the kitchen table, at fourteen minutes past six in the evening, and held them for eleven seconds, and switched them off, and put them back in the drawer, and said: "That's stupid."

I said: "What is?"

She said: "It's just a thing."

The stairs came back on the twenty-second of September, which is eleven days, which I have already said.

She went up them without a word about it, carrying a bowl of cereal that she was not supposed to be taking upstairs, which was almost certainly the point.

I was in the hall. I saw her get to the third stair from the top, which is where the dressing-room door comes into view, and I saw her not look at it, in a way that was a decision.

I did not say anything. I stood in the hall like an idiot with a tea towel and let my daughter walk past a door.

Ruth said afterwards, when I told her: "Did you want to clap?"

I said yes.

She said: "Good. Don't. Every time you make one of these a milestone, you tell her it was a mountain."

There is one more thing in the document.

In the December, after the hat came off, Ella asked me whether she could have her hair cut short.

Not shaved. Short — a bob, to about the jaw, which would have taken the growing-out stripe down to something you could not see.

And I said no.

I said it fast, and I said it in a voice, and I saw it land on her face and I could not take it back for about four days.

The reason I said no is that I could not stand it. That is the entire reason. It was the last thing in that house that was still exactly the way Iris's had been, and a woman with a set of clippers had come within thirteen centimetres of it, and my daughter was asking me for permission to finish the job.

That is what it felt like at the time. I want to write it down honestly because it is the ugliest thing in this account that belongs to me, and because a good deal of what comes later grows out of it.

I told Ruth in the next session, expecting to be taken apart.

She did not take me apart. She said one sentence and moved on to something else, and I have thought about it approximately once a week ever since:

"You've just found out what Claire was doing."
