She Shaved My Daughter's Head an Hour Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 17 — The Plea

Chapter 17 of 25

The recording was served on her solicitors on the twenty-eighth of November.

She was re-interviewed on the third of December and she answered questions this time, which she had not done in September.

She was charged with assault occasioning actual bodily harm, and she pleaded guilty to it on the nineteenth of January at the magistrates' court in Leeds, and the court kept the case for sentence rather than sending it up.

Actual bodily harm, for cutting hair. That surprised me and it surprised most people I have explained it to, including two men at the works who were certain I had got it wrong.

Priya Raval had told me it was coming, in the October, in the flat voice.

She said: "Hair is part of the body. There is a case about exactly this — a man cut his ex-partner's ponytail off. The court held that cutting a substantial amount of somebody's hair without consent can amount to actual bodily harm even though there is no bruise and no cut. Harm does not have to be a wound."

I said: "So it is bigger than I thought."

She said: "It is precisely the size it is. Do not go and tell your family she has been charged with something enormous. It is a section forty-seven, it carries a community order at this end of the range for somebody of good character, and if you spend the next four months telling people she is going to prison, you are going to have to live in the same town as that afterwards."

That was the most useful sentence anybody in the legal profession said to me, and it was about managing my own mouth.

I have been asked a hundred times why she did not go to prison.

Because the sentencing range for a first offence of that kind, with no significant injury, an early guilty plea and no previous convictions, is a community order, and no amount of what happened in that room changes the arithmetic on the page. Nobody was hospitalised. The graze towards the back of the crown healed in nine days. The photographs of Ella's head are photographs of an eight-year-old with a bare stripe and two ridges of stubble, which is one of the worst things I have ever looked at, and which the law can only weigh as harm, not as meaning.

Priya put it to me in September, before any of it, when I still thought there would be some enormous word in it.

She said: "The law measures the body. It cannot measure the meaning. That is not a defect you can fix by being angry at me."

She was sentenced on the twelfth of February.

A community order. Eighteen months. Sixty hours' unpaid work, and a rehabilitation activity requirement, and a restraining order prohibiting contact with Ella and with me and from entering a defined area which included my house and the works and my daughter's school.

Costs and a victim surcharge.

The restraining order was the part that mattered and it was the part I had been most frightened about, because I had understood by then that a criminal court exists to punish a person and not to protect a child, and that the two overlap by accident.

It was made for five years. There is no ceiling on that — a restraining order can run for a fixed period or until further order, which I had not known and which I made Owen say twice — and Priya Raval had asked for five and built the application over six weeks with the same appetite for detail that she brought to everything, and when it was granted she wrote good on the corner of her notes and turned the page.

I gave a victim personal statement.

Not about me. Ella was entitled to make one herself — children can, at any age, and I had assumed for weeks that they could not — but Ruth Ellery advised against putting an eight-year-old through it five months after the event, and Ella was asked, in the proper way, and said she did not want to, and so I made it on her behalf.

I wrote four drafts. The first was two thousand words and Owen read it and said one thing, which was: "Whose is this?"

I said what.

He said: "Three-quarters of this is about the wedding, and about your sister, and about a woman lying about a dog. That is your statement. She is not being sentenced for what she did to you."

The version I read out is four hundred and ten words and it has no adjectives in it that I could remove.

It says that my daughter did not go up her own staircase for eleven days.

It says that she wore a hat indoors from the twenty-third of September to the eleventh of December.

It says that she asked me eleven times over four months whether Claire was coming back.

It says that she got under a workbench when a router was switched on three metres away, with both hands flat over her head, and that when I sat down on the floor she said it's the same sound and then said you didn't know in order to make me feel better.

It says that on the eleventh of September my daughter's mother's memorial did not take place, and that ninety-one folded orders of service with Iris's photograph on the front are in a safe in my workshop, and that on the second page of each of them it says Reading — Ella Ashworth.

It says that she has stopped using the word memorial.

Then it says: she is well. She is doing well. She is not a tragedy and I will not have her turned into one, and the thing I want the court to understand is that all of the above happened to a child who is, on any ordinary morning, entirely all right.

That last paragraph was Ruth's instruction and I resented it and she was right, because there is a way of describing a child's harm that harms the child, and because those documents are kept, and because one day my daughter is going to read what her father said about her in a courtroom when she was eight.

Claire was in the dock. I had not seen her since the eleventh of September, five months and one day.

She had cut her hair.

I am aware of how that sounds and I have decided to leave it in because it is a fact and because I spent about four seconds of that hearing unable to think about anything else. Not shaved. Short, above the collar, from a good hairdresser.

Ruth said afterwards, when I told her, that I should be extremely careful about reading anything into it. Then she said: "Although."

Her barrister's mitigation was competent and I have already quoted the useful part of it.

He said she had made full admissions. He said she had lost her employment — which was true; two clients had gone, and a fourth was mine, which she had lost in September in an email of one sentence. He said she was in treatment. He said she had no previous convictions of any kind, which was true.

He said she was ashamed.

The magistrate said something in passing, in the middle of the sentencing remarks, which is the only sentence from any official source in this entire two years that I have written down and kept in my wallet.

She said:

"The aggravating feature here is not the injury, which was slight. It is that this was done to a child on the morning of her mother's memorial service, in a room that had been her mother's, and that its purpose — on the evidence of the defendant's own recorded words — was to make the child unfit to be seen. That is a considerable cruelty and it is not explained by loss of temper."

It is not explained by loss of temper.

Five months. That is how long it took, and it took a receipt in an envelope and a dress in Leeds and a poem in Ashby and a microphone nobody went back for, and at the end of it a woman in a courtroom said a sentence which is what Maya Delgado said to me in my kitchen on the thirteenth of September for nothing.

Claire did not look at me and I did not look at her, and that was not a decision either of us made; there is simply nowhere to look in those rooms.

Outside, on the steps, Owen said: "Do you want to go and have a cup of tea and let it settle, or do you want to go home?"

I said I wanted to go home.

I got back at twenty past two. Maya was there. Ella was at school. There was nothing to do.

I stood in my own kitchen for a while and I waited to feel the thing that people describe, the closure, the door shutting, the weight coming off.

It did not come. It has never come. I have asked two professionals about it and they both said the same thing in different words, which is that court is a mechanism for society, not a mechanism for a person, and that expecting it to do something to your insides is like expecting a hospital to make you happy.

What happened instead was that at ten past four I went to collect my daughter, and she came out of the door with her coat half on as usual, and got in the car, and said:

"Can Maya stay for tea?"

And I said yes.

And that was the twelfth of February, and it was the first day since the eleventh of September that nobody in my house said the word Claire.
