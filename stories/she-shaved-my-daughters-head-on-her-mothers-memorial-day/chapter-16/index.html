She Shaved My Daughter's Head Two Hours Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 16 — What She Said About Iris

Chapter 16 of 25

That's the whole point of you today.

I have carried that sentence around for two years and I have never once been able to say it out loud without stopping in the middle of it.

Not you look like her. Not even you're the image of her, which is the part everybody quotes and which is only a description of a fact.

That's the whole point of you.

To an eight-year-old, in the room where her mother used to dress, ninety minutes before her mother's memorial.

Priya Raval said it was the most useful line in the transcript and I asked her why, since it is not the line about the mother and it is not the line with the clippers in it.

She said: because it is a sentence about a purpose.

She said a person who loses their temper says cruel things about the person in front of them. You're a spoilt little girl. You're doing this on purpose. That is temper, and juries know temper, and temper is very nearly always forgiven when the person who lost it is sitting in a court looking wretched.

That's the whole point of you today is not about the child in front of her at all. It is a sentence about what the child is for, in an arrangement, on a particular day. It is administrative.

She said: "That is the sentence of somebody who has been thinking about your daughter as an item on a running order for some time. You cannot invent that in a bad minute."

I asked Ruth Ellery a different question about it, in the December, which is the one I actually wanted answered.

I asked whether Ella understood it.

Ruth said: "Which part?"

I said: the meaning. Whether an eight-year-old can hear a sentence like that and take from it what an adult takes.

And Ruth said no. Not the words. An eight-year-old does not have the machinery to hear that's the whole point of you today and extract from it a theory of her own position in a household.

Then she said: "But she doesn't need the words. She's had the information for two years. That sentence is just the first time anybody said it out loud in front of her."

I said what information.

Ruth put her pen down, which she did about four times in five months, and each of those four times has cost me something.

She said: "That there was a person living in her house who found the fact of her mother inconvenient. Children work that out immediately. They work it out from how quickly an adult changes the subject. Ella has known since she was six that Iris was a thing you did not bring up when Claire was in the room. She just didn't have a name for it, and she assumed — because children always assume this — that it was something about her."

I want to write down the two things that came out of my daughter's mouth in the following year that tell me Ruth was right, because I do not want this to rest on a psychologist's opinion.

The first was in the January, in the car, on the way back from swimming.

She said, out of nowhere, in the way children raise the enormous things: "Was Claire sad about Mum?"

I said: "I don't think she was, love. I think she found it hard that Mum was still here in the house."

And Ella said: "Mum's not in the house. Mum's dead."

And then, after about a mile:

"I'm in the house."

Eight years old.

I have never in my life had to concentrate harder on a road.

The second was more than a year later, in the spring, when she was ten, and it was not sad at all, and it is the reason I know we came out the other side of it.

We were in the kitchen and there was a photograph of Iris on the shelf by the door — one of two that are downstairs now, out of the nine that used to be — and Ella looked at it while she was eating something and said:

"I've got her eyebrows and it's not fair."

I said what.

She said: "They're mad. Look at them. They're mad."

And she went off to school.

That is the whole of the recovery, in one line, and no professional was in the room for it.

There is one more thing in the transcript about Iris and it is not a cruel sentence and it is the one I think about most.

It is at minute sixteen, in the middle of the argument, and it went past me completely the first time because I was waiting for the clippers.

Claire says: I have never once said a word about any of it.

She is right, and she says it as a complaint, and it is the only entirely honest thing she says in four minutes.

For two years and four months, that woman got up in a house arranged around a dead woman she had never met, and she painted the rooms one at a time, and she took a coffee mug off a shelf with my permission and a cardigan off a door with my permission, and she made an eight-year-old's bedroom nice, and she never once said: this is very hard and I do not know how to do it.

And I never asked, because I did not want the answer, because the answer would have required me to notice that I had hired a woman to remove my wife and then fallen in love with her and then asked her to live inside the removal.

Owen said, when I put some of this to him in his office, in the flattest voice he owns: "You are not responsible for what she did."

I said I know.

He said: "You are, however, responsible for the arrangement."

Then he said: "Those are separate. Do not let anybody, including yourself, make them one thing. Men who make them one thing end up either taking the blame for a criminal act, which is grotesque, or refusing to look at the arrangement, which is worse, because the arrangement is the only part you can do anything about next time."

There will not be a next time in the sense he meant. I have not been with anybody since and I am not looking, and Ruth and I have talked about whether that is damage or preference and have agreed, provisionally, at my age, that it is about sixty-forty preference and that I should stop poking it.

But there is a version of the arrangement everywhere.

At the works, there is a woman on the shop floor who has been there nineteen years and who everybody defers to and who nobody contradicts, and in the February after all this I sat in a meeting and watched a lad of about twenty-three not say a thing he obviously knew, and I recognised it from the inside out.

I stopped the meeting and asked him directly and he told me, and it was worth about nine thousand pounds.

That is not a moral. I am not going to tell you that my daughter's head was shaved so that I could learn to run a better business, which is the kind of sentence people put at the end of things like this and which is obscene.

It is only this: the mechanism that took the top off my life is not rare and it is not dramatic. It is a room in which the person with the most standing is never contradicted, and everybody around them privately does the arithmetic and stays quiet, and it holds, and it holds, and it holds.

And then one morning there is a person in that room who is eight, and who says no, twice, and does not know she is not supposed to.
