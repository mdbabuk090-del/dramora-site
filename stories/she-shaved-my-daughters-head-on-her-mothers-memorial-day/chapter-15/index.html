She Shaved My Daughter's Head an Hour Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 15 — Four Minutes Before I Walked In

Chapter 15 of 25

This is the transcript from fourteen minutes to eighteen minutes twenty.

It was prepared professionally and I have the certified version. What follows is that document with two things removed: a passage of about forty seconds in which my daughter is talking about lunch, which is nobody's business, and the timings, which make it unreadable.

I have not smoothed anything. Where people talk over each other it says so. Where I know what an object is, I have said so in brackets, and those brackets are mine and are not evidence.

CLAIRE: There you are. Maya, would you go down and check the flowers in the long room, they've sent the wrong greenery I think.

MAYA: I'll come down in a minute, I'm just finishing her hair.

CLAIRE: Now would be better.

MAYA: Two minutes.

(pause, about four seconds)

CLAIRE: Ella. Come here a minute, sweetheart, let me look at you.

(pause)

CLAIRE: You look lovely. You do. — Take that off, though.

ELLA: No.

CLAIRE: It doesn't go. It's a cream dress and it's a cream bow and you can't see it, it just looks like a lump. Take it off and I'll put a proper clip in, I've got a nice one.

ELLA: It's my mum's.

CLAIRE: I know whose it is.

(pause, about three seconds)

CLAIRE: Today's a day for everybody, all right? It isn't just yours. There are ninety people coming to this house and every single one of them is going to be looking at you in that thing and thinking about your mother, and your father is going to have to stand up in a room and talk, and you'll make it worse for him. Do you want to make it worse for him?

ELLA: No.

CLAIRE: No. So take it off.

ELLA: (inaudible)

CLAIRE: What?

ELLA: I'm reading the poem.

CLAIRE: I know you are. That's exactly what I mean. You'll be up there with the whole room —

MAYA: Claire.

CLAIRE: I'm talking to Ella.

MAYA: She's eight.

CLAIRE: Thank you, yes.

(pause, about two seconds)

CLAIRE: Maya, go downstairs.

MAYA: No.

(pause, about four seconds. Something is put down on a hard surface.)

CLAIRE: Right.

CLAIRE: (to Ella) Do you know what I've had for two years? I've had your mother's kitchen and your mother's colours and your mother's photograph in every single room I paint, and I have never once said a word about any of it. Not one. And I'm not asking for anything today except that you take one thing out of your hair so that this house can have a normal day.

ELLA: (crying) I want my dad.

CLAIRE: He's downstairs and he's got enough to deal with.

MAYA: (overlapping) — going to go and get him —

CLAIRE: You are not going anywhere.

(pause)

CLAIRE: Right. Fine. Do you know what, sweetheart? It's not the bow, is it. You could take the bow off and put it in a drawer and you'd still walk down those stairs and every single person in that room would say oh, she's the image of her. Wouldn't they. That's the whole point of you today.

(pause, about three seconds)

MAYA: Ella, come here. Come here to me.

CLAIRE: Sit still.

(A drawer, or a case. Then the clippers.)

That is where I am stopping.

The certified transcript continues for another fifty-one seconds before the door, and the words now you don't look like your mother appear in it exactly as Maya Delgado said they did in a kitchen on the thirteenth of September, when she was twenty-four years old and had been told, days earlier, that her room would be needed, and when there was not one single piece of paper in the world to back her up.

I have four things to say about that transcript and then I am going to leave it alone for the rest of this account.

The first is Maya.

Three times. I'll come down in a minute. Two minutes. No.

A twenty-four-year-old employee, in a house that belonged to her employer, on the morning of a memorial, with a woman who had already told her that her room was going, said no three times in four minutes and did not leave that room.

Everything that is known about what happened in there is known because of that. Not because of a microphone. The microphone only recorded it; if Maya had gone downstairs to look at greenery when she was told to, there would have been a woman and a child in that room, and the child would have been eight, and there would have been a version of events with nobody in it to contradict.

The film company saved the case. Maya saved my daughter.

There is a paragraph in chapter nineteen about what happened to her afterwards and it is the only chapter in this that I enjoyed writing.

The second is that it is not a plan.

Owen was right in his car park in November and he was right in a way that cost me. There is no plan in those four minutes. There is nothing in there about clippers until she reaches for them. What is in there is a woman making an argument — a good argument, in places, argued the way you would argue about a supplier — and losing it, twice, to a child and to an employee, and then reaching for the one thing in the room that would end an argument.

Premeditated humiliation, spontaneous execution. Priya's phrase, said in November before anybody outside that room had heard a word of this.

The third is the sentence that changed how I think about my own house, and it is not the cruel one.

It is: I've had your mother's kitchen and your mother's colours and your mother's photograph in every single room I paint, and I have never once said a word about any of it.

That is true.

She never did say a word. Not to me. In two years and four months, that woman never once said to my face that living in that house was hard, and I never once asked, and I have gone back through my memory of eighteen months of evenings looking for the moment I closed that door and I cannot find it, which does not mean it is not there.

It does not excuse one second of what she did and I am not offering it as one. Ruth Ellery said the thing that keeps it in proportion, and she said it fast, in the way she does when she thinks I am about to be unfair to myself: "Daniel. Half the second wives in this country live in a dead woman's kitchen. They don't do this. She is not the only person that has ever happened to. She is the person who reached for a machine."

The fourth is the one I did not see for weeks, and it is about my daughter.

I'm reading the poem.

Three words in the middle of it, said by an eight-year-old who was frightened, to an adult who was leaning on her, in the last four minutes of her life in which she had all her hair.

She was not defending the bow at that point. She had moved on. She was telling Claire Vance the actual thing, the thing underneath, which is that in ninety minutes she was going to stand up in front of the whole room and be her mother's daughter out loud, and that this was going to happen, and that Claire could not stop it.

She was right. Claire could not stop it. She could stop the day; she could not stop that.

It took five months and it happened in the March, and it is chapter twenty.
