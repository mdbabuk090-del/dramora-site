She Shaved My Daughter's Head Two Hours Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 07 — How Claire Came Into This House

Chapter 7 of 25

I did not meet Claire Vance at a party or in a bar or through a friend at a difficult time.

I hired her.

That is the fact that people find hardest to hold, and it is the fact that explains almost everything, and I have watched three separate people's faces change when I have said it out loud.

Nine months after Iris died, my sister came to the house on a Sunday and walked round the ground floor and said the thing that everybody had been waiting for somebody to say, which was that it could not stay like this.

She was right. It could not stay like that.

The house had become a set. Every room in it was arranged in the exact configuration of a specific week in June the previous year — the week Iris had last been well enough to come downstairs. The blanket over the arm of the sofa was where she had left it. Her reading glasses were on the shelf by the kitchen door. There was a Marks and Spencer bag for life hanging on the back of the utility door with a cardigan in it that she had put there to take to the hospital.

I had not left the bag there deliberately. That is the thing about a house like that. Nobody decides. You simply do not touch anything, and then a year goes by, and the not-touching has become a structure.

Beth said: "Ella's growing up in a museum."

That sentence did what it was built to do.

Claire Vance did the showroom in Leeds for us in the spring. She was thirty and she was very good and she had a way of standing in a room she had never been in before and saying three things about it in ninety seconds that were all true.

Beth had used her for the office. Beth recommended her.

I want to say this cleanly, because it is going to matter in the next chapter: my sister did not introduce Claire to me as a woman. She introduced her as a contractor. There was no scheme. There was no dinner with a spare chair. Beth gave me a name and a mobile number on a Sunday afternoon and said, she'll be kind about it, she does houses where someone's died, that's half her work.

That was true too.

Claire came on the fourteenth of May and walked round the ground floor for forty minutes and did not touch a single thing.

At the end of it she stood in the kitchen doorway and looked at the cardigan in the bag on the back of the door and said:

"I won't move anything until you've told me what it is. Everything in a house like this is either an object or a person. I can't tell which from here and I'm not going to guess."

I hired her that afternoon.

I have gone back to that sentence so many times that I have worn the shape off it.

Because here is the thing that took me until the following winter to understand: it is a genuinely excellent sentence. A person who says that to a widower in a kitchen is not necessarily performing. Her whole professional life was walking into houses where something had happened and being the one adult who was allowed to say this cupboard is just a cupboard.

She was paid to do a specific and difficult job, and the job was to take a dead woman's house apart, room by room, respectfully, with the owner's consent, so that a living family could go on living in it.

She was good at it. The house was better. It stopped being a set. Ella got a room with a wall she chose herself. The kitchen door stopped having a bag on it and I was, for about four months, extremely grateful in a way that made me stupid.

Somewhere in that work — and I do not know where, and I do not think there is a where — the professional exercise and the private one stopped being separable.

She had spent a year being the person authorised to decide which traces of Iris Ashworth could be removed.

And then she was going to marry me, and the authorisation stopped, and there was one trace left that she could not touch, and it was eight years old and it was walking round the house with its mother's face on it.

We started in September of that year, four months into the work. I was the one who started it. I want that on the record because there is a version of this in the town in which a clever younger woman moved on a bereaved man, and it is not true, and the truth is worse in a way that only matters to me.

I started it because she was in my house four days a week and she was the only adult in my life who was not managing me.

Everybody else was managing me. My sister was managing me. My works manager was managing me. My mother-in-law in Ontario, on the phone, was managing me from four thousand miles with enormous skill. Every one of them was very slightly holding their breath.

Claire was not, because Claire had not known Iris.

That is what I fell in love with. Not her. The absence of Iris in her.

I did not work that out on my own. Ruth Ellery got it out of me in a Tuesday appointment in February, with a box of tissues on the table between us that I did not use, and when she said it back to me I said that's not fair and she said no, it isn't, but is it true, and I sat there for a long time.

We got engaged in April, in the rain, on the hill above the reservoir, nearly two years after she walked round my ground floor.

Ella was there. I had thought about that for weeks and had decided that it would be better for her to be present at the moment than to be told afterwards, which I still think was right, and I had rehearsed with Claire what we would say.

What we said was that Claire was going to come and live here properly and be part of our family, and that nobody was going to be anybody's mum, and that Mummy is Mummy for ever.

Claire said that last part. Not me. She said it kneeling down on wet grass at the eye level of a seven-year-old, and she said it well, and Ella hugged her.

I have had to hold both of those things at once ever since: that she said that on a hill in April, and that she said you're not carrying your mother about on your head at my table in a bedroom in September.

People want one of them to be the fake one. That is what everybody asks, in various polite forms. Was any of it real?

The honest answer, which nobody enjoys, is that I think both were real, and that the second one grew out of the first, and that a woman who kneels on wet grass and promises a child that her dead mother is for ever may spend the next five months privately discovering that she cannot live inside her own promise.

She did not stop meaning it and start lying. She meant it, and then the house filled up with a dead woman's hair, and she began to keep a ledger.

There is one more thing about the hiring that I have to put down here because Owen made me look at it and I did not want to.

I paid for the house work personally. Sixty-one thousand pounds over fourteen months, in stages, into Vance Interiors Ltd.

When we became engaged in April, we did not stop. There was still the top floor to do. There was the dressing room at the end of the corridor with the dressing table with the lifting corner — which was, on the eleventh of September, four weeks away from being taken out and replaced, with my written agreement, at a cost of two thousand three hundred pounds.

Owen said: "Do you see it?"

I said I did not.

He said: "You paid her to remove your wife from your house. For fourteen months, in stages, with your signature on each one. And then you told her the last object was not for removal."

Then he said the thing that I have not been able to put down since:

"Every single thing that woman did to that house, she did with your permission. Except the last one."
