She Shaved My Daughter's Head an Hour Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 11 — The Push

Chapter 11 of 25

I have put this chapter here, in the middle, on purpose, because that is where it belongs and because there is a version of this account in which it goes at the end, in a paragraph, with the word of course in front of it.

On the eleventh of September I put my hand on a woman's upper arm and shoulder and pushed her hard enough that she went backwards off her feet into an armchair.

The clippers were already in my hand.

My daughter was already behind my legs.

There were two other adults in the room and neither of them was in any danger from anybody.

It was reported by the sound engineer.

Not by Claire — she reported it four days later through her solicitor, which is a different thing and which was tactical and which I do not hold against her. It was reported that morning, at about half past ten, by a man of about twenty-six called Rhys who had come to the house to run a microphone lead and who had been standing at the top of the stairs.

He did the right thing. I want that written down because for about a fortnight in the town he was regarded as having done something disloyal, and one of my own employees said something to him in a petrol station, and I found out about that in November and dealt with it.

He saw a man twice his size shove a woman into a chair and he rang the police from his van.

If he had not, I would have had to report it myself, and I would like to be able to tell you that I would have. Owen asked me that question in his office on the sixteenth of September, straight out, no cushioning — if the young man had not made the call, would you be sitting here? — and I said yes, and he looked at me for a while and said: "Possibly."

He was right to say possibly. I have thought about it for two years and I still do not know.

The investigation into what Claire did to Ella, and the investigation into what I did to Claire, ran separately and were dealt with separately, and that infuriated a number of people who love me.

My uncle said: "She attacked a child."

"Yes."

"You stopped her."

"Yes."

"So what's this about?"

"Because I'd already stopped her."

He did not follow that. He is seventy-one and he genuinely did not follow it, and I have watched perfectly decent men fail to follow it a dozen times since, in yards and pubs and at the far end of a bar at a trade dinner.

The order of events is the whole of it, and the order of events is not complicated:

Clippers in my hand. Child behind me. Then the push.

If you move the push to before the clippers, it is a different thing entirely and there would have been no investigation at all — a man taking a weapon off somebody who is using it on a child can put her through a wall and no one in this country will trouble him about it.

I did not do that. I disarmed her calmly, correctly, with two hands, and I moved my daughter behind me, and the situation ended.

And then, three or four seconds later, when there was nothing left to stop, I did it anyway.

Not because she was dangerous. Because she took a step towards me and said my name in the voice of somebody who still thought she was in a negotiation.

I have had a long time to be honest about what that push was, and the honest answer is not protective. It is that in that moment I could not bear to be spoken to in that voice, in that room, over that hair, and my body dealt with it before I had any part in the decision.

That is not a father. That is a man in a workshop yard when he was nineteen.

I was interviewed under caution on the eighteenth of September for two hours and ten minutes. I answered every question. Owen advised me to and I would have anyway.

The outcome was a conditional caution, which for people outside this country's system means: I admitted the offence, it went on my record, and there were conditions.

The conditions were a course, twelve sessions, and no further violent conduct, and there was a compensation element which Claire's solicitors dealt with and which I paid in full within a fortnight.

Some of my family regarded the caution as humiliating. One cousin used the word degrading, on the telephone, at length.

I asked him whether he thought that shoving a woman into a chair was dignified.

He has not rung since. That is a loss of approximately nothing.

The course was in a community centre in Barnsley on Tuesday evenings, in a room with a hatch to a kitchen and eleven plastic chairs.

There were seven of us. One of them was twenty and had put a hole in a door. One of them had been coming for a second time and did not want to be there and made that clear for the first four weeks and then stopped.

The man running it was called Phil. He had a lanyard and an unhurried manner and he had heard everything.

In the third week I did the thing that everybody does, apparently, which is that I explained my circumstances. I explained about the memorial and the hair and the eight-year-old and the fact that the danger had passed but only just, and that this was not the same as the other men in the room, and I did it well, because I am articulate and because I run a business and because I have spent twenty years being listened to in rooms.

Phil let me finish.

Then he said: "Right. So when the danger's passed, and you push her — what's the push doing?"

I said it was not doing anything. That was the point. It was after.

And Phil said: "So what's it for?"

I sat there in a plastic chair in Barnsley for what felt like a very long time.

Then I said: "It's for me."

He said: "Yeah."

That is the entire twelve weeks. Everything else was homework.

The one thing that course gave me which I use almost every week is the sequence.

Phil made us learn it like a phone number, and I resented it, and it works.

Move the child. Ring somebody. Leave the room. Lawyers.

That is it. That is the whole of the response to a person doing something unforgivable in front of you when the immediate danger has ended.

It is not satisfying. It is designed not to be satisfying.

I have used it twice since. Once at the works, with a supplier's man who said something about Maya that I will not write down, and once at a school gate eighteen months later with a father who had heard a version of this story and thought he was being funny about it in front of children.

Both times my hands did what they wanted to do and both times the rest of me went: move the child, ring somebody, leave the room, lawyers.

Both times I left with everything intact.

There is one last thing about the push and it is the only part of it that has ever made me want to argue.

Ella saw it.

She was behind my legs and she came round the side of me, which is what an eight-year-old does, and she was looking straight at Claire when it happened.

For about four months I told myself that this was a small harm inside a large one — that a child who had just had her head shaved could not be much damaged by watching her father shove somebody into a chair.

Ruth Ellery took that away from me in about ninety seconds, in the February, and she did it so gently that I did not notice until I was in the car park.

She said: "What did Ella learn that morning about what happens when somebody hurts you?"

I said: that her father would stop it.

She said: "And?"

And I sat there.

She said: "She learned both. You don't get to choose which one she took. She'll have the second one somewhere, and one day when she's fifteen it will come out of her mouth in an argument with you, and when it does, you say yes."

She was right, and it did, and it was not when Ella was fifteen. It was when she was eleven, in a car, about something else entirely, and she said: you pushed her though.

And I said: "Yes. I did. It was wrong and I got in trouble for it and I deserved to."

She thought about it for about half a mile.

Then she said: "Okay," and put the radio on.

That is the only conversation about it we have ever had, and I have never known whether that means it landed or whether it is still waiting.
