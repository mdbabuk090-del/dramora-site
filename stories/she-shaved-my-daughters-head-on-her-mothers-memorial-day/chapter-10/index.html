She Shaved My Daughter's Head an Hour Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 10 — Claire's Version

Chapter 10 of 25

I have Claire's account in three forms and they are not the same as each other, which is the whole of this chapter.

The first is the statement she gave through her solicitor on the fifteenth of September, four days after.

The second is what she said to me on the telephone on the twenty-sixth of September, for eleven minutes, which I should not have taken and which Owen told me afterwards, without heat, that I must never do again.

The third is what she said in interview on the third of December, after the recording had been disclosed to her, which is a different document entirely and belongs to chapter seventeen.

The written statement is four pages and it is a good piece of work. I do not mean that cynically. It was drafted by a competent solicitor with a client who is articulate, and it does what such a document is supposed to do, which is to tell the truth in the shape that helps you most.

It says:

That she had been under considerable strain in the weeks before the memorial service.

That she had a difficult relationship with the child's bereavement in the sense that she felt unable to raise concerns about it.

That on the morning in question there had been an argument about a hair ornament.

That she had taken hold of it and it had come away with some hair, which she deeply regretted.

That the child had become distressed and the nanny had become involved.

That she had picked up a set of clippers which were hers, kept in her overnight bag, having previously been bought for a dog.

That she had intended only to frighten.

That the child had moved her head backwards into the blade.

That she had immediately been horrified.

That she had never at any point said anything about the child's mother, and that the account given by Ms Delgado in that respect was untrue, and that Ms Delgado had been informed four days earlier that her accommodation would not continue after the marriage.

That Mr Ashworth had then entered the room, disarmed her, and pushed her with force into an armchair.

That she had sustained bruising and shock.

That she wished it recorded that she loved the child and had never previously raised a hand to her.

Read it again and count what is in it.

There are two facts in that document that are physically checkable, and one of them is the clippers, and one of them is the head movement.

The clippers were not hers and were not bought for a dog. That went in the autumn, with a receipt in an envelope, and it went permanently.

The head movement — the child moved her head backwards into the blade — did not survive either, and it went in a way I find harder to describe.

The stripe runs from the hairline at the front of Ella's head straight back over the crown. It is thirteen centimetres long, which was measured, because these things are measured.

A stripe like that is not what a blade leaves when a stationary hand meets a moving head. Everybody who looked at the photographs said some version of that, in careful language, and the careful language does not matter, because I had already seen the two ridges of stubble in the middle of it where the blade had lifted and been put back down.

You do not lift and put down if you are being jerked into.

You lift and put down if the first pass did not go all the way through, and you are finishing it.

The telephone call was on the twenty-sixth of September, at about half past nine at night, and I picked it up because it was a number I did not know and I had spent two weeks picking up numbers I did not know because of the insurance and the marquee.

She said: "Don't put the phone down."

So I did not, which was the mistake.

She cried for a while. It was not performed. I have thought about that a great deal and I am confident: nobody performs that well at half past nine at night to a man who is not going to be moved.

Then she said the thing she had rung up to say, and here it is as close to word for word as I can manage, because I wrote it on the back of an envelope within about four minutes of the call ending, which Owen said was the only sensible thing I had done all evening.

She said: "It was one second. Daniel, it was one second of my life. You've known me two years. You're going to take one second and make it the whole of me."

And I said — and this is the only thing I said in eleven minutes that I am glad about —

"It was thirteen centimetres."

She was quiet.

Then she said: "That isn't fair."

That exchange has stayed with me more than anything else that was said out of court, because it is the entire argument of the next two years in two sentences.

It was one second.

That is the defence. Not a legal defence — legally she was going to plead, and everybody knew it by then. It is the human defence, and it is the one that works on people, and it very nearly worked on me at half past nine at night on the twenty-sixth of September.

Because it has real weight. Everybody has a second. Everybody has said the unforgivable thing to somebody they love at two in the morning, or braked too late, or put their hand on a door and slammed it. A life is not the worst second in it. I believe that. I have needed to believe it since the eleventh of September for reasons that are entirely my own.

But it is only true where it is true.

The receipt is dated the second of September.

The dress for the other child was ordered on the twenty-second of August.

The poem went to Ashby on the twenty-eighth.

The sentence in the car park with my sister was the twentieth.

We'll see how she is was the fourth of September, when my daughter was perfectly well and riding a bicycle badly round a yard.

That is not a second. That is twenty-two days, at minimum, and it is more probably a summer.

What happened in one second was the blade. Everything that got the blade into that room took three weeks and involved a trade counter, a card, a dress shop in Leeds, a phone call to a sister-in-law, and a seven-year-old learning fourteen lines of a dead woman's poem in a kitchen in Ashby with her mother holding the phone.

Owen put it in a way I have used many times since, at the works, with people who have done something and want it to have been an accident.

He said: "One second is a defence for the act. It is never a defence for the arrangements."

There is one more part of the call.

At the end of it, when it had gone quiet and I was about to say the thing that ends a call, she said:

"How is she?"

And I said nothing at all for long enough that she said my name.

I did not tell her that Ella had a hat on indoors. I did not tell her about the hood, or that my daughter had asked whether the day for her mother had been cancelled because of her, or that she had stopped mentioning the poem.

I said: "You don't get to know that."

She said: "That's cruel."

I said: "Yes."

And that was true, and I have never regretted it, and I want to be honest that those two facts sit next to each other and always have.

I could have given her one sentence. It would have cost me nothing. There is a version of me that gives her one sentence, and that version is a better man in the abstract and a worse father in the particular, and I have made my peace with which one I am.

Ruth Ellery asked me once whether I had ever considered what that call was for.

I said: to get me to say it was one second.

She said: "No. You were the last person alive who was still going to tell her she was a good person. She rang up to see if that was gone."

Then she said: "It was gone."
