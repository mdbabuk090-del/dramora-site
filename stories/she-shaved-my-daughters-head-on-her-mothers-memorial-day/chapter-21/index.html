She Shaved My Daughter's Head an Hour Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 21 — Iris's Room

Chapter 21 of 25

I did the dressing room in the August, four years after Iris died and eleven months after the eleventh of September.

Not the whole house. The house had already been done, room by room, over fourteen months, by a woman who was extremely good at it and whom I paid sixty-one thousand pounds, and I have never undone one square metre of that work. The kitchen she designed is the kitchen. Ella's bedroom is the room Claire drew on a pad at my table in the November of that first year, with the window seat and the shelf that runs round three walls, and my daughter loves it, and there is no version of this in which I take a wall down out of spite.

People find that odd. My sister found it odd enough to say so.

I said: "She was good at her job. That's not the part that was wrong."

The dressing room was the room that never got done. It was on the schedule for October: new dressing table, two thousand three hundred pounds, my written agreement, the veneer with the lifting corner going out to the tip.

After the eleventh of September I shut the door.

Not locked. Ruth had put a stop to that in the first month. Shut.

For eleven months I went in about six times, always for a reason — a box, a suit, once to find a photograph for the orders of service — and I did not stay, and I did not sit down, and I hoovered it twice with the door open.

What made me do it in the end was not grief and it was not therapy. It was a leak.

There is a valley gutter over that corner of the roof and in the July it went, and there was a stain the size of a dinner plate on the ceiling in the corner above the window seat, and a man came and fixed the gutter and said the plaster would want doing.

So a room I could not go into needed a plasterer.

I stood in the doorway on a Saturday morning in the August with a coffee and looked at a wet mark on a ceiling and understood that I was about to pay a man from Barnsley to work in the room for three days while I went out.

I got a bin bag and a screwdriver and did it myself instead, and it took seven hours, and I want to write down what was in there because it is the only inventory in this account that made anything better.

The photograph of Iris at Filey, on the dressing table, with the frame that has a chip out of the bottom corner from a house move in 2014.

The dressing table, veneer lifting, which I made in my father's workshop in the second year I ran it. I got about as far as the top of the stairs with it and then brought it back. It is in the room now with a runner over the bad corner and Ella keeps her stuff on it.

Iris's jewellery, which is not much: her mother's chain, an amber thing she wore constantly and which I cannot look at, two pairs of earrings, and her wedding ring, which came off in the hospital in the last fortnight when her hands went and which I have never known what to do with.

Fourteen bottles of perfume of which nine were empty. That is a thing about a dead woman's dressing table that nobody tells you. She had been keeping them for the bottles.

A hairbrush, which I put in the bin bag and then took out of the bin bag and then put back in.

Two hundred and something photographs in a shoebox, unsorted, from before phones. Ella and I did those over three evenings in the September and it was the best thing we did that year.

A card from me to her from 2016 with an entirely idiotic drawing on it.

Her reading glasses, which had been on the shelf by the kitchen door for fourteen months until Claire asked, correctly and gently, whether they could go somewhere else, and which I had put in that room and forgotten.

And, in the drawer, at the back, under a scarf: the plastic sleeve with the four guards in it, from a set of clippers, which had come off the box on the second of September and which nobody had ever looked for.

I sat on the floor with that for a while.

Then I put it in the bin bag, and took the bin bag out to the yard, and put it in the skip that was there for the plaster, and went back in.

I did not ring Owen. There was nothing to ring anybody about; the case had been over for six months, she had pleaded, the guards proved nothing that the receipt had not proved twice over.

But I sat down on the floor of that room and had about ten minutes that I am not going to describe, and they were about the ordinariness of it. That the whole thing had come into my house in a box, in a canvas bag, with a plastic sleeve of four guards that got put in a drawer under a scarf because there was nowhere else to put them.

I painted it myself in September. Ella chose the colour, from Claire's own fan deck, which was still in the utility room drawer and which I had genuinely forgotten was there.

She picked a green.

I said: "Are you sure? It'll be dark in the winter."

And my daughter, nine by then, said: "It's the one Mum's got in the photo."

It is not. The dress in the Filey photograph is a completely different green and I have looked at it forty times since and I am right and she is wrong and I have never once said so.

I want to set down plainly what that room is now, because the whole shape of this thing depends on it and I get it wrong when I talk about it out loud.

It is not a shrine. There is one photograph in it and there used to be four.

It is not cleared out either. Iris's chain is in the drawer and the amber thing is in the drawer and her wedding ring is in the box with the bow and the letter from Ontario, and none of that is going anywhere, and when Ella is twenty-one it becomes hers to decide about and I have written that down in a will that Owen drafted and grumbled about.

What it is, mostly, is a room my daughter does her hair in.

That is what I could not have imagined on any day in that first year. Not a memorial, not a museum, not a cleared space with the ghost professionally removed. A fourteen-year-old with a straightener and a phone propped against a frame that has a chip out of the bottom corner, sitting at a bad dressing table with a lifting veneer, in a room the wrong green.

Beth said something at Christmas, the first Christmas after I did it, standing in the doorway with a drink.

She said: "You've made it nice."

And then she said — and she did not mean anything by it, and it went through me like a nail: "It's what Claire would have done, really, isn't it. In the end."

I said: "No."

She looked at me.

I said: "She'd have done it in the October. With my signature on it. And it would have been beautiful, and I'd have been grateful, and Ella would have watched her mother's room get taken to bits by somebody who was counting."

Beth said: "That's a bit hard."

And I said the thing I had worked out in the August, sitting on that floor with a plastic sleeve of clipper guards in my hand, and which is the only piece of understanding out of the whole two years that I would put in somebody else's hands:

"It's the same rooms, Beth. It was always going to be the same rooms. The whole thing was never about which rooms. It was about who's allowed to say when."
