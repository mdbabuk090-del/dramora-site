She Shaved My Daughter's Head Two Hours Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 04 — The Memorial That Was Cancelled

Chapter 4 of 25

Ninety-one people had been invited and eighty-four had said yes.

I know the number because Iris's mother had made the list on squared paper in Ontario and posted it to me, in an envelope, in July, with the names of people I had never met written in a hand that shakes now, and a note at the bottom saying you don't have to have all of them, love, but these are the ones who knew her before you did.

Her parents did not come.

That is the thing about the memorial that nobody outside the family understands and that I have stopped trying to explain at dinners. Iris's mother has a heart condition that means she has not flown since 2019. Iris's father is eighty-one and will not fly without her. They had not been able to come to the funeral either. They had watched their daughter's funeral on a laptop in a kitchen in Kingston, Ontario, with the sound going in and out, on a link that a friend of a friend set up on a phone propped against a jug.

Which is why, two years later, when I was finally able to stand up in a room, I hired a film company.

Two cameras. A proper sound feed. A stream with a password on it that would go to exactly four addresses and then be deleted, and a copy on a hard disk for them afterwards, because Iris's mother had said on the telephone, without any self-pity at all, I would like to be able to watch it more than once.

That was the entire reason those people were in my house that morning.

I have thought about this more than is good for me. Everything that came out in November — every fact that changed what my daughter's life was going to be — exists because two people in Canada were too ill to get on an aeroplane.

Eight hundred and forty pounds for the film company. Two thousand one hundred for the marquee. Twelve hundred for the caterer, of which four hundred was returnable. Four hundred and ten for the flowers, none of which was returnable, forty-one arrangements of white and cream and green, which sat in my coach house for six days getting worse.

Six thousand two hundred pounds, near enough, for a day that did not happen.

I am not telling you that because of the money. I am telling you because for about three weeks in the autumn the number went round the town in a form I did not recognise, which was: he's out six grand and he's still not having her back.

As though the six grand were the argument.

There is a way that a certain kind of person will reach, always, for the accounting. It happened at the workshop, from a supplier I had known twenty years, who put his hand on my shoulder in the yard in October and said, kindly, that these things blow over, and that a wedding is a very hard thing to cancel.

I said: "It isn't, though. You just say the sentence."

He laughed. He thought it was a joke.

That is the whole of it, actually. That is the entire mechanism, and it took me a long time to see it, and Claire Vance saw it before I did.

A wedding is treated as too large to stop. Deposits, guests, aeroplanes, a marquee, a dress, a mother-in-law's squared paper. Everybody agrees in advance that it cannot be stopped, and then everybody has to tolerate whatever happens underneath it.

She said there is no wedding to me as a threat, standing over the hair of an eight-year-old, because she believed that the wedding was the biggest object in the room.

It was not. It was the cheapest object in the room. It cost six thousand two hundred pounds and one sentence.

Beth handled the telephone calls that evening and I let her, and that was a mistake of a kind I made repeatedly that autumn — the mistake of being grateful.

She told people there had been an incident. She told people Ella had been hurt. She did not tell people how, and by the third day the version circulating in Calderwell was that a child had been injured by clippers at the Ashworth house, which is technically every word of it true and which had, embedded in it, an entire other story: a mother-in-law-to-be helping with a child's hair before a service, an accident, a family closing ranks.

Two women in the town believed that version for nine weeks and one of them said something about it to Maya in a shop.

I did not correct it, because Owen Pryce told me not to, and Owen Pryce was right.

But you should know what happens in the space where a family does not say a thing out loud. Something grows in it. It always grows in the direction of the person with more standing. Nobody decides that. It is simply the shape of the ground.

On the fourth day I telephoned Ontario.

I had put it off for three days and I had rehearsed it in the workshop, out loud, alone, like a man learning lines.

Iris's mother listened all the way through without saying anything at all, which is a thing that runs in that family and which Iris did too, and which used to frighten me in the early years until I understood it was not coldness, it was a woman refusing to interrupt.

When I finished she said: "Where is Ella now."

Not how could this happen. Not what did the police say. Not are you all right.

"Asleep," I said. "Maya's with her."

"Maya's the young one. The one with the hair."

"Yes."

"Good," she said.

There was a long pause on a telephone line four thousand miles long.

Then she said: "Daniel. Was it about Iris?"

And I said — because I still believed it at that point, because I still had the shape of it in my head as one terrible minute in a life, a woman losing her temper on a bad morning — I said:

"I think it was about the bow."

And Iris's mother, eighty years old, in a kitchen in Kingston, said:

"The bow is Iris."

I did not understand that sentence for another nine weeks, and when I did understand it, it was in a solicitor's office with a transcript in front of me and it went through me like a wire.

She had it in four seconds on a bad telephone line. It took me until November and a recording.

That is not because she is cleverer than me, although she may be. It is because she was not in the room, and she was not going to marry anybody, and she had nothing at all invested in the answer being small.

I had a great deal invested in the answer being small.

I had two years of telling people I was doing well. I had a workshop of eighty employees who had watched me lose my wife and had watched me come back to work in seven weeks because I did not know what else to do with my hands. I had a sister who had introduced me to a woman in a way that made her responsible for it, and who wanted very much for it to have been an accident. I had an eight-year-old whose entire remaining parent was a man who had already once chosen wrong.

Everybody in my life needed that morning to be one minute long.

And so for nine weeks it was one minute long.

The last thing about the memorial that did not happen.

The marquee company came back on the Thursday to take the frame down, and I went out to sign for it, and the foreman said something to me that I have kept.

He said: "We had a lad take one down in June the day after a wedding. Bride'd broken her wrist in the car park at two in the morning. They'd still gone through with the whole day."

I said: "Why are you telling me that?"

He said: "Because most of them go through with it. That's all."

Then he put the paperwork in my hand and went to help with the poles.

Most of them go through with it.

I have thought about the men and women in those marquees, all over the country, every Saturday, standing in a room having agreed with themselves and each other that whatever happened in the car park at two in the morning is smaller than the deposit.

I very nearly was one of them. I want to be honest about the margin.

If she had said sorry — if, at four minutes past nine, standing by that dressing table with the clippers still running, Claire Vance had put them down and gone white and said oh God, oh God, what have I done — I do not know what I would have done. I would like to tell you I would still have taken the ring. I have said so, out loud, to a counsellor, and he waited, in the way they do.

What actually saved my daughter from a version of her life in which that woman was in the house was not my judgement.

It was that Claire did not apologise.

She threatened me. She reached for the biggest object in the room and swung it at me, and it turned out to be made of paper, and it went straight through.
