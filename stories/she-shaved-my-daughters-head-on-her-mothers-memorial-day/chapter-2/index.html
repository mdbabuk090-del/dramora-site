She Shaved My Daughter's Head Two Hours Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 02 — What the Doctor Wrote

Chapter 2 of 25

The ninety guests were dealt with by my sister.

I want to be fair to Beth where I can be, because there is a great deal further on in this that I cannot be fair to her about, and this is one of the places where she was faultless. She stood at the gate for two hours and ten minutes and turned people round in their cars. She did it without giving anybody a reason and without letting a single one of them past, including a cousin of Iris's who had driven from Northumberland and cried in the lane.

She told them there had been an accident in the house.

That was the word that went out, and it went out at ten past nine, and it took the better part of a year to get it back.

Dr Aled Morgan came at ten. He has been the family doctor since before I was married and he did not ask a single question on the way through the yard.

He looked at Ella's head in the workshop office with the desk lamp turned round, because she would not go back into the house, and he did it slowly, and he narrated the whole thing to her while he did it — I'm going to touch here, tell me if that's sore, that's it, that's all that was — in a voice that never went up at the end.

There were no cuts requiring treatment.

There was one area towards the back of the crown, about the size of a thumbprint, where the blade had been pressed and dragged and the skin was grazed and inflamed. He cleaned it. He put nothing on it.

No injury to the scalp otherwise. No injury to the neck. A red mark on the upper right arm where a hand had held her, four marks, consistent with fingers, which he measured against his own hand and photographed with Ella's agreement and my agreement, in that order — hers first. He asked her first. He asked an eight-year-old for permission before he photographed her arm and he waited for her to say yes.

His note, which I have a copy of, says: No head injury. No treatment indicated. Forced removal of hair, frontal to vertex in a single strip, with associated grip marks to right upper arm. Child distressed and self-conscious. Advise minimal questioning pending specialist involvement.

Minimal questioning.

I want to say something about that, because I got it wrong, and I got it wrong on purpose, out of the best motive I had that day, which is the way most of my mistakes have gone.

I did not ask Ella what had happened.

I did not ask her that morning, and I did not ask her that week, because a doctor had written minimal questioning and because every instinct I had was screaming that the worst thing I could do to my daughter on the morning of her mother's memorial was to sit her down and make her the source of information about her own body.

That was decent of me and it was correct and it cost me nine weeks.

Because what a child says in the first hour and what a child says in the ninth week are treated very differently by everybody, and I did not know that, and nobody told me until it was already true.

Claire left the house at twenty to eleven.

She did not leave with anybody. She drove herself. She went to a hotel in the town at the bottom of the hill, the one with the car park that floods, and she checked in under her own name and she stayed there for four nights, and on the second of those nights she instructed a solicitor.

Her clothes stayed in my bedroom for six days. That is a thing that happens which nobody warns you about — the wardrobe, afterwards, with two people's lives in it, and having to decide who is going to be the one to take them out.

Before she left she stood in the kitchen doorway and said one thing to me, across the room, with Beth in the way.

She said: "You know I would never hurt her."

I have thought about the tense of that sentence more than any other sentence in this. Would never. Not did not. Not have not.

I said nothing at all, and she took that as an answer, and she was right to.

At eleven o'clock, which was when the service was to have started, I was sitting on the step of the workshop with my daughter asleep against my chest under my jacket, because she had gone from shaking to talking too fast to asleep, which Dr Morgan said was normal and which felt like watching a small machine break down in stages.

The marquee company arrived at eleven fifteen to turn the tables.

I told them to stop and go home and I would pay for the day. The foreman was very kind about it in the particular way of a man who has seen weddings collapse and has learned not to look at anybody's face while he coils a cable.

At quarter to twelve the film company came and asked what I wanted them to do.

I said: pack up.

That is the single most expensive sentence of my life and I did not know it when I said it. If I had said anything else — if I had said leave it all set up, if I had said come back next week — I do not know when I would have found out what I found out in November, or whether I would have found out at all.

They packed up. Two cameras, three stands, a monitor, a bag of cable, a case of radio microphones.

A case of radio microphones with one slot empty in it.

Nobody counted them. Why would anybody count them. There were ninety guests being turned round in a lane and a child asleep on a step with half her hair gone.

The rest of the first day I remember in the wrong order, which I am told is usual.

I remember Maya on the phone in the corridor to her mother, in Spanish, with her hand over her eyes.

I remember that at some point in the afternoon somebody had put the forty-one flower arrangements in the coach house because there was nowhere else for them, and the coach house doors were open, and the smell of it from twenty metres away was the smell of a funeral, which is a smell I had spent two years getting out of my nose.

I remember Beth saying, more than once, "It'll look like it was nothing, you'll see, in a week it'll look like nothing," and me thinking she meant Ella's hair, and me being grateful, and not understanding until much later that she had been talking about something else entirely.

And I remember at nine o'clock that night going upstairs on my own to the room at the end of the corridor and standing in the doorway.

Somebody had hoovered.

I do not know who. It was not Maya, who had gone home at seven. It was not Beth, who had been at the gate and then in the kitchen. There were four people in that house that day who might have done it, and I have never established which, and it does not matter, and I have never entirely let it go either.

The carpet was clean. The clippers were gone from the dressing table — into a bag, as it turned out, in a cupboard in the boot room, which is a thing my sister did, correctly, and told me about a fortnight afterwards.

The ring was still there.

It had been on top of the hair and now it was on the bare veneer of the table with the lifting corner, next to Ella's ivory bow, and there was a wide clean fan-shaped mark on the carpet where the hair had been lifted away.

Iris's photograph was face down.

I stood there for a while. Then I put it back up.

At half past nine Ella came out of her room, which she was not supposed to be able to do without the stair gate making a noise, and stood in the corridor in her pyjamas with a hood up.

She had put the hood up. Eight years old, in her own house, at half past nine at night, with nobody there but her father.

She said: "Is the party still happening?"

She meant the memorial. She had heard us call it a service and she had heard us call it lunch and at eight years old the word she had for a day when ninety people come to your house is party.

"No," I said. "Not today."

She thought about that.

"Because of me?"

I have been asked what the worst moment was. People ask that. They ask it kindly and they ask it at dinner and they do not know what they are asking for.

It was not the doorway at four minutes past nine. It was not the hair against the photograph. It was not the sound she made.

It was an eight-year-old with a hood up asking whether the day for her mother had been cancelled because of her.

"No," I said. "Not because of you. Because of a thing an adult did. Nothing to do with you."

"Claire," she said.

"Yes."

She stood there with one hand up under the hood, over the side of her head, which was a thing she was going to keep doing for the next four months and which I would come to be able to see from the other end of a room.

Then she said:

"Is she coming back?"

And I said, "No."

And I want it on the record that at half past nine on the night of the eleventh of September I said that, and I meant it, and it was not a promise I made at any point afterwards under pressure or in a solicitor's office or out of anger. I made it at the top of the stairs to a child in a hood, on the first night, before I knew one single fact about why it had happened.

Everything I found out afterwards only ever told me how right it had been.
