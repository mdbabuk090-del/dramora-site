She Shaved My Daughter's Head Two Hours Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 25 — The Bow, Later

Chapter 25 of 25

Ella is fourteen and there is a thing at her school in the summer that is technically a prize evening and is in practice a chance for four hundred people to stand in a marquee on a playing field.

She was reading at it. Not her mother's poem — a thing she had written herself for an anthology the school does, which is about a bus, and which is better than the poem on the back of the prescription letter, and I have not said that out loud to anybody in this family.

On the Wednesday before it she came into the kitchen and said:

"Can I get Mum's bow out of the safe?"

Six years.

That bow had been in a box in the workshop safe for five years, since the February after it happened, when I put it there, and told myself it was for keeping, and Ruth Ellery said, in her office above the dentist: that's all right. But it isn't your bow. Iris didn't make it for you.

I had thought about that sentence probably four hundred times and had done nothing about it, and had been perfectly aware that I was doing nothing about it, which is the sophisticated version of doing nothing.

I said: "Course you can."

Then I said: "Do you want me to get it?"

And she said: "I'll come."

We went down to the works on the Thursday after tea. It was still light. There was nobody there except a lad doing an evening on the spray line who nodded at us through the glass.

I have not described the safe. It is a green Chubb thing from about 1975 that was my father's and that is far too big for anything we keep in it, and it is in the corner of the office under a shelf of ring binders.

In it: the deeds, the insurance, my will, my father's watch, and a box.

In the box: ninety-one folded orders of service with the wrong date on them. A letter from Ontario, four sides. Iris's wedding ring. A letter on one side of A4 signed Claire. And a bow, ivory silk, about the width of my palm, with a seam at the back doubled over on itself because a woman who could not sew ran out of patience in the March.

Ella got it out and turned it over.

She said: "It's a bit rubbish, isn't it."

I said: "It's terrible."

She said: "The loops aren't even the same."

I said: "She got fed up."

And my daughter laughed at her mother, in a workshop office, at fourteen, holding the thing in both hands.

Then she said: "Does it still — is it all right? To wear it?"

And I understood that we had arrived at the actual conversation, five years late, and that I had about four seconds.

I said: "It's yours. It's always been yours."

She said: "That's not what I asked."

Fourteen.

So I sat on the edge of the desk and said the true thing, which I had not said out loud to her ever.

I said: "You're asking whether it's going to upset me."

She said yes.

I said: "It might. That's my problem and I'll deal with it in the car."

And Ella said: "Okay," and put it in her bag, and asked whether we could get chips on the way home.

She wore it on the Friday.

I sat in a marquee on a playing field with four hundred people and watched my daughter walk up onto a low stage with her hair up and a badly made ivory bow at the back of it, and read a poem about a bus.

Nobody in that marquee knew what they were looking at except three people: me, Maya, who had driven over from Wakefield and was sitting four rows back with Tom, and Beth, who was at the end of my row with her programme rolled up in one hand.

Nobody clapped for the bow. Nobody knew. It was a fourteen-year-old with something in her hair.

That is the whole of it. That is what six years and a court and a receipt in an envelope and a microphone nobody went back for actually bought: the right for a thing to be ordinary.

Afterwards there was tea in a hall and Ella went off with about nine other girls, and Maya came over and stood next to me and did not say anything for a bit.

Then she said: "She wore it, then."

I said: "She asked."

Maya said: "I know. She texted me on Wednesday."

I said: "Did she."

And Maya Delgado, who is thirty now and advises a local authority on the welfare of small children and who begins every request to leave a room with no thank you, said:

"She wanted to know if you'd be all right."

That is the thing I want to put down before I finish, and it is not about Claire Vance and it is not about my sister.

For six years my daughter has been managing me.

Carefully, and kindly, and with a skill that she did not get from me. Taking her plate to the sink in the October when she was eight. Asking Maya on a Wednesday whether her father would cope with a hair bow. Saying you didn't know from under a workbench with her hands over her head, at eight years old, to make the man who had switched the router on feel less bad.

Ruth Ellery told me in the fifth year that the person I had not forgiven was not Claire and not Beth, and I said who then, and she waited, in the way they do, and I did not answer her.

The answer is that I was the person who brought her into the house.

Not the wedding. Not the engagement. The hiring. A man in a kitchen in May, being told by a woman he had known for four minutes that everything in a house like this is either an object or a person and she was not going to guess which — and hiring her on the spot to sort out a grief he could not face, and paying her sixty-one thousand pounds over fourteen months to do it one room at a time, and never once asking her what it was doing to her, and never once asking his own daughter what it was doing to her either.

Every single thing that woman removed from my house, she removed with my permission.

Except the last one.

I said some of that to Maya in the school hall, badly, holding a cup of tea.

She let me get to the end of it.

Then she said: "Daniel. Can I say a thing I've wanted to say for about six years?"

I said go on.

And she said: "You've got it the wrong way round. You didn't bring her in because you couldn't face it. You brought her in because you were going to face it. That's why you started at all. The house needed doing and you knew it and you did something about it. She was just — " and she did the thing with her hand that she does, " — she was the wrong person for a right job."

I said: "That's generous."

And Maya said: "It's not, it's just true. And anyway, you can't have it. You want to sit here and be the reason. You're not the reason. She's the reason. She bought them on the second of September."

We got home at about half past ten.

Ella went straight up. I heard her on the main staircase, past the dressing-room door, the way she has walked past it every day for six years.

I went round the house turning things off, which I do now and which I did not do for the first two years because it was Iris's job and I could not have it, and which I have done every night since an eight-year-old sat on the stairs in the dark and told me that somebody has to.

The lamp in the long room. The kitchen. The lamp on the hall table over the drawer where a poem sat for four months in the June after a funeral, before anybody found it.

And at the bottom of the stairs I found the bow on the newel post, where she had taken it off and left it, the way you leave a thing you are going to want tomorrow.

I stood there for a while with my hand on it.

Then I left it exactly where it was, and turned the last light off, and went to bed.
