She Shaved My Daughter's Head an Hour Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 12 — The Difference

Chapter 12 of 25

Owen Pryce has done my family's work for thirty-one years. He did my father's will. He did the purchase of the second unit in 2011 and he did the employment tribunal in 2016 that we lost and should have lost.

He is not a criminal solicitor and he said so at the first meeting and brought in somebody who was, a woman called Priya Raval who works out of Leeds and who is the most frightening polite person I have ever sat across a table from.

But Owen came to every meeting, because I asked him to, and because he understood the thing that Priya did not need to understand in order to do her job.

The thing he understood was that I was not trying to win anything. I had already won everything that was available to win by four minutes past nine on the eleventh of September. The wedding was off, the ring was on a dressing table, the woman was out of my house.

What I wanted was to know what kind of thing had happened.

Owen is the only person in the whole two years who never once told me that this did not matter.

He sat me down on the fifth of November — nine days before the recording surfaced, though nobody knew that yet — and drew two columns on a piece of A4 with a fountain pen, which he still uses, and which I found infuriating for about a year and now find that I miss.

At the top of the left column he wrote: A second.

At the top of the right column he wrote: A plan.

Then he said: "Everything you have found since September goes in one of these two columns. Not where you want it. Where it goes."

We did it out loud. It took about forty minutes.

A second: the argument about the bow. The bow coming away with hair in it. That being genuinely accidental, which I accept and always have. The state of her hands. The fact that she stopped after one pass and did not, on any account including Maya's, attempt a second. The horror on her face when I came through the door, which Maya saw as well and which is in Maya's statement, and which I did not want to be true and which Maya put in anyway, and that is exactly why I believe every other word Maya has ever said.

A plan: the clippers, bought on the second of September, guards asked about at the counter. The dress, ordered on the twenty-second of August. The poem, sent on the twenty-eighth. We'll see how she is, on the fourth. The sentence to my sister on the twentieth. The sentence to Maya in the garden centre in August. The arrangement, four days before, that Maya's room would be needed — which removed the only adult who had ever said no to her in that house.

Owen put the cap on the pen.

He said: "Do you see what you have?"

I said I had a plan.

And he said no.

He said: "You have a plan for an outcome, and a second for a method. They are not the same thing and you must not let yourself believe they are, because if you do, and it turns out you are wrong, you will spend the rest of your life having been unfair to a woman who did something appalling. That is a very poor position to be in. It is much worse than being angry."

Then he explained it, and I have used the explanation since with other people, and I will put it here in his words as near as I can get.

The plan was: Ella is not going to stand up and read that poem.

That is what the dress is for, and the phone call to Ashby, and we'll see how she is. Somebody who intends nothing at all does not order a second child's dress in the correct size six days before asking whether that child could learn the words.

What the plan did not necessarily contain was the method.

Because there are a dozen ways to stop an eight-year-old reading a poem, and most of them are not criminal. You can make her cry at half past eight and let the adults conclude she is not up to it. You can get to the father the night before with concerns about the child's wellbeing. You can simply be standing there, at ten to eleven, saying darling, are you sure, nobody minds, and a great many eight-year-olds will fold.

Owen said: "A woman who buys clippers nine days before may have bought them intending to use them. Or she may have bought them and put them in the room the way you put a hammer on a table when you are angry with a wall. Both of those women are dangerous. Only one of them walked in there that morning meaning to do it."

I said: "Does it matter which?"

And he took his glasses off.

He said: "It matters to two people. It matters to the court, a bit, at sentence. And it matters to your daughter in about nine years' time when she reads this file, because she is going to want to know whether the woman who lived in her house for two years was pretending the whole time, or whether she was somebody who turned into that. Those are different childhoods."

I asked him what he thought.

He said he did not deal in what he thought.

I asked him again in the car park, which is where you get the truth out of solicitors.

He said: "I think she bought them for the sound."

I did not understand.

He said: "To have them there. So that it could be said in a room, with the child present, that this could happen. I think she intended a threat that would make an eight-year-old refuse to go downstairs. And I think she was extremely surprised at about ten to nine to find that the child would not fold and the nanny would not leave."

Then he said the sentence I have never been able to put down:

"The plan was to frighten. What she had not planned for was being defied by a twenty-four-year-old and an eight-year-old at the same time. That is when the second happened. Second and plan, in that order, both true."

I want to say plainly what that means, because it is the finding of this whole account and it is not the satisfying one.

Nobody walked into that room intending to shave a child's head.

Somebody walked into that room having spent three weeks arranging for a child to be removed from her mother's memorial, carrying a tool bought for the purpose of making that removal credible, having already ensured that the one adult who would object would be gone from the house within weeks.

And then two people in that room said no to her, one of whom was eight, and the arrangements ran out, and she reached for the only thing she had left.

That is worse than a plan, actually. A plan would mean there was a limit somewhere.

Priya Raval, who had been letting us talk, said one thing at the end of that meeting, closing her folder.

She said: "For what it's worth, Mr Ashworth, that is roughly what the prosecution will say if it goes to trial. Premeditated humiliation, spontaneous execution. It reads badly in a courtroom because it is untidy."

I said: "Is untidy bad?"

She said: "Untidy is nearly always true. Tidy is nearly always a story somebody built."

Nine days later a man from the film company rang me about a hard disk, and everything in Owen's two columns turned out to be right, in the exact proportions he had guessed, in a woman's own voice.
