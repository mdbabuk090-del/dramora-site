She Shaved My Daughter's Head an Hour Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 24 — Beth, Later

Chapter 24 of 25

My sister has never apologised for the car park and I have never asked her to, and we have had eleven Christmases about it.

Six, actually. It feels like eleven.

Here is where it got to, and I am putting it here rather than in the chapter about August because it took years and because it is the part of this that is not resolved, and I have decided not to resolve it artificially at the end of a story.

For the first year we were careful with each other. She did the food at the memorial in the March and she was faultless and neither of us mentioned anything.

In the second year she started, once or twice, at the edges. A thing at a barbecue about how she had never trusted Claire really. A thing in a car about how she wished she had said something earlier — not I wish I had told you what she said, which is a specific sentence about a specific act, but I wish I'd said something, which is a mist.

I did not take her up on either of them. I have thought about why not, and the answer is that I did not want the conversation, and that this is the identical instinct that ran my house for two years, and that I have not solved it either.

In the third year we had it out. Not by design. At a funeral, of all things — an uncle's — in a car park, of all places, which neither of us noticed until afterwards.

She said: "You're never going to let this go, are you."

And I said: "You've never once said the sentence."

She said: "What sentence?"

I said: "*I knew, and I didn't tell you, and that was mine.*"

And Beth said: "Because I didn't know."

That is the thing that took me three more years to be able to hold.

She is telling the truth.

She did not know. Nobody in this account knew, including me, including Maya, who came closest and who has said herself she did not know, she just could not leave the room.

What Beth had was a sentence in a car park in August about a widower being frightened of his own daughter. It is not knowledge. If you handed that sentence to a hundred sisters, ninety-nine of them would file it under she can be a bit much and forget it inside a week, and they would be right to, because that is what that sentence is nearly always about.

She was unlucky. That is nearly the whole of it. My sister was standing next to a piece of information that turned out, months later, to be part of something, and there was no way to know that at the time, and the only reason it looks like knowledge now is that we are standing at the far end of it.

I know that. I have known it since the third year.

And here is the part I have not been able to get past, and I am going to write it once and then stop.

It is not August.

It is the twenty-ninth of October, in Owen Pryce's office, when he read out the paragraph about the dress ordered on the twenty-second of August, and my sister said oh.

Not what? Not that can't be right.

Oh.

That syllable is a woman having a thing confirmed, and it means that between the twentieth of August and the twenty-ninth of October — through the eleventh of September, through eleven days of my daughter using the back stairs, through a hat indoors and a hood at half past nine at night, through six weeks of me sitting on her sofa saying I can't work out why it happened — my sister had that sentence in her head and it had already started to mean something to her.

Not knowledge. Not proof. A shape she was already carrying, and which she carried past me eleven or twelve times while I asked the question out loud in her own kitchen.

I said something like that to her in that car park at that funeral, badly, and she cried, and I did not.

And she said: "What do you want me to do about it now?"

And I said: "Nothing. There is nothing to do about it now. That's what makes it hard, Beth. If there was something to do about it, we'd have done it four years ago and it would be finished."

Where it got to is this.

She comes at Christmas. She has Ella one weekend most months and they get on enormously and there is a thing they do with a jigsaw that I am not allowed to be involved in. She is my daughter's aunt, permanently, and she is good at it, and I have never once put a single obstacle in front of that and never will, and if I died tomorrow Beth is the person Ella would go to and Beth knows it because it is in the will Owen drafted.

And she and I talk about the works, and the roof, and our mother's grave, and holidays, and we do not talk about anything else.

That is not a rupture. It is not a family destroyed. Anybody watching us at a table in December would see a brother and sister who are perfectly all right.

There is just a room we do not go into, and both of us know where the door is, and this is a house where somebody kept a room shut for eleven months once before and it did not turn out to be a solution.

Maya said something about it, in the fifth year, at Ella's birthday, in the kitchen, with a tea towel.

She had watched Beth and me do the thing we do, which is to be scrupulously pleasant.

She said: "You know she's frightened of you."

I said don't be daft.

Maya said: "She is. She watches your face when she talks about Ella."

I said that was ridiculous, and then I thought about it for a day and a half, and then I rang my sister and asked her to come and do the jigsaw thing on a Sunday instead of a Saturday because Ella had swimming, and she said "oh — yes — right, fine," in a voice I had not heard since we were children, which was relief.

That is the closest thing to a resolution in this chapter and it consists of a change to a date.

Ruth Ellery, whom I did see once more, in the fifth year, for one appointment, about this specifically, said the thing that I have decided to end on.

I asked her whether I was being unfair to my sister.

She said: "Probably. Are you asking me to tell you to stop?"

I said I supposed I was.

And she said: "I'm not going to, because it wouldn't work, and because you're not doing anything to her. You are being slightly cold to a woman who was slightly cowardly. That is a very ordinary family, Daniel. It is not a wound, it is a temperature."

Then she said: "The person you have not forgiven is not your sister."

I said: "Claire?"

And Ruth Ellery said: "No. You forgave Claire the day the letter came and you have not noticed yet."

And she was right, and it took me another year to notice, and it is the last chapter.
