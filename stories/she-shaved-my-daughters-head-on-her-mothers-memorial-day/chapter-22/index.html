She Shaved My Daughter's Head Two Hours Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 22 — The First Haircut After

Chapter 22 of 25

Eleven months.

That is how long it took, and for the first four of them I thought the number was going to be the whole rest of her childhood.

The stripe grew, obviously. Hair does. By the December it was two centimetres of soft stuff standing up in the middle of her head that would not lie down and that she could not do anything with, and by the March, when she stood on the box, it was long enough to be part of the general situation.

The general situation was bad. I want to say that, because there is a way of telling this in which a child's hair grows back and everything is fine, and the truth is that for the better part of a year my daughter's head looked wrong and she knew it and other children knew it.

We did it at home. Maya trimmed the ends twice. After May I did it, badly, with scissors, three times, and she let me, which I understood even at the time was a gift.

She would not go to a salon.

Not asked not to. Would not. In the January, at the shopping centre in Leeds, we walked past one with the door open and she stopped in the middle of the concourse and put her hand up on the top of her head, and I said "we're not going in there" about four times and had to say it in a normal voice while my chest was doing something else entirely.

Ruth was quite relaxed about it, which I found infuriating.

She said: "She's eight. Being frightened of hairdressers is not a life sentence. Don't push it, don't make it a project, and don't tell her she's brave for going past one."

It happened in the August, the same month I did the dressing room, and it happened because of a girl called Priya Tolan, who is nothing to do with Priya Raval and who is, at the time of writing, the person my daughter has been friends with for the longest.

They were going to be in the school play in the September. Ella had a part which required, in the way of school plays, a plait.

She could not do a plait. Not because of her hair, which by then was long enough for most things; because she could not do a plait, and I certainly could not, and Maya had left in the May.

Priya Tolan's mother does hair. Not in a salon — in her kitchen, in the evenings, for people in the village, which is a thing that exists everywhere and that I had lived four miles from for nine years without knowing.

Ella asked to go.

She asked in the way she asks about big things, which is at speed and while doing something else. "Priya's mum can do it and can I go on Thursday."

I said: "Do you want me to come?"

She said: "No."

I have thought a great deal about that no.

There is a version of this chapter, and I nearly wrote it, in which a father takes his daughter to the hairdresser eleven months after the event and sits opposite her and something happens between them.

What actually happened is that my daughter arranged the whole thing herself with a nine-year-old and a woman in a kitchen four miles away, and told me about it as a fait accompli, and asked me not to come.

I sat in the car outside for fifty minutes like a criminal.

She came out with a plait, and a bag of chips that Priya's mother had sent her out with, and got in the car and said:

"She's got the little clipper thing for the back of the neck."

I said, very evenly: "Right."

Ella said: "I said no thank you."

Then she ate a chip.

No thank you.

Maya's phrase. Three years of it in my kitchen, at low volume, every time somebody asked her to leave a room. My daughter picked it up off her and put it in her pocket and got it out in a stranger's kitchen at nine years old.

I did not say anything about it in the car. I am reasonably proud of that.

I rang Maya that night and told her and she was quiet for a second and then said "oh, that's good," in a completely different voice from her usual one, and we talked about something else.

The rest of it is short and undramatic, which is the point.

Priya's mother did it three more times that year. Then in the January Ella went to an actual salon in the town with Priya and Priya's mother and had it cut properly, and I was not there, and I did not know until she came home.

She was nine when she first sat in a chair on her own with a gown on and a stranger behind her holding scissors, and eleven when she stopped needing somebody she knew to be in the room, and twelve before I saw her, once, in a mirror in a salon window, sitting with her back to the door.

That last one took the longest and nobody ever mentioned it out loud, including me.

There is one more thing about hair and then I am finished with it.

In the second year, when she was ten, she asked me again about having it cut short.

The same question I had said no to in the December, fast, in a voice, and had not been able to take back for four days.

This time I said: "Up to you."

And she said: "Really?"

And I said: "It's your hair. It's not — " and then I had to stop for a second, because I had got to the end of a sentence and found something at the bottom of it. " — it's not anybody else's."

She thought about it for about a week and then didn't.

Ruth said, when I told her: "What did you feel when you said yes?"

I said sick.

She said: "And what did you feel afterwards?"

And I sat in that chair above the dentist for a while.

Then I said: "That it was the first time since she was seven that anybody in this family said something about that child's hair that wasn't about her mother."

Ruth put her pen down.

That was the last session. She said she did not think she needed to see me any more, and I said I would like to keep coming, and she said "that's not the same thing, is it," and I laughed for the first time in that room.

She still sees Ella, twice a year, an appointment that exists for no reason except that it exists, and which Ella regards as an enormous inconvenience, and which will carry on until Ella says otherwise.

That was Ruth's condition and I have never asked her to explain it.
