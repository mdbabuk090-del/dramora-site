She Shaved My Daughter's Head Two Hours Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 08 — My Sister Knew

Chapter 8 of 25

Beth is six years older than me and she raised me for about eighteen months when our mother was ill, and there is no version of my life in which I do not owe her.

I say that first because of what comes after it.

She found out on the twenty-ninth of October, in Owen Pryce's office, that a statement had been taken from Claire's sister-in-law about a seven-year-old learning a poem. She had driven me there. She sat in the car park for fifty minutes and then came in for the last ten because I asked her to.

Owen read her the paragraph about the dress being ordered on the twenty-second of August.

And my sister said: "Oh."

Not oh God. Not that can't be right. One syllable, flat, the sound of a person having a thing confirmed.

I have gone over that moment so many times that I no longer trust my memory of the room, but I trust the syllable.

Owen heard it too. He is sixty and he has been doing this for thirty-six years and he did not look up from the page. He said, in exactly the voice he uses for everything: "Beth, is there something you know?"

And she said: "It's not — it isn't the kind of thing you repeat."

Here is what my sister knew.

On the twentieth of August, three weeks and a day before the memorial, Beth and Claire had been to look at a fabric warehouse in Doncaster for the works reception job. They had gone in one car. They had come back at about six and had sat in the car park behind Beth's office for a while because they were talking.

They had been talking about the wedding, which was five weeks away then, and about the honeymoon, and about the room at the back that Maya used.

And Claire had said — Beth's own account, given to the police in November, which I have read twice and will not read again:

"Once we're married it'll settle down. She'll be all right once she stops being the only thing in that house. Somebody's got to be the adult about it. Daniel can't, he's still frightened of her, he thinks if he says no to her Iris dies again."

Beth said she had said: "That's a bit much, Claire."

And Claire had said: "Well. Somebody has to say it."

And then they had talked about parking permits, and Beth had driven home.

That is it. That is the whole thing.

I want to be as fair as I am able to be, because I have had two years to be unfair and I have used most of them.

There is nothing in those sentences about hair. There is nothing about clippers, or a poem, or a niece, or a bare stripe from the hairline back across the crown of an eight-year-old's head. A woman in a car park saying that a widower is too soft with his motherless child is not a plan. It is not even unusual. Half the country says it about the other half.

If Beth had come to me on the twenty-first of August and repeated it word for word, I would have been annoyed with Claire for a day and I would have married her in five weeks.

I know that. I have said it out loud to Owen and to Ruth Ellery and once, badly, to Beth herself.

So why do I still, two years on, find it difficult to sit across a table from my own sister at Christmas.

Because of what she did in the nine weeks afterwards.

Not in August. In September, October, November — after she had stood at my gate for two hours and ten minutes turning cars round in a lane.

She heard me say, in her kitchen, in the last week of September, that I could not work out why it had happened. She heard me say it again in October. She heard me say — and this is the one I cannot get out — she heard me say, sitting on her sofa with a cup of tea going cold in my hand, that I was starting to wonder whether the whole thing had been about the bow and nothing else, and whether I had cancelled a wedding over one terrible minute in a woman's life.

She said: "You did the right thing."

She did not say: I know something that would help you.

She had it. She had the one thing I did not have, which was evidence that on the twentieth of August, three weeks before, Claire Vance was already carrying a fully formed theory in which my daughter was a problem of household management to be corrected after the wedding.

That is not proof of what happened in the dressing room. Owen was clear with me about that too, and he was right, and it never went anywhere near a court.

But I was not in a court. I was a man in a kitchen trying to decide what kind of thing had happened to his child, and my sister let me do that arithmetic for nine weeks with a piece missing that she was holding.

I asked her why, in November, in her kitchen, with both of us standing up.

She gave me three answers in about four minutes and I have thought about all three.

The first was: "It was said in confidence."

I did not answer that one. There is nothing to say to it. A confidence is a thing between two adults about a third adult; it stops being a confidence somewhere around the point where the third party is eight.

The second was: "I didn't want you to think it was my fault."

That one is true and it is human and I have some sympathy with it, because she introduced Claire, and because she had said Ella's growing up in a museum on a Sunday in the spring, and because she had spent nine weeks quietly terrified that everything downstream of those two facts was hers.

It is still the wrong reason. Protecting your own position is the reason people always give. It is what Maya was doing in August and it is what the house manager was doing when he wrote a note about a family-preference dispute. My sister did the same thing that the twenty-four-year-old employee did — except the twenty-four-year-old had something to lose, and my sister did not.

The third answer was the honest one, and she gave it standing at the sink with her back to me.

She said: "Because if I'd told you, you'd have had to do something about it. And then it would have been me who stopped your wedding."

There is a thing that happens in families with a bit of money and a bit of standing, and I have watched it operate my whole life without ever seeing it, in the way you do not see the shape of your own kitchen.

Everybody defers upward, and nobody wants to be the one who carries the bad thing into the room.

Maya did not tell me what she heard in the garden centre, because it would have been the nanny saying it. Beth did not tell me what she heard in the car park, because it would have been the sister stopping the wedding. The house manager wrote family-preference dispute in a note in July about a woman turning a photograph face down, because escalating it would have been him deciding that the future Mrs Ashworth was a problem.

Three separate adults, none of them stupid, none of them unkind, all of them doing the same arithmetic: the cost of saying this lands on me, and the cost of not saying it lands somewhere I cannot see.

And at the centre of that arrangement, receiving nothing, was me — a man who had spent two years telling everybody that he was fine and that the house was fine and that Claire was very good, and who had made it perfectly clear, in a hundred small ways he could not now list, that he did not want to be told otherwise.

Nobody built that system. It is what grows when a man will not be contradicted and does not know he will not be contradicted.

I have changed some things at the works since. There is a route now by which a person can raise something about a child or a safeguarding matter that does not pass through anybody's line manager, and it was not my idea, it was the HR consultant's, and I signed it in about ninety seconds.

You cannot install one of those in a family.

Beth and I are all right now, in the way that means we speak every week and have never once discussed it since that kitchen.

She comes at Christmas. Ella loves her. Ella does not know any of this and will not until she is old enough to read the file, which she is entitled to do and which I have kept complete for that reason, including the parts that are about me.

Once, about a year afterwards, Beth said to me at the sink, in the same spot: "Do you want me to say sorry again?"

And I said no.

And she said: "Good, because I don't know what for. I didn't do anything."

And I said: "I know. That's it, that's the whole thing," and she did not understand, and I did not explain it, and we did the washing up.

She is right that she did not do anything.

That is what I cannot get past.
