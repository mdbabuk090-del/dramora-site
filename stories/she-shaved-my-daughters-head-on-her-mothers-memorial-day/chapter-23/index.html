She Shaved My Daughter's Head an Hour Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 23 — The Letter

Chapter 23 of 25

Claire wrote to Ella in the fourth year.

It did not come to my house. The restraining order had eighteen months left to run on it at that point and she did not breach it, at any time, in any way, over five years — I want that recorded, because I have said a great many hard things about her in this account and that one belongs on the other side of the page.

It came through a solicitor to Owen Pryce, in an envelope inside an envelope, with a covering letter that said the writer understood entirely if it was not passed on, and asked for it not to be returned but destroyed.

Owen rang me and said: "I have something and I want you to sit down before I tell you what it is, and then I want you to do nothing about it for a fortnight."

I did nothing about it for nine days, which for me is a fortnight.

I did not read it first.

That decision took four sessions' worth of thinking and I did not have a psychologist any more, so I did it the hard way, in a workshop, over about three weeks, and then I rang Ruth Ellery and paid for one appointment to check my working.

The argument for reading it first is obvious. She was eleven. There was a real possibility of it containing something that would hurt her, and I am her father, and it is my job to open doors first.

The argument against is that she was eleven, and that it was addressed to her, and that the entire structure of what had been done to her was that adults kept deciding, for excellent reasons, what she could and could not have.

Ruth listened to the whole thing and said: "You've already decided."

I said I wanted a professional opinion.

She said: "You've got one. I'd read it first."

I said: "Really?"

And Ruth said: "Yes. And I think you're going to do the other thing, and I think that's also defensible, and I want you to notice that you've asked me in order to be able to say a psychologist was involved."

That is why she was worth seventy pounds an hour.

I did the other thing.

I sat down with Ella at the kitchen table on a Sunday and told her that Claire had written her a letter, that it was in the house, that I had not read it, that she could read it now or in a year or never, that I would read it with her or not, and that nobody was going to ask her about it afterwards.

She said: "Have you read it?"

I said no.

She said: "Why not?"

I said: "Because it's yours."

She looked at the envelope for a while, on the table.

Then she said: "Can I read it upstairs?"

She was up there for eleven minutes.

I did the washing up for eleven minutes with my hands in water that had gone cold.

She came down and put it on the table and said: "You can read it if you want."

I said: "Do you want me to?"

She said: "I don't mind."

That is an eleven-year-old for yes, and I have got that wrong before, and I did not get it wrong that day. I read it at the table with her sitting opposite me eating an apple.

It is one side of A4 and I am going to put the whole of it here because it is short and because leaving parts out would be a kind of lying.

Ella,

I cut your hair in your mother's room on the morning of her memorial. I did it deliberately. I did it so that you would not be able to stand up and read your mother's poem in front of everybody.

It was not because you were naughty and it was not because of the bow. You did not do anything at all. There was nothing you could have done differently.

I did it because I was jealous of your mother, who was dead, and because I could not be jealous of her out loud, so I was jealous of her at you instead. That is the whole reason. There is not a better one underneath it.

I also want to say clearly, because I think adults have probably said careful things to you: I knew what I was doing. I bought the clippers nine days before. I asked your cousin Sophie to learn the poem. Nobody made me do any of it and I was not out of my mind.

You made me very welcome when I came into your house and you were six. I remember that and I have not forgiven myself for it and I am not asking you to.

You do not have to write back. Please do not write back if you would rather not, and please do not let anybody tell you that you should. Your father will not have told you to, I am fairly sure of that.

I am sorry. It is not enough and I know it is not enough, and I would rather send something that is not enough than nothing at all.

Claire

I put it down.

Ella said: "Is it true? The nine days thing."

I said yes.

She said: "You told me that ages ago."

I said I had.

She ate the apple for a bit.

Then she said: "It's better than the other one."

I said what other one.

And my daughter said: "The one where she says she didn't mean it."

She never replied. She has never mentioned replying. She was not sad for the rest of that day and she did not sleep badly and there was no delayed thing three weeks later, which I had braced for so hard that its non-arrival was almost annoying.

What she did do, about six months later, was ask where the letter was kept.

I said in the box in the safe with the bow and her grandmother's letter and the orders of service.

She said: "Right."

And that was all, and it is still there.

The one professional opinion I did get afterwards was Ruth's, at the twice-a-year appointment, and I had to ask permission to hear it, which is how that works — Ruth asked Ella whether she minded if she told me what she thought about the letter, and Ella said, apparently: "he can know."

Ruth said the letter was, technically speaking, close to the best thing of its kind she had seen in thirty years, and that she suspected several drafts and a good therapist behind it.

Then she said the thing that I have kept:

"It is unusual because it does not ask for anything. Almost all of them ask for something. They ask to be understood, or they explain what was happening in the writer's life, or they say they think about the child every day, which sounds like love and is in fact a request. This one gives her the facts and gets out."

I said: "Does that mean she's changed?"

And Ruth said: "It means she has learned how to write a letter. It might mean the other thing as well, and there is no way for you to find out, and you do not need to know."

There is a version of the ending of this in which the letter is a redemption and the two of them meet in a neutral room and something is closed.

That has not happened and I do not expect it to.

Claire Vance lives in Leeds. She works. She married a man called David in the sixth year — Mark told me in the yard, in exactly the tone he tells me about a boiler — and I understand there are stepchildren, and I have thought about that for longer than I have told anybody.

I asked Owen once, professionally, whether there was anything to be done about that thought.

He said no. He said the conviction is on the record, and the record does what the record does, and that a man in my position going any further than that would be a man deciding he is a court.

Then he said: "You know what she wrote at the end. I knew what I was doing. People who write that sentence to an eleven-year-old have generally stopped being the danger they were."

I said: "Generally."

And Owen Pryce, who has done my family's work for thirty-seven years and who is now sixty-six and still uses a fountain pen, said: "Yes. Generally. That is the most anybody gets, Daniel. You've had more certainty out of this than nearly anybody ever gets, and it cost you a microphone nobody went back for."
