She Shaved My Daughter's Head Two Hours Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 05 — Nine Days

Chapter 5 of 25

The clippers were a Wahl cordless set, the kind a barber uses, with a charging stand and four guards in a plastic sleeve.

Claire's account said they were hers, that she had bought them for a cockapoo called Wilf that she had rehomed with her brother in the spring when she began spending four nights a week at my house, and that they had been living in the bottom of her overnight bag ever since.

That is a good answer. I want to give it its due. It explains why a set of clippers was in a dressing room. It explains why they were charged. It makes the whole thing a story about an object that happened to be to hand, which is the difference between a bad minute and something else entirely.

It was not true, and the way it stopped being true is so ordinary that I still find it hard to describe without sounding as though I am making it up.

A woman in the accounts office at my own workshop opened an envelope.

Claire had done four projects for us over the eighteen months. Not the house — the house was private, and I paid for the house myself, which matters later. The four projects were commercial: a showroom refit in Leeds, two trade-fair stands, and the reception at the works.

She invoiced through a company, Vance Interiors Ltd, and she expensed materials, and because she had once had a row with an accountant about digital receipts she was in the habit of doing something that almost nobody does any more, which is putting the paper ones in an envelope and posting them.

On the thirtieth of September an envelope arrived at the works with twelve receipts in it for the last trade stand.

Ten of them were for the trade stand.

The twelfth was a Wahl Super Taper cordless kit, £73.99, bought at a trade counter on the industrial estate at Ashby, at 11:41 in the morning on the second of September.

Nine days before the memorial.

Six months after Wilf went to live with her brother.

The woman in accounts, whose name is Pam and who has worked for my father and then for me for twenty-six years, looked at it for a while, and then did not put it in the pile, and then did not put it in the bin, and then walked up to my office with it and put it on the desk and said: "Daniel, I don't know if this is anything."

Owen Pryce asked me two questions about that receipt and both of them were the right ones.

The first was: is it possible she bought a second set for the dog after the dog had gone.

The second was: what does the trade counter keep.

The trade counter keeps card transaction records and, because it is a trade counter on an industrial estate that has had two break-ins, forty-five days of camera footage on a loop.

The receipt was the thirtieth of September. The purchase was the second.

We were inside the loop by six days.

I want to be precise about what happened next, because there is a version of this story where I am a clever man who put it all together, and that version is a lie.

I did not obtain that footage. I did not ask anybody at the trade counter for anything. Owen took the receipt to the officer in the case at nine o'clock the following morning, and the officer requested the footage through the proper channel, and it came back nine days later, and I did not see it for another three weeks.

The reason I mention that is that in October I very nearly drove down there myself. I got as far as the car. Owen said one sentence to me on the telephone that stopped it, and the sentence was:

"If you go, it becomes about you."

He has said many useful things to me. That is the one I have used most since, in the workshop, in a school office, once at three in the morning to myself in a kitchen.

If you go, it becomes about you.

The footage showed a woman in a green coat at the counter at 11:41 on the second of September.

She asked for clippers. There is no sound; the counter man remembered the conversation and made a statement about it four weeks later, which is not the same as sound, and which a defence would have gone at hard if it had ever come to that.

He remembered her because she asked him a question he did not expect and did not get asked in that shop, which was whether the guards could be taken off.

He said yes.

She asked whether it would still work without them.

He said yes, but you don't want to do that on a person, love. That is his phrase, in his statement, and he says he laughed when he said it and that she laughed too.

Then she paid with a card in the name of Vance Interiors Ltd and put the box in a canvas bag and left.

Here is what the receipt did and did not do.

It did not prove that she went to that trade counter intending to shave my daughter's head. Owen was extremely clear with me about that, twice, sitting forward with his hands on his knees, because he could see what was happening behind my face.

There are innocent readings. She had a dog once. People buy things. Nine days is nine days and a person can buy a set of clippers on a Tuesday for no reason at all.

What it did was destroy the sentence they were in my bag.

Because if the clippers were bought nine days before the memorial, then the account she had given four days after it — through a solicitor, in writing, having had time and advice — was not a mistake about a detail. It was a story constructed to make an object innocent.

And a person who constructs a story to make an object innocent knows the object is not innocent.

Owen said: "This is not proof of what she meant to do. It is proof of what she knows she did."

I have said that I did not ask Ella what happened.

On the second of October, the day after Pam brought me the envelope, I broke that. Not with a question — I had been drilled by then, by a psychologist called Ruth Ellery whom I will come to, on the difference between asking a child a question and putting an idea into a child.

I was sitting on the floor of Ella's room with my back against the bed. She was doing something at her desk. Her hair was at the stage where it had gone from a shaved patch to a fuzz you could feel with the flat of your hand, and she had a hat on indoors, which she had started doing in the last week of September and which Ruth had told me to let her do.

I did not ask her anything. I said, to the room, in the flat voice I had been taught:

"If you ever want to tell me anything about that morning, I'll listen, and I won't be cross, and I won't do anything about it unless you want me to."

She kept drawing for about forty seconds.

Then she said: "She was cross before."

I said: "Mm."

That is all you are allowed to say. Mm. It is the hardest thing I have ever done with my mouth.

Ella said: "In the car. When we got the flowers for Mummy. She was cross in the shop and she was smiling."

Then she said: "She said my hair was too much."

I said: "Mm."

And Ella said, without turning round, in the voice of a child describing a fact about the world:

"Because Mummy in the picture has it."

That is not evidence. I want to be very clear.

An eight-year-old, three weeks after the event, having been living in a house where adults have been talking, saying something to her father who is sitting on her floor. Every part of that is contaminated. Ruth Ellery said so herself, kindly, when I told her, and then she said something else.

She said: "It isn't evidence. But you asked me in September whether Ella understood what had happened to her, and you were hoping she didn't."

"Yes."

"She does," Ruth said. "She's understood it from the first day. She's been waiting for you."
