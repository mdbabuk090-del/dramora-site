She Shaved My Daughter's Head an Hour Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 09 — The Bow Iris Made

Chapter 9 of 25

The bow was ivory silk, about the width of my palm, and it was made out of the offcuts of my wife's grandmother's wedding dress.

That is a sentence that sounds like an heirloom and it is not one. Nobody kept that dress in a box in an attic. It was in a cupboard in Kingston, Ontario, going yellow, and when Iris and I got married her mother cut two metres out of the skirt of it to make a lining for something, and the rest of it went to a charity shop, and Iris kept the offcuts in a bag in the bottom of a wardrobe for nine years because she was the kind of person who could not throw fabric away and did not know why.

She made the bow in the March. In the same eight weeks she wrote the poem.

She could not sew. I want to be accurate about this because Ella has heard people say her mother was clever with her hands and it is not true. She was a physiotherapist who could dead-lift a fifteen-stone man off a bathroom floor and she could not sew a button on. The bow is not well made. There is a seam at the back of it that is doubled over on itself because she ran out of patience, and the loops are not the same size, and you can see all of that from a metre away.

She made it for Ella's seventh birthday, which was in the June, and which she did not get to.

Ella wore it for the first time at her mother's funeral.

I did not put it in her hair that day. Maya did. I was not capable of putting anything in anybody's hair on that day, and Maya, who was twenty-two and had been working in the house for eleven months, took a seven-year-old into a bathroom and did it, and did not tell me she had done it, and I saw it in the second row of the crematorium and had to look at the ceiling for a while.

After that it became the thing.

Not every day. She was not a child who wore one item obsessively; Ruth Ellery asked me about that carefully, twice, because there is a version of it that is a symptom. It was not that. It was: birthdays, Christmas, the day of the school play, the day she started year three, the Sunday we went to put the stone in.

The days that counted. She had worked out on her own, at seven, that there was a category of day, and she had assigned an object to it.

And the memorial was going to be the biggest one.

Now. Here is the thing about the bow that I did not see for a long time, and that Claire saw immediately, and that Iris's mother got in four seconds on a telephone line from Ontario.

A photograph of a dead woman on a dressing table is a thing you can get used to. You can walk past it eleven times a day. It sits still and it does not change and after fourteen months it becomes part of the wall.

A bow in a living child's hair is not that.

It moves around the house. It arrives at breakfast. It is at the table on the days that count — which are precisely the days that a new woman marrying into that house would also be counting, because they are the days when she is being watched to see whether she is the mother yet.

Every time that bow came out of a drawer, it announced that this was a day of significance, and that on days of significance this family belonged to somebody who was not in the room.

Claire never once said anything about the photograph. She dusted round it for fourteen months.

She said something about the bow four times that I know of, and all four are in the file:

It's a bit much for school, isn't it. (April, to Maya.)

You could put that away for best. (June, to Ella, and Ella said it was for best, and Claire laughed and said good girl.)

It'd be a mercy if she just cut it off, wouldn't it. That hair. (August, garden centre — the hair, not the bow, and I will come back to why those had become the same object.)

And on the eleventh of September, in the dressing room, a minute or two after nine: you're not carrying your mother about on your head at my table.

On your head.

That is the sentence that turned the bow into the hair.

Because at some point in that summer — and I cannot date it, and there may be no date — the object Claire could not tolerate stopped being a piece of silk that could be put in a drawer and became the thing it was pinned to.

Ella has Iris's hair. Not a bit like it. It is the same hair: the same dark chestnut, the same weight, the same wave that starts about halfway down and cannot be brushed out. There is a photograph of Iris at nine on the stairs at her parents' house in Kingston, and there is a photograph of Ella at seven in the same jumper, because her grandmother sent it, and I have shown those two photographs to about four people in my life and every one of them has taken a moment.

The photograph on the dressing table — the one the hair came down against — is Iris at thirty-one, on the beach at Filey, with her hair down.

So on the eleventh of September there were three of them in that room, if you count the way Claire had started counting: a dead woman on the table, a piece of her wedding dress on a child's head, and the child's hair, which was the dead woman's hair, growing back every day no matter what anybody did about the house.

You can put a bow in a drawer.

You can take a photograph off a table — and she did, in the July, and turned it face down, and Maya turned it back.

There is only one of the three you cannot get out of the room without a tool.

I have wondered whether Claire understood any of that about herself. I asked Owen once and he said it was not a question a solicitor could help me with. I asked Ruth Ellery and she said something more useful, which was that people do not generally do things like this because they have understood themselves. They do them because they have not.

The nearest thing to an answer came in the plea hearing, from her own barrister, in mitigation, and I heard it sitting in the second row with my hands on my knees.

He said his client had been under significant strain in the period before the ceremony. He said she had been carrying out an extended and emotionally complex professional engagement in the home of the deceased while simultaneously preparing to join that family. He said — and this is the phrase, I wrote it on the back of the parking ticket in my pocket — that she had lost the ability to distinguish between her professional role and her personal position in the house.

I sat there and thought: that is the truest sentence anybody has said about this, and it is being said in mitigation.

Because it is exactly what happened, and it is not an excuse, and it is nobody's fault but the two of us who arranged it.

I hired a woman to remove my dead wife from my house.

She was extremely good at it. Fourteen months, sixty-one thousand pounds, one room at a time, with my signature on every stage, and at the end of the fourteen months she had removed everything I had authorised her to remove and the house was better and I was grateful.

And then there were three things left that were not on the schedule, and one of them was a photograph, and one of them was a bow, and one of them was eight years old.

The bow is in a box in the workshop safe now, with the ninety-one folded orders of service.

Not because it is contaminated. Ruth was very firm with me about that, in a session in the February, when I told her what I had done with it.

She said: "Does Ella know it's in there?"

I said yes.

She said: "Did she ask for it to be in there, or did you put it there?"

And I sat in that chair for a while.

Then I said: "I put it there."

She said: "Then it's yours in the safe, not hers."

I said that I had not been able to look at it since the eleventh of September without seeing the dressing table.

And Ruth Ellery said the thing that took me another eleven months to be able to act on, and which is chapter twenty-five of this, and which I did not thank her for at the time:

"That's all right. But it isn't your bow. Iris didn't make it for you."
