She Shaved My Daughter's Head Two Hours Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 06 — The Poem

Chapter 6 of 25

Iris wrote one poem in her life that anybody else ever saw.

She was not a poet. She was a physiotherapist who worked with stroke patients for twelve years and could put a grown man back on his feet by being ruder to him than his family were allowed to be. She read almost nothing. She had opinions about music that I found genuinely embarrassing.

In the March before she died, in the eight weeks when the second round of steroids gave her a stretch of clear afternoons, she wrote fourteen lines on the back of a repeat-prescription letter and put it in the drawer of the hall table, and I found it in the June after, in a drawer I had been avoiding.

It is not a good poem. I have been told by a woman at Ella's school that it is a good poem and I have thanked her and I do not agree. It has a rhyme in it that does not work and a line about a kitchen door that only means anything to four people alive.

It is about the house. It is about a woman going round a house at night turning things off, and the last two lines are about how the house will not notice when it is somebody else doing it.

Those two lines are why we were having a memorial at all. I had been unable to read them out at the funeral. I had told myself for two years that when I could read them out, that would be the day.

Then in July, sitting on the wall by the vegetable beds, Ella said she wanted to do it.

She was eight. I said no.

I said no for about a fortnight and I said it for reasons that I still think were sound: that it was a lot of people, that she had never spoken in front of more than a class, that she would be standing up in a room full of adults who would all be crying, and that if it went wrong it would go wrong for ever.

She kept asking. Not on and on — she is not that kind of child. She asked about once every four days, in the way you would raise an agenda item.

In the end I asked her why.

She said: "Because I can say it without the paper."

She could. She had learned it, on her own, off a photocopy I did not know she had, sometime in the spring. She said it to me on that wall with her hands on her knees, all fourteen lines, and got the rhyme that does not work slightly wrong in the same place I always get it wrong, and I said yes.

So it went on the order of service. It was printed. Ninety-one copies of a folded card with a photograph of Iris on the front, and inside, halfway down:

Reading — Ella Ashworth

Those cards were in a box in the coach house for four days after the eleventh of September, under the flowers, and then I put them in the workshop safe, which is not a rational place to keep ninety-one folded cards.

On the twenty-fourth of October, Owen Pryce telephoned and asked me whether Ella had ever met a child called Sophie Vance.

Sophie is Claire's brother's daughter. She was seven. She lived at that time in Ashby, nine miles away, and she had been to my house twice: once at Easter, once for a barbecue in July.

Ella did not much like her, in the way that eight-year-olds do not much like seven-year-olds, which is about nothing.

I said yes, twice, why.

Owen said: "Because her mother has made a statement."

Sophie's mother — Claire's sister-in-law, a woman I had met three times and thought was pleasant, which she is — had been asked routinely, as part of the file, whether she was aware of any arrangements concerning the memorial.

And she said yes.

She said that in the last week of August, Claire had telephoned her and asked whether Sophie was still doing her speech and drama on a Wednesday, and had said that there was a possibility Sophie might be asked to do a reading at a family service in September, and would she mind learning something in case.

She said Claire had sent her the poem by message on the twenty-eighth of August.

She said Sophie had practised it eleven or twelve times over the following fortnight, in the kitchen, with her mother holding the phone with the words on it.

She said that when she had asked Claire, on the fourth of September, whether it was definite yet, Claire had replied: Ella might not be up to it. We'll see how she is.

I read that statement standing up, in Owen's office, and had to put it down and go and stand at the window.

We'll see how she is.

On the fourth of September my daughter was entirely well. She was riding a bicycle badly on the yard. She was, that week, absolutely certain and vocal about the fact that she was going to stand up in front of ninety-one people and say her mother's poem without the paper.

There was no version of the fourth of September in which anybody was waiting to see how she was.

Unless somebody already knew what she was going to be.

Owen took me through the whole thing again the following day, slowly, because he had watched me leave his office the day before and did not like the way I had done it.

He said: this is the most important document in the file, and it is also the one you must be most careful with.

Because there is an innocent reading. There is always an innocent reading, and it is his job to say it to me before somebody else does.

The innocent reading is this: a woman about to become a stepmother, in the run-up to an emotionally enormous day, privately worries that an eight-year-old will fall apart in front of ninety people. She quietly arranges a backup so that the moment is covered. She does not tell the father because the father is fragile and because she thinks it will not be needed. When she says we'll see how she is, she means only that.

Owen said: "A jury of twelve people, seven of whom have children, would understand that reading perfectly."

I said: "Do you believe it?"

And he did the thing he does, which is to take his glasses off.

He said: "The dress."

Sophie had a dress.

Cream, with a green sash, from a shop in Leeds that does not do next-day delivery. It was ordered on the twenty-second of August, in Sophie's size, and paid for on a card belonging to Vance Interiors Ltd, and delivered to Claire's brother's house.

The order was placed six days before the poem was sent.

Six days before.

Owen put his glasses back on and said, in the same voice he uses for talking about drainage easements:

"You do not buy the dress before you ask whether the child will learn the words. Unless you already know which child is going to be standing there."

There is a version of me that would like to tell you that at this point I felt something dramatic. I have read accounts by people in my position which have a great deal of ice water in the veins and hands going cold.

What I felt was extremely stupid.

Because I had walked past all of it. Every one of these things happened in my own life, in front of me, in a five-week window: an envelope of receipts, a phone call to Ashby, a dress arriving at a house nine miles away, a woman in a garden centre saying it would be a mercy, a young employee saying no three times in a bedroom.

And what I had been doing in that same five-week window, according to the calendar on my own desk, was choosing hymns and worrying about parking.

Ruth Ellery told me later that this is close to universal and has a name, and that the name is not helpful, and that the thing to understand is that people do not miss the signs because they are foolish. They miss them because seeing them would require dismantling something they have already spent a year building.

I said: "I wasn't building anything. I was getting on with it."

She said: "You were building the version of your life where you were all right."

Ella never asked, afterwards, whether the poem had happened.

That is the part I did not expect. I had braced for it. I had rehearsed an answer about how we would do it another day, and I had a whole speech about how a poem is not a day, and I never once got to use it.

She simply stopped mentioning it. From the eleventh of September, for five months, she did not say one word about the poem, or the reading, or her mother's handwriting on the back of a prescription letter.

I asked Ruth about it in November.

She said: "She's taken it off the table."

I said: "Why?"

And Ruth said: "Because the last time she wanted something out loud, this is what happened."
