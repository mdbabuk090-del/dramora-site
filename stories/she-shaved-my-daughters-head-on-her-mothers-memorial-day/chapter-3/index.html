She Shaved My Daughter's Head Two Hours Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 03 — Maya's Statement

Chapter 3 of 25

Maya Delgado had worked in my house for two years and four months.

Three days a week, term time, plus whatever we agreed in the holidays. She was not a nanny in the sense that people from a certain kind of family mean it. She was twenty-two when she started, six months after Iris was diagnosed, and the job as advertised was help with a child and a household during a period of illness, which is an advertisement I wrote myself at a kitchen table at two in the morning and which I could not read back now without sitting down.

She had done a childcare qualification and two years at a nursery in the town. Her mother cleans at the hospital. She stayed on after Iris died because I asked her to and because Ella held on to her leg in the hall when I asked, in front of her, which was not fair of me and which worked.

She was not a governess. She was not staff in the way my grandmother would have used the word. She sorted uniform, she did the school run twice a week, she knew that Ella will eat anything at all if it is cut up and nothing whatever if it is not, and she was the only adult in that house who had never once in two years said the phrase your poor daddy where the child could hear it.

I say all this because of what happened to her account of that morning, and because of the reason it happened, and because the reason is me.

She gave her statement to the police on the second day, in the kitchen, with a woman constable and a mug of tea she did not drink.

I have read it. It is two and a half pages.

She said that at just before nine Claire had come into Ella's room where Maya was doing her hair, and had asked Maya to go down and check that the flowers for the long room had come in the right colours.

Maya said she would go in a minute.

Claire said now, please.

Maya said she would go when she had finished Ella's hair.

That is the first thing in the statement that matters and I did not understand it for weeks. A twenty-four-year-old employee, on the morning of a memorial, in a house full of people, said no to the woman who was going to be married to her employer in five weeks. Twice.

The constable asked her why she had not gone.

Maya said: "Because I wasn't going to leave her."

The constable asked: leave who.

Maya said: "Ella. With her."

She was asked when she had started thinking like that.

She said August.

Then, in the statement: Claire told Ella to take the bow out of her hair.

Ella said no.

Claire said it was not a day for that bow. She said it in front of Maya. She said, and this is Maya's wording in the statement, you're not carrying your mother about on your head at my table.

My table.

Ella put both hands on the bow, which is what an eight-year-old does, and Claire took hold of the bow and it came out with some hair with it, and Ella made the sound I heard downstairs.

Maya put herself between them. She said she said, "Claire, don't," and that Claire told her to leave the room, and that she said no for the third time.

Then Claire picked up the clippers.

They were on the dressing table. Maya's statement says she had noticed them there when she came into the room at about ten to nine and had assumed they were mine, and that when Claire picked them up she still assumed it was a threat and nothing else, because nobody in that room had any reason to think otherwise.

The clippers were not mine.

I do not own a set of clippers. I have my hair cut in the town by a man who has cut it since I was nine.

She was asked how long it took.

She said: "It was one go. She did one go and I got hold of Ella and pulled her back and then Mr Ashworth came in."

She was asked whether Claire had said anything while she did it.

And here is the sentence that I built nine weeks on top of, and that a solicitor took apart in ninety seconds in November, gently, and correctly.

Maya said: "She said, now you don't look like your mother."

The trouble with that sentence is not that it is unbelievable.

The trouble is who says it.

Claire's account, given four days later through her solicitor, agreed with about sixty per cent of Maya's. She agreed there had been an argument about the bow. She agreed she had taken hold of it. She agreed she had picked up the clippers — hers, bought for a dog she had had until the spring, she said, and kept in her overnight bag, which is a set of facts I will come back to in chapter five because one of them is not true.

She said she had meant to frighten her.

She said Ella had jerked her head backwards into the blade.

She said she had never said anything about Iris at all, that she would never have said such a thing, and that Maya Delgado had made it up.

And then she said the thing that did the damage.

She said Maya Delgado had made it up because Maya Delgado had been told, four days before the memorial, that her room in the house would be needed after the wedding.

That was true.

Not the making up. The room.

Claire had told her. Not me — I did not know until I read it in a document. Claire had had a conversation with Maya in the utility room on the seventh of September in which she had explained, pleasantly, that after the wedding the arrangement would be reviewed, that the room at the back would be wanted for other purposes, and that of course nothing was decided.

Of course nothing was decided.

So when it came to it, on paper, in the flat language these things end up in: the only witness to the words spoken in that room was a twenty-four-year-old employee who had been given notice, in substance, by the accused, four days before.

I have had that explained to me by three separate people, all of whom were doing their jobs properly, none of whom were being cruel.

It is called a motive to fabricate.

It does not mean she lied. It means that when it is her word against the other, her word is worth less, and everybody in the room knows the arithmetic including her.

Maya came to me on the sixth day and offered to resign.

We were in the kitchen. She had her coat on before she said it, which told me she had planned the whole thing including the leaving.

I said no, automatically, immediately, the way you do — and then I heard myself, and I stopped, because I had just done to her exactly what everybody in that situation does, which is to make a decision about a woman's life in her employer's kitchen without asking her anything.

So I asked her what she wanted.

She stood there for a long moment.

"I don't know," she said.

That was the first honest answer anybody had given me since the eleventh.

We sat down. I did the thing I should have done in August, which was to ask her what she had seen in August.

And she told me about the car park.

Not my sister's car park — that came later, and my sister's is worse. A different one. The Tuesday before the bank holiday, outside the garden centre on the Ashby road, where Maya had taken Ella to buy a plant for Iris's stone.

Claire had come with them. Claire had been perfectly pleasant all morning.

And at the till, when Ella had chosen a small white geranium because it matched the bow, Claire had said — lightly, to Maya, over the child's head, in the voice of somebody making conversation:

"It'd be a mercy if she just cut it off, wouldn't it. That hair. She'd be a completely different child."

Maya said she had laughed, because that is what you do, and had felt sick in the car, and had not been able to explain to herself why.

I said: "Why didn't you tell me?"

And Maya Delgado, twenty-four, three days a week, looked at me across my own kitchen table and said:

"Because it would have been me saying it."

I have gone back to that answer many times.

Not because you wouldn't have believed me. She never said that, and I do not think she thought it. She thought something worse and more accurate.

She thought that in that house, in the run-up to that wedding, a sentence like that one, repeated by a twenty-four-year-old who was paid to be there, would have become a story about a twenty-four-year-old who was paid to be there.

She was not being cowardly. She was doing arithmetic.

I had built a house where a young woman could watch something and be right about it and still calculate, correctly, that saying it out loud would cost her more than staying quiet.

That is not Claire's doing. Claire arrived in April of the previous year.

That is mine.
