She Shaved My Daughter's Head Two Hours Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 18 — Sophie's Father

Chapter 18 of 25

Claire's brother is called Mark and he fits kitchens.

I had met him five times: twice at my house, once at his, once at a pub in Ashby with about a dozen other people, and once at the engagement thing in April that Claire wanted and I did not.

He is two years older than her. He drives a van with his own name on it. He is the kind of man who, at somebody else's party, ends up looking at the boiler.

He wrote to me in the March, a month after the sentencing, on paper, in a hand that slopes badly, and the letter came to the works and not the house because he did not know whether he was allowed to send anything to the house. That is in the first line of it: I've sent this to the yard because I didn't know if I'm allowed to send it to yours. If I'm not allowed to send it at all, chuck it, and I'm sorry.

I did not answer it for nine days.

Then I rang him and said come to the works, not the house, on a Saturday, when there is nobody there.

He came at ten. He had his van and he had a folder.

He would not sit down for about four minutes. He stood in my father's office with his hands in his jacket and said the sentence he had come to say, which he had obviously rehearsed in the van:

"My daughter learned that poem."

I said I knew.

He said: "In my kitchen. On the phone. Her mum holding the phone up. Eleven or twelve times, we counted after, when the police came."

I said I knew that too.

Then he said the part he had not rehearsed, and his voice went.

He said: "I've had to explain to a seven-year-old why she isn't allowed to say the poem any more. And she keeps asking if she did something."

Here is what I have to be honest about, and it is not creditable.

For four months I had thought about Sophie Vance approximately never. She had been a fact in a file. Cecilia dress ordered 22 August, size 7–8, cream with green sash, paid Vance Interiors Ltd. An item of evidence in a column of Owen's A4 page.

She was seven years old, she was nine miles away, and she had spent a fortnight learning fourteen lines written by a woman she had never met, on the back of a repeat-prescription letter, because her auntie had asked her to and because her mother had held the phone.

And at the end of it a police officer had come to her house.

That child has also had a psychologist. Her father told me that in my office and then apologised for telling me, in case it sounded like a competition.

He put the folder on the desk.

In it were: the message from Claire on the twenty-eighth of August with the poem in it; the order confirmation for the dress; and four pages of dates and times he had written out himself in biro, unasked, before the police had ever contacted them, because on the fourteenth of September his wife had said hang on in a kitchen and they had both sat down.

He had given all of it to the police in October, voluntarily, before they knew to ask for it.

He did not tell me that. I found out from Priya in April.

What he said in my office was: "I don't want anything. I'm not asking for anything. I've come because somebody should have come and there isn't anybody else."

I said what about your dad.

And Mark Vance looked at the window for a while and said: "He thinks she's been very hard done by."

Their parents did not come, and have never come, and I understand it better than the family probably think I do.

His father's position, as reported to me at second hand and never varied since, is that Claire made a terrible mistake with a hairbrush and a temper, that the family she was marrying into has money and lawyers, and that a woman's whole life has been taken away over a haircut that grew back.

I do not think he is a monster. I think he is seventy-three and it is his daughter, and there is a recording that he has never listened to and will never listen to, and there is a version of events available to him in which he does not have to.

I said something to Mark about that in the office, some careful thing.

He said: "He's heard it. My mum played it to him."

I said: "And?"

He said: "And he went out to the greenhouse."

Then we sat, both of us, for a bit, in my father's office on a Saturday morning, a man in his thirties and a man in his forties with the same thing in the room.

He said, eventually: "Can I ask you something and you can tell me to get lost."

I said go on.

He said: "Did she — was there ever a time she was all right with your Ella? Genuinely. Because our Soph loved her. She's the auntie who does the good presents. And I've got to work out what to do with that."

That is the best question anybody has asked me about any of this, and it was asked by a man who fits kitchens, in a folding chair, holding his own knees.

I said yes.

I told him about the hill above the reservoir in April, in the rain, Claire kneeling down on wet grass at the eye level of a seven-year-old to say that nobody was going to be anybody's mum and that Mummy is Mummy for ever, and Ella hugging her.

I said: "She meant it when she said it. I'm as sure of that as I'm sure of anything. And I think she couldn't live inside it, and instead of saying so to me she started keeping score, and by August my daughter had become a problem to be managed by a woman who was extremely good at managing things."

Mark said: "So what do I tell Soph?"

I said I did not know. I said I had a psychologist who cost seventy pounds an hour and I would give him her number, and I did, and he rang her, and he paid for it himself out of a kitchen-fitting business, which I only know because Ruth mentioned in the summer that she had been seeing a family from Ashby and would not be able to see us in the same week.

Before he left he said one more thing, in the yard, with the van door open.

He said: "She rings my mum every Sunday. She's doing the unpaid work Thursdays and Fridays. She's — she's all right, you know. That's the thing nobody tells you. She's not in a gutter. She's got a flat in Chapel Allerton and she's got some work back."

I said: "Good."

He looked at me.

I said it again, and I meant it, and this is a thing I have had to explain to about four people since, including my sister, who could not get over it.

I did not need Claire Vance to be destroyed. I had never needed that. If she had been destroyed it would have made no difference to a single one of the eleven days my daughter used the back stairs.

What I needed was for her to be out, permanently, with an order of a court behind it, and for the thing she did to be written down accurately in a place where my daughter can one day read it.

Both of those had happened by the twelfth of February.

Everything after that — where she lives, whether she works, whether she is happy on a Sunday — is a life going on somewhere that is not mine, and the day I understood that I was not required to have an opinion about it was one of the better days of that year.

Sophie is fourteen now, the same as Ella. They are not friends. They are not enemies; they simply live nine miles apart and go to different schools and have no reason.

But in the summer of the year Ella turned twelve, there was a village fair at Ashby, and we went because a man from the works had a stall, and Sophie Vance was working the tombola with two of her mates.

Ella went over.

I stood about forty feet away next to a bouncy castle and did not breathe properly for approximately four minutes.

I do not know what they said. I have never asked and I am not going to. When Ella came back she had a bottle of shampoo she had won, which is the sort of thing the universe does, and she said:

"She's all right."

And then she said, in the tone of somebody closing a file:

"She said sorry about the poem. I said it wasn't her, she was seven."

Which is, word for word, more or less exactly what I had said to Mark Vance in a folding chair in my father's office, and which she had not been in the room for.
