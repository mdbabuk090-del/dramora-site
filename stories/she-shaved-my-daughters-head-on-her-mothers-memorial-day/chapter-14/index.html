She Shaved My Daughter's Head an Hour Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 14 — Twenty-Six Minutes

Chapter 14 of 25

The man from the film company rang me on the fourteenth of November at twenty past four in the afternoon.

His name is Gareth. He owns the company; there are three of them. He was ringing about an invoice.

I want that to be as flat as it actually was, because for two months afterwards people kept trying to make it into something. It was not a discovery. Nobody worked anything out. A man rang up about eight hundred and forty pounds.

He said he was sorry to chase, and that he had held it back because of the circumstances, and that his accountant had made him, and that if I wanted him to leave it he would leave it.

I said pay it, obviously, send it, I'll do it now.

Then he said: "And I've got your disk here. What do you want me to do with it?"

I said what disk.

He explained it twice because I did not take it in the first time.

They had done the sound test at nine, as per the call sheet. Two radio mics: one on me, in the kitchen, which is the one Rhys was clipping to my collar when I heard the noise upstairs; and one placed in the room where the family would be getting ready, so they could check the level in the upstairs corridor for the shot of Ella coming down.

Standard, he said. You put it on a surface, you switch it on, you go and look at your meters.

It went on the dressing table in the room at the end of the corridor at eight forty-five.

Nobody went back for it. At quarter to twelve, when I told them to pack up, they packed up two cameras, three stands, a monitor and a bag of cable, and a case of microphones with one slot empty, and they went home, and the mic was still where it had been left, and it was still recording internally, because that is what those units do — they record to a card at the same time as they transmit, so you have a backup if the radio drops.

It recorded for twenty-six minutes and eleven seconds, until Rhys came back up at seventeen minutes past nine to get his tape and picked it up and switched it off without looking at it.

Then it sat in a box in a unit in Wakefield for nine weeks, because the job had been abandoned and nobody had ingested the cards.

In the November, doing the accounts, Gareth had gone through the abandoned jobs to see what could be wiped and reused.

He had found a file with a date on it.

He had not listened to it. He said that twice, unprompted, both times sounding faintly sick. He said: "It's your house. I don't listen to people's houses."

I said: "Gareth, don't touch it."

He said all right.

I said: "Don't wipe it, don't copy it, don't put it on a laptop, don't send it to me. Leave it where it is and write down what you just told me with the date and the time on it."

He said all right, and then he said, in a slightly different voice, "Should I be worried?"

I said no.

Then I rang Owen Pryce at twenty-nine minutes past four and Owen rang Priya Raval and by six o'clock that evening a police officer had been assigned to go and collect a card from a unit on an industrial estate in Wakefield with a continuity form.

I did not go. I did not ask to go. If you go, it becomes about you.

I want to write down what those next eight days were like, because they are the only part of this where I behaved like somebody in a film and I am not proud of it.

I did not sleep properly. I went to the works and sat in the office and did nothing that could be identified afterwards as work. On the sixteenth I drove to Wakefield for no reason and got as far as the roundabout at the bottom of the industrial estate and turned round and came home, and I have never told anybody that until this sentence.

Because I knew what was on it.

That is the thing. I did not hope — I had gone past hoping in October. I knew, with the whole of my body, that there were four minutes on that card from before I came through the door, and that on those four minutes was the answer to the question I had been carrying since the eleventh of September, which was not what did she do but what kind of thing was it.

And underneath that I knew something else, which took me longer to admit and which I said out loud to Ruth Ellery on the twentieth, in her office above the dentist:

I was frightened it would clear her.

Not frightened for her. Frightened that it would turn out to be a woman losing her temper in eleven seconds, and shouting, and a bad noise, and nothing else — and that I would then have to hold, for the rest of my life, that I had put my hand on somebody and pushed her over a bow and a temper.

Ruth said: "And if it does?"

I said: "Then I'll have been right for the wrong reason."

She said: "You'd still have been right. She shaved a child's head. You keep needing the paperwork to say something the child already told you."

On the twenty-second of November, Priya Raval rang and asked me to come to Leeds.

Owen came. We sat in a room with a table and a laptop and a police officer whose name I have, and Priya said the thing they are obliged to say, which is that I did not have to listen to it, and that I could have it read to me, and that some people find that easier.

I said I would listen.

She said: "There is a fair amount of your daughter on it."

I said I know.

She said: "Mr Ashworth. There is a fair amount of your daughter on it."

I said: "Play it."

It runs twenty-six minutes and eleven seconds. Here is the shape of it, because I am going to give the words themselves in the next chapter and I do not want to give the words without the shape.

Minutes 0 to 9. An empty room with a window open somewhere. Traffic on the ridge road. Once, a long way off, the sound of the marquee people laughing at something in the garden.

Minute 9 to minute 14. Maya and Ella come in. Maya is doing her hair. They have a conversation about whether Ella is allowed to have two of something at lunch, and Ella says a thing about her mother's poem that I have listened to more times than is good for me, and Maya says you'll be brilliant, and Ella says I know, in the flat certain voice of a child who does know.

Minute 14. Claire comes in.

Minutes 14 to 18. The part that is in the next chapter.

Minute 18, at about twenty seconds. The clippers.

Minute 18 to minute 19. My daughter.

I am not going to describe that. It is on a card in an evidence store and it has been listened to by seven people who had to and it will never be listened to by anybody else, and when Ella is old enough to be told it exists she will be told it exists and it will be her decision, and I already know what she will decide, because she is her mother's child, and Ruth agrees with me, and God help us both.

Minute 19, at eleven seconds. Me, on the stairs. You can hear the door.

Minute 19 to minute 21. Everything I have already written in chapter one, including the part that belongs to me. All of it. The whole thing, in sound, with nobody's memory in the way. My own voice saying what wedding, which does not sound the way I have heard it in my head for two months. The chair. That is on there too, and I have never asked to hear that part again.

Minute 21 to minute 26. An empty room. Then footsteps, and a young man's voice saying "oh, sod it" to himself about something unrelated, and the recording stops.

When it finished, nobody in the room said anything for a while.

Then Priya Raval closed the laptop and said: "That is the case."

And Owen Pryce, who has known me thirty-one years and who did my father's will, put his hand flat on the table next to my arm without touching it, which is the most demonstrative thing I have ever seen the man do.
