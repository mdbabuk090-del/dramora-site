She Shaved My Daughter's Head Two Hours Before Her Mother's Memorial

Chapter 19 — Maya Says No To Me

Chapter 19 of 25

In the March I offered Maya Delgado a job that did not exist.

I had thought about it for six weeks and I had built the whole thing in my head, and I was very pleased with myself, which is always the sign.

Head of household. A proper title, a proper contract, a salary that was almost double what she was on, five days a week, her own budget, authority over the cleaner and the gardener and whoever came after, and — this was the part I was proudest of — a clause I had asked Owen to draft which said that any concern regarding the welfare of a child in that house could be raised directly with the estate's solicitor without passing through me.

I had the letter printed. I gave it to her in the kitchen on a Tuesday with a coffee, like an idiot.

She read it twice.

Then she said: "Can I think about it?"

I said of course.

She came back on the Friday and said no.

I did not handle it well. I want that recorded because there is a version of me in this account who learns things, and he is real but he is slower than he looks.

I said: "Is it the money?"

She said no.

I said: "Is it the hours? Because the hours are negotiable, obviously, whatever you want —"

And Maya, who was twenty-four and had been in my house for two years and ten months, put her hand flat on the table and said:

"Daniel. It's a job you made for me."

Here is what she said, over about forty minutes, and I have thought about it more than almost anything else anybody has said to me since.

She said that she had not slept properly for four months. That when Ella went out of a room she counted the seconds until she came back and could not stop. That in the November she had had a thing in a supermarket she did not have a word for, where she had to sit down on the end of an aisle, and that she had not told me because I had enough on.

She said that when she came into my kitchen every morning, the first thing that happened to her was the eleventh of September, and that this had been true for a hundred and seventy days and was showing no sign of stopping.

She said: "You want to give me that house. I can't be in that house."

And then she said the thing that I have quoted to four people since, and every one of them has gone quiet:

"You keep trying to pay me for that morning. I don't want to be paid for it. If you pay me for it, then that's what I did with my twenties."

I said: "You saved her."

She said: "I said no three times to a woman about a hair bow. That's all I did. It's what anybody would have done."

I said that was demonstrably untrue, and that I could name four adults in that house that summer who had heard something and done nothing, one of whom was my sister and one of whom was me.

And she said: "Yes. And if I'd been forty and had a mortgage on this village I might have been one of them. Don't make me a saint, I was just there and I hadn't got anything to lose yet."

That is the single most honest thing anybody said to me in two years.

She left in the May.

Not thrown out — she gave eight weeks and worked every day of them, which I did not ask for. She went to a nursery group in Wakefield, then did her level five, then went into early-years advisory work with the local authority, which is where she is now, four years on, and which she is extremely good at because she is the sort of person who does not get talked out of things by anybody's manner.

She wrote her own reference. I mean that literally: I sat down to write it and produced two pages of the worst sentimental rubbish you have ever read, and she took it off me and said "give me twenty minutes" and produced one page of dates, responsibilities and outcomes, with one line at the bottom that said: Ms Delgado has consistently demonstrated the willingness to escalate concerns regarding a child's welfare irrespective of the seniority of the person concerned.

I signed it. It is the only reference I have ever signed that I would put on a wall.

The thing I got wrong took me a further eighteen months to understand, and it was Ruth who put it to me, of course.

I had done exactly the same thing to Maya that I had spent two years failing to see myself doing to everybody else.

I had decided what would be good for her, in my own kitchen, on my own paper, without asking her one single question in advance. It happened to be generous instead of stingy, and it was still the same act: a man arranging a young woman's life around the requirements of his own conscience.

Ruth said: "What would asking have looked like?"

I said: "*What do you want to do next?*"

She said: "Yes. Four words. Did you say them?"

I did not say them until the Friday, after she had already said no, and by then they were a formality.

Ella cried when she went. Properly, for about two days, and then intermittently for a month.

Maya had prepared for that better than I had. She had told Ella in the March, herself, six weeks before, with my agreement — that she was going to work with lots of children instead of one, that this was not because of anything, that they would still see each other, and that she was not disappearing.

Then she did the thing that made it true, which is that she turned up.

Birthday. Christmas. The school thing in the July. She has been to every single one, for four years, and she is at the table this Christmas, and she brings a boyfriend now called Tom who is quiet and who plays chess with Ella badly on purpose.

There is one more part and it is the reason this chapter exists.

In the second year, when Ella was ten, she asked me at some point in a car — everything happens in cars — what Maya's job was called now.

I said early-years advisory officer.

She said: "What does she do?"

I said: she goes into nurseries and schools and helps them work out how to look after children properly, and part of her job is what to do when a grown-up in a child's life is doing something wrong.

Ella thought about that for about a mile.

Then she said: "That's because of me."

And I said — and I had about four seconds to get this right, and I have never known whether I did —

"Some of it. And some of it is because she was already like that, which is why she was in the room."

Ella said: "Yeah."

And then she said: "She's still cross about the flowers."

I said what flowers.

And my daughter said, from the back seat, at ten years old, in the tone of someone reporting something mildly funny:

"When Claire told her to go and look at the flowers. She says whenever anyone tells her to go and check something now, she goes no thank you first and then goes."

I nearly had to pull over.

Four years on, a woman who is paid by a local authority to advise on the welfare of small children begins every request to leave a room with no thank you — and my daughter thinks it is a joke, and knows it is not, and has filed it somewhere she can get at it, which is precisely where it needs to be.
