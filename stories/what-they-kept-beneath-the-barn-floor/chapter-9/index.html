What They Kept Beneath the Barn Floor

Chapter 09 — The Letter

Chapter 9 of 10

The letter came in February, five months after the verdict, in an envelope with a Gatesville return address and my name on it in my sister's handwriting.

I put it in the drawer under the microwave and left it there for eleven days.

I want to be honest about why. It was not that I was afraid of what was in it. It was that I already knew what was in it, and I did not want to find out I was right, because being right about my sister has stopped being any kind of comfort.

I was right. It is four pages. It is not a confession and it is not an apology, though it contains sentences that look like both if you take them out of the paragraphs around them.

What it is, mostly, is an account.

Two years with Daddy while I was in Louisiana. The house she lost in 2016 and the way nobody in the family offered her anything, not a room, not a loan, not a phone call. The front desk at the dental practice. April, and Mama redoing the will, and telling her about it in the kitchen — my sister devotes most of the second page to that conversation, and I have read it a number of times, and I believe her account of it. I believe Mama said what Della says she said, which was: Wade will do right by it.

Four words. My mother was not being cruel. My mother has never in her life understood that a sentence can be a door closing.

And then on the third page my sister writes:

I know how it looks. But I need you to understand that I never once thought of it as hurting her. I thought of it as waiting her out.

That is the closest thing to the truth in the whole four pages and I do not think she knows it.

There is one paragraph I have not been able to put down.

She writes about the twenty-eighth of August. The day before. She writes that Pearl came and asked her to help move the hay bales, and that she said no, and walked the child out of the barn and shut the door.

And then she writes:

I stood out in the yard afterward and I thought about telling you. I want you to know that. I stood out there for a while.

I have read that maybe forty times and I cannot decide whether it is the most human sentence my sister has ever written or the worst thing in the letter.

Because she stood in the yard. And then she got in her truck and drove forty minutes back to Mineral Wells, and the next evening she came back out with a land agent and a stack of contracts and put them on an oil drum eight feet from her mother's head, and she put fresh tape over her mother's mouth first so that it would go smoothly.

She stood in the yard for a while.

That is what people do instead. That is the entire disease. I stood in my mother's kitchen with a coffee mug and my daughter told me there was something under the barn and I stood there too.

The difference between us is not moral clarity. It is that nobody was paying me four million dollars to keep standing.

She asked, at the end of the letter, whether Mama would write to her.

I did not answer it for a long time. I did not tell my mother about the letter at all for two weeks, and when I finally did — over supper, badly, the way I do everything — she put her fork down and looked at her plate and said, "All right."

That was all, that night.

Three days later she asked me for the address.

I do not know what she wrote. I have never asked and I never will. I know that she sat at the kitchen table for most of an evening with a legal pad, and I know that she took the truck to town herself the next morning to mail it, and I know that when she came back she went out and worked in the tack room for four hours doing something that did not need doing.

They write about once a month now.

I have made my peace with that in the sense that I have stopped saying anything about it out loud. My mother is seventy-five. She had two children. One of them left for eleven years and came home in time to nearly sign her land away, and one of them put her under a floor, and she is going to spend whatever she has left being a mother to both of us because there is not a version of her that does otherwise.

I have not written back to Della. I do not know that I will.

Pearl asks about her aunt maybe twice a year, and she asks the same question every time, in different words.

She wants to know whether Aunt Della was pretending the whole time.

She means the summers. She means the fair in 2024 when Della bought her a funnel cake and let her ride the swings four times in a row. She means a hundred small ordinary kindnesses over nine years, and what she is actually asking, in the way nine-year-olds and now ten-year-olds ask enormous things, is whether a person can love you and do a thing like that, or whether the loving part must have been a lie the entire time.

The first time she asked, I told her I did not know.

The second time, I told her the truth, which is worse and which I think she needed.

I said: no. She was not pretending. She loved you the whole time, and she loved your grandmother the whole time, and she did it anyway. People are not one thing.

Pearl thought about that for a good while.

Then she said, "That's scarier."

She is right. It is scarier. A monster you can watch for. What I did not know how to tell a ten-year-old is that the useful lesson is not some people are secretly bad, it is the ones who love you are exactly the ones close enough to do it, and the only protection anybody has ever come up with is the thing she did.

Which is: when you hear something under the floor, you say so out loud. And when they tell you it is the pump house, you say the pump house is on the other side.

She had a bad autumn. I should put that down, because a lot of people wanted the story to end in that barn with a nine-year-old hero, and that is not how it went.

She stopped sleeping in the tack room. She would not go in the barn alone until December. There were about six weeks in there where she would not let my mother out of a room without following her, and one afternoon I found her sitting on the cellar doors — closed, bar off, the whole thing bolted open now with a chain we put through the brackets so it physically cannot be barred — just sitting on them, cross-legged, doing nothing.

I sat down next to her and did not say anything for a while.

She said, "How long was it?"

"Seventeen days."

"How many did I hear?"

And I understood then what she had been doing out there, and it was arithmetic.

"Four," I said. "You heard four of them."

"So there was thirteen where I didn't."

"Pearlie. You were the only person in the whole county who heard anything at all."

She said, "I know." And then, after a while: "But I heard it on the second night."

There is no answer to that. I have looked for one for a year and a half. She heard it on the second night and she told two adults, and one of them was the person who put her grandmother down there and one of them was me, and the only reason it took another four days is that the second adult was making coffee and did not want to walk out to the barn.

What I said, eventually, was: "Then it's my thirteen days. Not yours."

She let me have that. She is a kind kid and she let me have it, and I could tell she did not entirely believe it, and I am not sure I do either.
