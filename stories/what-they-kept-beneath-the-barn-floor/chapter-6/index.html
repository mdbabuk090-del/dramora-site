What They Kept Beneath the Barn Floor

Chapter 06 — The Man Who Never Signed Anything

Chapter 6 of 10

Ray Whitaker was arrested on the eleventh of September, nine days after he walked out of my mother's barn with the contracts under his arm.

He is fifty-five years old. He is not a criminal in the way you would want him to be. He has a wife and a grown son and a house in Weatherford with a Bermuda lawn, and he had worked land acquisition for the same corridor company for nineteen years, and in nineteen years he had never been accused of anything at all.

He never signed a single document in this whole business. That was the thing that nearly saved him.

Everything went through Della. The power of attorney was in her name. The signature line was hers. The call to the office on the twenty-second was hers. Whitaker's name appears on the contracts only where it must, as the agent of record, in a printed line under a company letterhead, and a printed line is not a confession.

What convicted him was a text message and a fifteen-minute gap.

The text was sent on the twenty-second of August, forty minutes after Della called his office to say the family was ready to move.

It went from Whitaker to a colleague at the acquisition desk, a woman named Prentice, and it said:

Kessler POA came through. Don't ask.

Three words on the end of it. Don't ask.

Whitaker's attorney argued for two days that a man saying don't ask is a man expressing distaste, not knowledge. That he found the family situation unpleasant, that old ladies and their children fighting over land is the worst part of the job, and that a tired man at four in the afternoon typing three words to a co-worker is not the same as a man who knows where a woman is being kept.

It was a decent argument. It nearly worked.

The fifteen-minute gap is what finished it.

On the twenty-ninth of August, the evening in the barn, Whitaker's truck left my mother's turnaround at 7:41 p.m. We know that because his own truck has a fleet tracker on it and the company handed the data over without much of a fight.

The 911 call went out at 7:38.

He was standing in that barn when I made it. He heard me give the dispatcher the address. He heard me say my mother has been locked in a cellar. And then he walked out to his truck, and he did not leave for three minutes, and in those three minutes he took the contracts off the oil drum and put them in his cab, and then he drove to the end of the county road and sat at the stop sign for eleven minutes with the engine running before he turned toward Weatherford.

He did not call anybody. He did not call his office, he did not call his wife, he did not call 911 himself. He sat at a stop sign for eleven minutes.

The prosecutor built forty minutes of closing argument on those eleven minutes, and what she said at the end of it was the only part I actually remember.

She said: An innocent man in that truck is driving to town to tell somebody. A confused man is calling his office. This man sat at a stop sign and worked out what he could still keep.

He took the stand. His attorney did not want him to and he did it anyway, which tells you something about how sure he was that he could talk his way through it.

He said he had believed the power of attorney was legitimate. He said Della had told him her mother was recovering from a stroke and had authorized the sale, and that this was the ordinary way land moves in a county full of old people — through a son or a daughter with a form.

He was asked whether he had ever spoken to Hazel Kessler directly.

He said no.

He was asked whether he had spoken to her in April, when she declined the third offer.

He said yes.

He was asked whether the woman he spoke to in April had struck him as confused, or frail, or in any way unable to manage her own affairs.

There was a long pause on that one. It is in the transcript as (no response), and then the judge asked him to answer, and he said:

"No. She was sharp."

"She turned you down."

"Yes."

"To your face."

"Yes."

"And four months later her daughter walked into your office with a piece of paper, and you did not call her."

He said he did not think it was his place.

There is a piece of this I did not learn until the trial, and it is the piece I think about when I cannot sleep.

Whitaker went out to the ranch on the twenty-sixth of August.

Not the twenty-ninth. The twenty-sixth. Three days before, in the middle of the afternoon, when I was in Mineral Wells getting a starter for the truck and Pearl was at the house.

Della met him there. They walked the south third for about an hour, and then they came back up to the yard, and then — according to Whitaker's own testimony, offered up freely, as though it helped him — they went into the barn to get out of the heat and Della poured him a cup of coffee out of a thermos and they stood there for twenty minutes talking about the survey line.

The prosecutor asked him where they had been standing.

He said by the hay.

She asked him to be more specific. She had a photograph. She put it up on the screen — a sheriff's office photograph of my mother's barn floor with the bales pushed aside and the cellar doors open and a yellow evidence marker beside the broken bracket.

She asked him to show the court where he had stood and drunk his coffee.

He pointed at the doors.

My mother had been under his feet for twelve days at that point. She testified that she heard two people that afternoon. A man's voice and Della's. She said she could not make out words, only the shape of a conversation, two people talking easily, the way people do when nothing is wrong.

She said she knocked.

She said she knocked for a long time.

He got four years. Conspiracy to commit theft of property over three hundred thousand dollars, and one count of failure to report a felony.

He will be out before my mother's fence line is finished.

I sat in the back of that courtroom and listened to his wife cry two rows ahead of me and I could not get to any feeling about it at all. He never touched my mother. He never tied anything or barred anything or drove out from Mineral Wells with a dish towel in his truck. He is a man who was handed a piece of paper he had every reason to doubt, and he looked at four million dollars, and he decided that the doubt was somebody else's job.

That is most of the harm in the world, I have come to think. Not the person who does it. The four or five people standing close enough to ask a question who decide it is not their place.

I was one of them. I was in the kitchen with a coffee mug and my daughter said there's something under the barn, and I said probably a possum.

Whitaker drank coffee standing on the doors.

I do not know how to weigh those two things against each other and I have stopped trying.
