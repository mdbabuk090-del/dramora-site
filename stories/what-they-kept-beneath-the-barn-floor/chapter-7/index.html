What They Kept Beneath the Barn Floor

Chapter 07 — She Fell

Chapter 7 of 10

My sister's first story lasted about ninety minutes.

She stood at the edge of that open cellar on the twenty-ninth of August and said, to nobody, she fell, she went down there and she fell, and she was still saying a version of it when the deputies got there. Mama had gone down for the storm shutters and taken a fall and been trapped, and it was terrible, and thank God for Pearl.

It is not a stupid story. Nine times out of ten it is what actually happened. Old woman, cellar steps, nobody around. It is exactly the story I would have believed if I had come home a week later and found the doors open and my mother gone.

It died on one question, and Corriveau asked it in the barn, standing over the doors with her flashlight, before anybody had even got my mother into the ambulance.

"Ma'am, who put the bar on?"

Della said she did not know.

Corriveau asked how a woman who fell down her own cellar steps came to have a bar dropped across the doors from the outside.

Della said she did not know. Maybe it fell. Maybe it had been up on the wall and it fell.

Corriveau put her flashlight on the two wooden brackets — one torn out by my nine-year-old, the other one seated and solid and screwed to the door — and she did not say anything at all. She just held the light there.

I watched my sister look at those brackets and understand that they could not be explained.

The second story lasted four days.

In the second story, my mother went down there on her own and Della found her and could not get her out, and panicked, and did not call anybody. Della had a bad back. Della was overwhelmed. Della had been going to call the ambulance every single day and had not been able to make herself do it, and had been bringing food and water in the meantime, and the bar — the bar was to keep her from trying the steps and hurting herself worse.

That one is worse than the first one, in a way, because there is enough true material in it to make a person hesitate. She had been bringing food. That part happened. There is a chain of grocery receipts from a store in Mineral Wells with bread and cold chicken on it, every third day for two and a half weeks, and the defence put those receipts up in front of the jury like they meant something.

What killed the second story was the notary.

Because a woman who is panicking and cannot make herself call an ambulance does not, on day seven, drive to a strip center with a forged general power of attorney and a church friend to witness it.

The moment they had the notary, the whole thing changed shape. It stopped being a woman who found her mother in a hole. It became a woman who had a mother in a hole and a closing date.

The third story is the one she went to trial with, and I have to be fair to it, because it is the only one that has any human being in it.

She admitted she put her mother down there.

She admitted the doors and the bar and the dish towel. She admitted the tape, and said — and this is a thing she said several times, in that reasonable voice — that the tape was only for the first day, when Mama was shouting, and that after that she did not use it again except when she had to be at the ranch with somebody else there.

She used it on the twenty-sixth, when Whitaker was drinking coffee.

She used it on the twenty-ninth, before she drove out with the contracts.

What she would not admit, from the first interview through to the verdict, was that she ever intended for her mother not to come back up.

Her position was that she was going to let her out. That she had always been going to let her out. That once the sale closed and the money was in an account and it was too late for Hazel to undo it, she was going to open those doors and take her mother to a hospital and tell a version of the falling story, and her mother — this is the part that tells you everything — her mother would not have turned her in.

She was probably right about that. That is the terrible thing. I have sat with my mother in a lawyer's office and watched her try to find a way to not press charges against her own daughter, and it took the district attorney and me and two hours to get her to stop.

My sister spent seventeen days starving an old woman on a dirt floor, and the thing she was counting on the entire time was that old woman's love for her.

She was offered eight years to plead. She turned it down.

I do not think it was arrogance, exactly. I sat two rows behind her for nine days and what I saw was a woman who genuinely believed that if she could only get the whole thing explained properly — the years with Daddy, the house she lost, the forty-minute drives, the way Mama had done the will in April without telling her — then somebody would nod and say well, when you put it that way.

She testified for most of a day.

She was good for about forty minutes. She cried in the right places and she did not cry too much. She said she had been out of her mind. She said she had gone out there on the fourteenth to have one more conversation about the offer and it had turned into a fight and she had done something she could not take back, and every hour after that made it harder to take back, and by the end she was just a person driving out with bread every three days because she did not know what else to do.

Then the prosecutor stood up and asked her one question about the twenty-eighth of August.

"Mrs. Boyd, on the twenty-eighth, your niece asked you to help her move the hay bales."

"I don't recall that."

"Your niece testified that you told her the bales were too heavy, and then you walked her out of the barn and closed the door behind her."

"I don't recall it that way."

"Did you close the barn door?"

"I may have closed the door."

"Why?"

And there it was. Nine days of trial and it came down to a woman standing in a witness box trying to think of a reason to close a barn door on a nine-year-old, and not being able to find one, because there is not one.

She said: "It was hot."

The barn is open at both ends. There are gaps between every wall board wide enough to put your hand through. Everybody in that room had seen the photographs.

That was the end of it. You could feel it go.

The jury was out four hours and ten minutes.

Unlawful restraint of an elderly individual. Injury to an elderly individual by omission. Forgery of a government instrument. Conspiracy to commit theft of property over three hundred thousand dollars.

Twenty-two years.

She will be sixty-eight.

When they read it, she turned around and looked at the back of the room, and I have gone back and forth for a long time about who she was looking for. I decided eventually that she was looking for Mama.

Mama was not there. She had asked me to bring her and then at the last minute she could not do it, and she sat in the truck in the parking garage in Weatherford for four hours with a paperback she did not open.

She asked me one thing on the drive home.

She asked whether Della had eaten anything that day.
