What They Kept Beneath the Barn Floor

Chapter 02 — Half a Second Too Late

Chapter 2 of 10

Della turned to me and put her hand on my forearm, and her voice went warm again.

"Mama's at the county hospital, Wade."

She said it to me. Not to Pearl. That is worth writing down, because I have thought about it a great deal since. My sister had a nine-year-old three feet away from her insisting on something, and she did not argue with the child. She talked to me. She had been managing me for two weeks by then, over the phone, from four hours away, and she was not about to stop and start managing a little girl instead.

I had Pearl by the shoulders still. She had stopped fighting me.

She looked straight up at her aunt and she said:

"She's under the floor."

Four words. Flat. Not screaming it. She said it the way you would tell somebody their headlights were on.

And I was looking right at my sister's face when she heard it.

Della's expression did not break. I need to be precise here because this is the whole thing, this is the hinge the entire rest of my life swung on, and I have had to describe it to a deputy and then to an investigator and then to a room full of people in Palo Pinto County who all wanted to know how I could not have known.

Her expression did not break. It froze.

The warmth stayed exactly where it was, and underneath it everything stopped. Her jaw set and stayed set. Her breath caught — one small hitch, the kind you would never hear across a room but I was standing close enough to feel it. And her eyes went down and to the left, to the remaining hay bale and the boards beside it, one flick, half an inch of movement, and came right back up to my face.

Then she put the smile back on.

Half a second too late.

I have known that woman my entire life. She is four years older than me. She taught me to drive a stick shift in the north pasture when I was thirteen and she was seventeen and she yelled at me the entire time. I know what her face does when she is angry and when she is tired and when she is lying about something small, like whether she has been smoking again.

I had never seen it do that.

I did not say anything. I want to be clear that I did not have a thought in my head that said my sister has done something. It was nothing that finished. It was like when you are driving and something goes past in the ditch and you are already two hundred yards down the road before your brain says that was a person.

What I actually felt was a kind of confusion that came up through my chest and sat down on top of my anger and put it out.

Pearl went out of my hands.

I had gone slack without meaning to, and she was nine and she was fast, and she was gone across that floor before I closed my fingers. She hit the remaining hay bale with her shoulder, the whole weight of her, and drove it a foot and a half sideways off the boards.

And the floor stopped being a floor.

There is an outline in the planking there. Two doors, laid flat and horizontal, hinged at the outer edges. The boards on them run the same direction as the barn floor and they are the same age and the same colour, which is why they had been invisible to me for the entire twenty minutes I had been standing eight feet away from them arguing about my mother.

Across the seam where the two doors met there was a rusted iron bar, dropped into a pair of old wooden brackets.

I had grown up in that barn. I knew there was a storm cellar under it. Everybody out here has one; you build them for the spring, when the sky goes green. Ours has a set of packed-earth steps and a dirt floor and it smelled of potatoes when I was a boy. I had not been down it since before my father died.

But I had never once in my life seen a bar across it.

You do not bar a storm cellar from the outside. The entire point of the thing is that a person gets into it in a hurry. My father built those doors and he built them to open from underneath with a shoulder, and there was no bar on them in 1994 and no bar on them in 2007 and no reason on this earth for there to be a bar on them now.

I looked at that bar for what felt like a long time.

Then I turned around and looked at my sister.

Not at the doors. At her. I want that in the record too, because a lot of people afterward wanted to know what tipped it, and it was not the doors. Anybody could explain a cellar door. It was that my hands had gone cold and I wanted to see her face, and I did not entirely know why.

Della was not looking at the doors either.

She was looking at Ray Whitaker.

And he met her eyes for about half a second, and then the colour went out of his face and he looked at the ground.

"Leave it be," Della said.

She reached out for Pearl. Her fingers were shaking. I could see it from six feet away, that fine fast tremor in the hand of a woman who is holding herself together with both fists.

And her voice was wrong. It had lost all the warmth and gone thin and quick, riding high in her throat.

"Leave it be, Pearl."

My daughter did not even look at her.

She went down on the boards and got both hands around that iron bar and pulled, and it did not move, because it was seated and rusted and it weighed more than she did.

So she went and got the shovel off the floor where I had knocked it out of her hands.

I did not stop her.

I want to say that I made a decision, that I understood the situation and chose to let my daughter open that cellar. That would be a lie. I did not decide anything. I stood in my mother's barn with my sister's papers eight feet away on the oil drum and I watched a nine-year-old drag a shovel across the floor, and I did not lift a hand to help her and I did not lift a hand to stop her.

She jammed the blade under the bar, up against the near bracket. She got both hands on the handle up high, and she leaned her whole body on it, and she hung there off the end of it with her boots coming up off the boards.

The wood of the bracket tore.

Not the bar — the bracket. The old dry wood split away from the door with a sound like a branch coming down, and the bar swung loose and clanged, and the near door lifted about an inch on its hinges.

Underneath it was black.

And out of that inch of black came a sound.

It was thin. It was very small. It did not have any words in it. It came up out of the ground under my feet and it was, without any possibility of my being wrong about it, a woman.

I heard myself say one word.

"Mama?"

Behind me, I heard my sister's hand come down against her leg.
