What They Kept Beneath the Barn Floor

Chapter 10 — Eleven Hundred and Four

Chapter 10 of 10

The pipeline went through in the end.

Not across my mother's place. They ran it two and a half miles south, across four smaller tracts and one corner of the Hendricks land, and it cost the company somewhere north of nine million dollars extra and took an additional fourteen months. There is a survey stake at the fence line on our south third that has been there since 2021 and I have not pulled it up, and I could not tell you exactly why.

People out here have opinions about that. There was a man at the feed store in Mineral Wells last spring who told me, in a friendly voice, in front of four other men, that a whole lot of folks would have taken the four million.

I said I expect they would have.

He said, "Not worth much, is it. Rock and mesquite."

And I said, "No sir. Not much."

There is no version of that conversation where I come out ahead, so I have stopped trying to have it. He is not wrong about the land. Eleven hundred and four acres of thin caliche over rock, one tank, a creek that runs four months in a good year. If you put it on a spreadsheet it is the worst asset anybody in this family has ever owned.

My mother went down her own cellar steps for a stack of storm shutters and did not come back up for seventeen days over it.

The spreadsheet is not the whole of it, is all I can say. I do not have a better argument than that and I have stopped looking for one.

The trust closed in November of last year. I am the trustee, which I did not ask for and do not particularly want.

What it means in practice is that I cannot sell it intact for twenty years and neither can anybody after me until 2046, and that when my mother dies the land goes on running the way it runs now, and that Pearl will be twenty-nine years old before anybody can make a decision about it at all.

I asked my mother, once, whether she wanted to change any of it. Whether she wanted to put Della back in, in some form, for whenever she comes out. She will be sixty-eight. There will be nothing waiting for her.

Mama said no.

Then, about a minute later, she said: "Not the land."

I have thought about that qualification a great deal. I think what she means is that there will be a room. I think when my sister comes out in 2048, if she comes out and if my mother is alive, which she will not be, there would have been a room.

That is between the two of them. I have decided it is not mine.

We fixed the barn floor in March.

I want to describe it exactly, because it is the last thing I have to put down and because it took me four days to do about six hours of work.

I pulled the two doors off entirely. Both of them, hinges and all. The wood was sound — my father built them in 1988 out of two-inch plank and they will outlast this house — but I took them off and I cut them up and I burned them in the pit behind the machine shed, and my mother came out and watched me do it and did not say a word for or against.

Then I framed the opening properly and put in a new set. They open on gas struts now, the kind you get for a truck bed cover, so that a person on the steps can put a hand up and the door lifts on its own. There is no bar. There are no brackets on the outside. There is a length of rebar welded across the underside of the near door as a handhold, which is the only modification my mother asked for, and she asked for it in one sentence in a normal voice while I was measuring.

She said: "Put something down there a person can grab."

And then she went and did something else.

We did not move the hay. That is the part visitors always get wrong when they come out and want to see it. They expect the bales to be gone. The bales are still there, against the north wall, six feet clear of the doors now, because a working barn needs hay in it and because moving them would be a way of pretending it happened somewhere else.

Pearl helped me put the new doors on. She is eleven now. She held the struts while I got the screws in and she asked whether they would still work if somebody wanted to bar them, and I showed her how the frame is built so there is nothing to bar them to.

She checked it herself. She got down on the floor with a flashlight and went all the way around the frame, both sides, taking her time.

Then she said, "Okay."

I have one more thing and then I am finished.

In August, a year on, my mother turned seventy-five, and we had people out. Not many. Some cousins from Graham, the Hendricks, a woman she has known since 1970 who drove up from Brownwood.

Somebody had brought a cooler and somebody else had brought too much brisket, and at some point in the afternoon everybody ended up in the barn, because that is where the shade is and that is where people end up.

They were standing on the cellar doors.

Not deliberately. There were maybe fourteen people in that barn and the doors are in the middle of the floor and there is no reason not to stand on them. Somebody had set a plate down on the near one.

I looked over and my mother was standing about four feet from the edge of them with a paper cup in her hand, listening to a cousin tell a story about a horse, and she was fine. She was laughing at the right places. She has been fine for a year in the way that she is fine about most things, which is not the same as being all right but is not nothing either.

And then I saw Pearl.

She was over by the wall with a plate she was not eating off, and she was watching the doors. Not watching the people. The doors. She had her back against the boards and she was keeping an eye on that patch of floor the entire time, the way you would watch a stove you were not sure was off.

I went over and stood next to her.

She said, "There's a plate on it."

I said, "I know."

She said, "That's fine though."

I said, "That's fine."

And we stood there together for a while, out of the sun, and I did not tell her to go play and she did not go, and after a bit somebody picked the plate up and moved it and neither of us said anything about that either.

She is eleven. She will be watching that floor when she is forty. I have made my peace with a lot of this and I have not made my peace with that, and I do not expect to.

But she heard it on the second night.

And when they told her it was the pump house, she said the pump house is on the other side.
