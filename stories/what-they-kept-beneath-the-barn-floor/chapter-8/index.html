What They Kept Beneath the Barn Floor

Chapter 08 — What My Mother Did Not Say

Chapter 8 of 10

They kept her in Weatherford for nine days.

Dehydration, two cracked ribs from the fall down the steps on the fourteenth, a urinary infection that had gone further than anybody was comfortable with, and eleven pounds off a woman who did not have eleven pounds to give. Her hands were the worst of it. Seventeen days with her wrists crossed and tied had done something to the nerves on the left side, and there are two fingers she still cannot close all the way.

She was the easiest patient on that floor. The nurses told me so, more than once, in the tone people use when they are being kind about something that worries them.

She did not cry. Not in the ambulance, not in the emergency department, not when the investigator came on the second day with a recorder. She answered every question she was asked, in order, in a flat clear voice, and when she did not know something she said she did not know.

The only time in nine days I saw my mother's face come apart was on the fourth day, when a nurse's aide came in to change the bed and asked her, making conversation, whether she had family close by.

She said: "I have a son."

That was all. And then she looked at the window for a while.

She has two children. She has had two children for forty-six years.

Here is the thing about my mother that I did not understand until all this, and I am forty-two years old.

She is not a warm woman. She was not a warm mother. She grew up on a place outside Graham with four brothers and a father who did not talk, and she came out of that able to do absolutely anything with her hands and almost nothing with her mouth. She has never once in my life said she was proud of me. When Pearl was born she looked at her through the nursery glass for about two minutes and then went and found the janitor to ask about the ice machine, and I was young enough then to be hurt by it.

I thought that meant she did not feel things very much.

What I know now is that she feels them exactly like anybody else and has simply never had anywhere to put them. Seventeen days on a dirt floor and she counted the sunrises, because counting is a thing you can do. When the tape came off her mouth the first thing she asked about was the child. When she gave her statement she gave it in order, with dates, because order is a thing you can hold on to.

And when the district attorney's office sat her down and explained that her daughter was facing twenty-five years to life, my mother's entire response — the whole of it, the total — was:

"Is there a way I don't have to do this?"

She did not mean is there a way she goes free. She is not stupid and she never once said Della should not be punished. She meant: is there a way that I, personally, do not have to be the instrument of it.

There was not. She testified for two hours and ten minutes.

I sat in the back for it and I could not look at her the whole time, so I looked at the court reporter's hands.

The prosecutor took her through the fourteenth of August. Down the steps for the storm shutters. The truck outside. Calling up Wade? and hearing her daughter answer.

She was fine through all of that.

Then the prosecutor asked her what Della had said when she put the tape on.

And my mother sat in that box for a long time. Long enough that the judge leaned over and asked if she needed water.

She said no.

Then she said: "She said she was sorry."

"Your daughter apologized to you while she was doing it?"

"Yes."

"How many times?"

"I don't know. Several."

"Mrs. Kessler, over the seventeen days, did your daughter ever apologize again?"

"Every time," my mother said. "Every time she came."

The defence had one job with my mother, and to their credit they understood what it was. They were not going to shake her. They were going to try to get her to say the thing she had already told the district attorney's office four times, which was that she did not believe her daughter had ever intended to kill her.

The cross went about eleven minutes and it was almost gentle.

Did Della bring you water? Yes.

Did she bring you food? Yes.

Did she ever strike you? No.

Did she ever threaten to leave you down there permanently? No.

Mrs. Kessler, in your own opinion, do you believe your daughter intended for you to die in that cellar?

And my mother said, "No."

The defence attorney sat down almost happy.

Then the prosecutor stood back up for redirect and asked one question, and it is the only thing anybody who was in that room still talks about.

She said: "Mrs. Kessler, on the twenty-sixth of August, you heard two people standing on the doors. You knocked. Nobody came. On that day, did you believe you were going to die down there?"

My mother said, "Yes."

"Both of those things are true at the same time?"

"Yes," my mother said. "She wasn't going to kill me. I was just going to die."

She has never asked me what she should have done differently. She has never said she should have signed it, though I know she has thought it, because I have thought it, and because there was one evening in October when she said, out of nothing, looking at the window: "It's only land."

I said: "Mama."

And she said: "No, I know. I know."

That is as close as she has come.

She will not talk about the cellar. Not the dark, not the cold, not what she did for seventeen days to keep her mind in one piece — I have never asked and she has never offered, and there is a whole territory in my mother now that I understand I am not going to be let into.

There is one thing she told me, once, in the truck, in the same offhand way Pearl tells me things.

She said that on about the tenth or eleventh day she stopped calling out when she heard a vehicle.

I asked her why.

She said, "Because it was always her."
