What They Kept Beneath the Barn Floor

Chapter 05 — What the Girl Heard

Chapter 5 of 10

Deputy Corriveau took Pearl's statement on the front porch, on the second of September, with me sitting on the step where my daughter could see my boots.

She was good at it. I want that written down, because a great many adults were not good at this and she was. She did not crouch down and make her voice go up at the end. She sat on the porch boards with her hat off and she asked my nine-year-old the same questions she would have asked me, in the same voice, and she waited through the long gaps without filling them.

"When did you first hear something, Pearl?"

"The second night."

"The second night after you came home?"

"Yes ma'am. The twenty-fifth."

"How do you know it was the twenty-fifth?"

"Because we got here on the twenty-fourth and that was the day after."

We came home on the twenty-fourth of August. Me and Pearl, in my truck, eleven months of Fort Worth in a duffel bag in the bed.

My mother was not at the house. Della was, and Della had a story ready before the engine was off: Mama had taken a bad turn a week and a half back, she was up at the county hospital in Weatherford, she was doing all right but she was tired and she did not want anybody seeing her yet.

I asked to go up there that first night. Della said visiting hours were done. I asked the next morning. Della said the doctor had her on rest and had asked family to hold off for a few days. On the twenty-sixth I said I was driving up anyway and Della said fine, but call first, and then twenty minutes later she came back and said she had spoken to the nurses' station and Mama had had a rough night and could I please just give it until the weekend.

Every single one of those was a door closing, and I walked into every one of them and turned around and walked away.

I did not call the hospital myself. Not once, in five days.

I have tried to explain that to people. The best I can do is this: she is my sister. You do not audit your sister. There is no version of me from a month earlier who picks up a telephone to check whether Della is lying about our mother being sick, because the act of dialling that number is itself an accusation, and I would have been ashamed to make it.

She counted on that. She counted on it correctly.

Pearl slept in the tack room the first week. That was her idea and I let her, because it has a cot in it and a window that looks out at the pens, and she had been eleven months in an apartment complex in Fort Worth with a parking lot outside her window, and she wanted to hear things.

She heard things.

"It was like knocking," she told the deputy. "Not fast. Like — " and she made a fist and hit the porch board three times, slow, evenly spaced, hard. Then a gap. Then three more.

Corriveau wrote that down and did not say anything for a while.

"Did you tell anybody?"

"I told Aunt Della."

I had not known that. I found that out on my own front porch on the second of September, sitting on a step, listening to my daughter answer a question I had never asked her.

"When did you tell her?"

"In the morning. The next morning. I said I heard knocking in the barn at night and she said it was the pump house."

"What did you say?"

"I said the pump house is on the other side."

Pearl said that flatly. Not proud of it. It was just true — the pump house sits eighty yards northeast of the barn, and a nine-year-old who has spent every summer of her life on that place knows exactly which direction the pump kicks on from.

"And what did your aunt say to that?"

"She said I was making things up because I missed Grandma."

She told me too.

That is the part I have to carry and there is no way to set it down. She told me on the twenty-seventh of August, in the kitchen, while I was making coffee, and she was standing at the counter in her socks with her hair still flat on one side from sleeping.

She said: "Daddy, there's something under the barn."

And I said — and I remember it exactly, I remember the mug in my hand and the smell of the coffee — I said: "Probably a possum, Pearlie. I'll look at it."

And then I did not look at it.

Not that day. Not the next day. I had eleven months of gate repair and a well pump that was cycling wrong and a truck that needed a starter, and I put look under the barn for a possum on the list somewhere below all of that, and the list is why my mother spent three more nights on a dirt floor.

Corriveau asked her, at the end, if there was anything else she wanted to say.

Pearl thought about it a long time. Then she said:

"I didn't know it was Grandma. I thought it was a animal that was stuck."

She started crying then, for the first time in the whole four days. Not when she was hitting the floor. Not when we got the tape off. Then. Because she was nine, and she had worked it out, and what she had worked out was that if she had known it was her grandmother she would have made more noise, and she thought that was her fault.

On the twenty-eighth, the day before, she went and asked her aunt one more time.

She told me about this later, in October, in the truck, out of nowhere, the way kids let things out when you are not looking at them.

She had gone up to Della in the barn and said she wanted to move the hay bales.

And Della had said no.

Not sharply. Della never did anything sharply. She said the bales were too heavy for her and to leave them be, and then — Pearl was very clear about this and repeated it twice — her aunt walked her out of the barn with a hand on her back, all the way out into the yard, and shut the barn door behind them.

That was the twenty-eighth.

The next evening, my sister came out to the ranch with a man in a grey jacket and a stack of contracts and set them down on an oil drum eight feet from her mother's head.

And the first thing she did when she got out of the truck, before she said hello to anybody, was look at the barn to see where the child was.

Pearl remembered that. Nine years old, and she remembered that her aunt looked for her first.

I did not remember it at all. I was carrying a hay ring across the yard and I had my back to the whole thing.
