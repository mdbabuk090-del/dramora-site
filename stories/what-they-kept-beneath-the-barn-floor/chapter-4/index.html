What They Kept Beneath the Barn Floor

Chapter 04 — Eleven Hundred Acres

Chapter 4 of 10

The land is why. It was always going to be the land. There is not anything else out here worth eleven hundred acres of anybody's conscience.

My mother owns — owned, still owns, will die owning if I have anything to do with it — eleven hundred and four acres in the south end of Palo Pinto County. My father bought the first four hundred in 1978 with money he did not have and spent the next thirty years buying the rest of it a section at a time. It is not good land. It is thin caliche soil over rock, mesquite coming back faster than you can grub it out, one decent tank and a creek that runs about four months in a good year. We ran horses on it because you cannot run much else.

What it has is a right of way.

There is a pipeline corridor that comes up out of the Barnett shale and wants to go northwest, and the survey line that makes sense — the one that does not cross the river twice and does not go through the county park — comes across the south third of my mother's place. Whitaker's people had been at her about it since 2020. First a lease. Then an easement. Then, when she would not take either, an outright purchase of the south third with a clause that let them come back for the rest.

The last offer, the one that was sitting on that oil drum, was for four million one hundred thousand dollars.

My mother turned down four million one hundred thousand dollars three times.

People ask why, and they ask it in a tone. The answer is not complicated and it is not sentimental. She is seventy-four. She has been on that place for forty-eight years. She has enough money to run it and enough to be buried, and she had already decided what happens to the land after she is gone, and what happens is that it does not get cut into pieces.

She told Whitaker no in 2021, no in 2023, and no in April of this year.

Della was in the room for the one in April.

Here is where I have to talk about my sister honestly, and it is harder than the rest of it.

Della is not a stupid woman and she was not a monster at seventeen. She stayed. That matters and I am not going to pretend it does not. When our father got sick in 2011, I was on a rig in Louisiana and Della was forty minutes away in Mineral Wells, and it was Della who drove out three and four times a week for two years. Del sat with him. Del did the insurance calls. I came home for eleven days total across the whole thing and I have never once been able to say that out loud without it tasting like something.

She married a man named Boyd who ran a well service company that went under in 2016 and took her house with it. They divorced in 2019. She has been renting a two-bedroom off Highway 180 ever since and working the front desk at a dental practice.

And every year of that, forty minutes away, there sat eleven hundred acres.

I know what my sister told herself. She told me some of it herself, later, in a room with a table bolted to the floor. She told herself that she had earned it. That she had done the years with Daddy while I was gone. That the land was going to come to us anyway and Mama was just being stubborn, and that four million dollars was sitting there rotting while she counted out her own groceries.

She told herself that the only thing standing between her and what she was owed was an old woman who would not sign a piece of paper.

And then in April, our mother did a thing that I do not think Della ever forgave.

She had her will redone.

It was not dramatic and it was not done to punish anybody. She is seventy-four and it had not been updated since Daddy died. But the new one puts the land in a trust that cannot be sold intact for twenty years, and it names the trustee, and the trustee is not Della.

It is me.

I did not ask for that. I did not know about it until the eleventh of September, sitting in a lawyer's office in Weatherford with a bruise on my forearm from a cellar door. My mother had put me in charge of a thing I had spent eleven months four hours away from, and she had done it without telling either one of us.

Except she did tell one of us.

She told Della. In April. In her own kitchen.

The power of attorney is the piece that turns this from a family fight into a criminal matter, and it is worth going slowly on, because it is the one thing my sister did that she could not talk her way around afterward.

On the twenty-first of August — seven days after my mother went down those steps — Della walked into a notary's office in a strip center in Mineral Wells with a general power of attorney form, a driver's licence in my mother's name, and a woman named Charlene Purdy.

Charlene Purdy is sixty-eight. She goes to the same church as my sister. She was told that Hazel Kessler was recovering from a stroke, could not travel, and had asked her old friend to stand in and confirm the signature.

She signed as a witness.

The notary — and I have read his statement, and I do not think he is a bad man, I think he is a man who processes forty documents a day in a strip center — notarized a signature he did not watch anybody make.

The signature on that form is not my mother's. It is not even a very good attempt at my mother's. Hazel Kessler makes a capital H that goes down below the line and comes back up with a hook on it, and she has made it that way on every cheque she has written since 1974, and the H on that power of attorney is an ordinary schoolbook H.

My sister spent seventeen days trying to get an old woman in a hole to sign a document.

When the old woman would not, she went and had somebody else sign it.

And on the twenty-second of August, with that piece of paper in her hand, Della Kessler Boyd called Ray Whitaker's office and told them the family was ready to move on the south third.

The closing was scheduled for the second of September.

That is four days after Pearl hit the floor with a shovel.

I have run that number more times than is healthy. Four days. If my rig contract had run its full term I would not have been home until the eighth. If Pearl had stayed in Fort Worth with her mother that week, the way we had originally planned, there would have been nobody in that barn but a man holding a pen and two people who wanted him to use it.

I would have signed it. I want to be plain about that, because there is a version of this story where I come out of it looking like a man who figured something out, and that man does not exist.

My sister told me our mother had a stroke and my sister put her finger on a line and I picked up the pen.

The only reason my mother is alive is that a nine-year-old would not shut up.
