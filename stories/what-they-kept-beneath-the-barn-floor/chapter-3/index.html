What They Kept Beneath the Barn Floor

Chapter 03 — Seventeen Days

Chapter 3 of 10

I threw the doors back myself.

I do not remember crossing the floor. I remember the weight of them coming up — they are heavy, two-inch plank, and the hinges screamed — and then I remember the light going down the steps ahead of me in one long orange wedge, the sunset coming in through the west wall and pouring straight down into that hole.

My mother was sitting on the bottom step.

She had her back against the earth wall. Her wrists were tied in front of her, crossed, with a strip of cloth — I found out later it was a torn dish towel from her own kitchen, one of the yellow ones with the hen on it. There was silver duct tape across her mouth. Her hair had come down out of its pins on one side. Her cardigan, the knitted one she has had since I was in high school, had slid off her right shoulder and she had not been able to pull it back up because of her hands.

She was alive.

The light hit her and she squeezed her eyes shut against it and turned her face away, and it was the ordinary human way a person moves when a light comes on too fast, and I think that is the moment my legs stopped working properly.

She opened her eyes. She looked up out of the hole.

She saw Pearl first.

My daughter was on her knees at the near edge with both hands flat on the boards, and my mother looked up at her grandchild and her whole face came apart. Not fear. The other thing. Her eyes filled and went over and she made a sound behind the tape that I am not going to try to write down.

Then she saw me.

I got down on the boards. I did not have any words. I said "Mama" again, like a fool, like she had not heard me the first time, and I was reaching down and I could not reach her because the steps go down further than you think.

And then my mother's eyes moved past me.

She looked over my shoulder at the edge of the hole, where my sister was standing.

And everything that had just come into her face went straight back out of it.

The relief drained off. What came up in its place was quiet, and it was careful, and it was fear — not the loud kind, not a scream. The kind an animal has when it has already learned what a particular person does. She went still and she watched Della the way you watch a dog you know.

I turned my head and followed my mother's eyes.

Della was not looking down into that cellar at all.

Her mother was tied up eleven feet away from her, in the dark, in the dirt, and my sister was not looking at her. She was looking at me. At my face. Reading it. Working out, in real time, how much I had put together.

We looked at each other.

She swallowed.

And the composure that woman had held for two weeks over the telephone and for twenty minutes in that barn finally came off her face, all at once, like something falling off a wall.

Behind her, Ray Whitaker turned his head and looked down at the land contracts on the oil drum.

Not at my mother. At the paperwork.

I saw him do it.

Nobody said anything at all.

I got her out.

I want to put down here what that actually took, because the way I had always imagined a thing like this, a man just picks his mother up. It was not like that. She could not stand. She had been sitting on a step or lying on a dirt floor for seventeen days and her legs would not hold her, and she was seventy-four, and she weighed almost nothing and she was still too heavy for the angle of those steps.

Pearl went down first. That is the part nobody tells you either. My daughter went down that hole ahead of me and got the tape off her grandmother's mouth, working at the corner of it with her fingernails, careful, saying sorry, sorry, sorry every time it pulled.

The first thing my mother said, when the tape came off, was not my name and it was not Della's.

She said: "Is the girl all right?"

Seventeen days. That was the first thing.

We got her up into the barn. I put my jacket under her head and Pearl sat on the floor with her grandmother's head in her lap, and I called 911 with my hands shaking so badly I got the last digit wrong twice.

Della did not run. People always want to know that, and the answer is no. She stood at the edge of the open cellar with her arms down by her sides and she watched us, and at one point she said, to nobody in particular, in a perfectly reasonable voice: "She fell. She went down there and she fell."

I did not answer her. I did not trust what would come out.

Ray Whitaker walked out of the barn at some point in there. I did not see him go. When the deputies came, his truck was already gone from the turnaround and the contracts were gone off the oil drum.

He had taken the papers.

He had stepped over my mother's cellar doors to do it.

She had gone down there on the fourteenth of August.

I know the date because she knows the date. Seventeen days, and she kept every one of them. That is my mother. She counted the sunrises by the light coming through the gap at the top of the doors, and when she was not sure she counted low, and she was right to within a day.

She had gone down for the storm shutters. There is a stack of them in the cellar, the plywood ones my father cut for the kitchen windows, and there had been weather coming up out of the southwest that Thursday. She was on the third step when she heard the truck.

She thought it was me. She had known for a month that I was coming home from the Fort Worth contract, and she stood on those steps in the dark with her arms full of plywood and called up, "Wade?"

And a voice said, "It's me, Mama."

Della had driven out from Mineral Wells. That is a forty-minute drive. She had a torn dish towel and a roll of tape in the truck.

My mother told the sheriff's investigator that she was not afraid at first. Even when the door came down. She thought it was a mistake, or a joke, or that her daughter was drunk. She sat down on the bottom step in the dark and she waited to hear the bar come off.

She heard it go on instead.

Della came back every third day.

That is the part I still cannot get to sit right in my head, and I have had a lot of time. This was not something my sister did in a rage and then could not undo. She drove out from Mineral Wells, forty minutes each way, every third day for seventeen days. She lifted one door about a foot. She put down a jug of water and a plate — bread, cold chicken, once a peach — and she lowered the door and put the bar back on.

She never once let the doors open wide enough for my mother to see her face.

And every one of those times, my mother said, she talked. Through the gap. Perfectly calmly. The same three or four things, over and over, in the voice she uses on the phone.

You're being stubborn, Mama.

This would already be over if you'd signed it.

Wade's coming home. Do you want him to find you like this?

Seventeen days. My mother did not sign it.
