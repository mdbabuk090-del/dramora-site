Chapter 10 — Seven More Names

Chapter 10 of 10

The body buried under Eleanor Whitmore's name belonged to Mara Jennings.

She was thirty-one when she disappeared.

She had no connection to the Whitmore family that investigators could find. No company shares. No trust. No inheritance. No powerful relatives.

For three years, her family had believed she left California voluntarily after an argument with her sister.

They had searched for her anyway.

Posters.

Missing-person databases.

Calls to hospitals.

Nothing.

Then police told them Mara had been buried beneath another woman's name in a cemetery two hundred miles away.

The identification did not solve the mystery.

It deepened it.

Mara's employment records showed that she had worked as a bookkeeper for a small medical billing company.

The company processed claims for North Valley.

Six weeks before her disappearance, she had contacted a state regulator about duplicate patient numbers and payments attached to people who seemed to exist under several identities.

Her complaint was closed after the company reported that she had been fired for theft.

No theft charges were ever filed.

Two months later, her body was used to make Eleanor legally dead.

"She found the system," Torres said.

We were in a conference room filled with evidence boxes, whiteboards, and enough coffee cups to prove the investigation had outgrown ordinary working hours.

"And someone made her disappear," I said.

Torres looked toward the board.

"Maybe. We prove it. We don't assume it."

That was one reason I trusted her.

The story was already dramatic enough. It did not need us to invent certainty.

At the hospital, Eleanor's physical condition was improving. The baby remained stable. Independent doctors controlled every medication. No one connected to Reed's network could enter the floor without police approval.

Alexander visited only after asking permission.

Some days Eleanor said yes.

Some days she said no.

He accepted both answers.

Lily visited once.

I almost said no when Eleanor requested it. My daughter had already been exposed to too many adult lies. But Eleanor asked to thank her, and Lily had been asking about "the real pregnant lady" every morning.

They met in a quiet hospital sitting room.

Lily stood near the door at first.

"I'm sorry your baby picture was in that bag."

Eleanor smiled faintly.

"You didn't put it there."

"I told everyone about it."

"You told your mother what you saw. Your mother checked. That helped the police find me."

Lily considered that.

"So I wasn't being nosy?"

"You were being eight."

That made her laugh.

It was the first uncomplicated sound I had heard around the case in days.

Then Lily looked at Eleanor's stomach.

"Is the baby okay?"

"Yes."

"Does she know all this happened?"

"No."

"Good."

Eleanor's eyes filled.

"Yes. Good."

After Lily left, Eleanor told me something she had not yet told Torres.

At North Valley, there had been nights when she heard people moved through the corridor after midnight. They were never brought past her room in daylight. Staff used numbers instead of names.

"I thought they were psychiatric patients," she said. "Then I heard one woman yelling that her husband was trying to steal her company. Another kept saying she had signed nothing."

"How many?"

"I don't know."

"Did you see them?"

"Not clearly."

"Did you see the black-square mark?"

"On some folders."

I called Torres from the hallway.

By evening, investigators had reopened every entry in Reed's private ledger.

The seven black-square files were the priority.

One belonged to Mara Jennings.

Two matched living people who had been declared legally incompetent in other states.

One matched a woman who had died in a private-care facility after signing away control of a family manufacturing business.

Three names did not appear in any ordinary database.

No birth records.

No death records.

No addresses.

Nothing.

Torres believed the names might be aliases.

Then a financial investigator found a common detail.

Each black-square case was connected, directly or indirectly, to a large transfer of assets within ninety days of the patient's disappearance, incapacity, or death.

Not all of the money went to the same people.

That was why no one had connected the cases.

The method was consistent.

The beneficiaries were not.

North Valley was not serving one family.

It was serving a business model.

Dr. Reed had provided medical credibility.

Cynthia had provided legal and nonprofit structures in some cases.

Julian had used that machinery for the Whitmore plan.

But the machinery existed before Julian needed it.

That changed everything.

Late that night, Torres came to the Grand Regent.

I was in my office reviewing the gala timeline again when she placed a thin file on my desk.

"We found something in Reed's archived server."

Inside were seven patient intake forms.

Each carried the black square.

Each contained a handwritten note beside the emergency-contact field:

FAMILY DISPUTE - ASSET RISK.

Six forms had photographs.

The seventh did not.

Instead, it had a scheduled admission date.

Two weeks in the future.

The patient had not arrived yet.

Which meant, for the first time, we were not looking at someone who had already disappeared.

We were looking at the next person.

Torres tapped the name.

"Claire, this woman doesn't know she's on the list."

I read it once.

Then again.

I knew the name.

Not from the Whitmores.

From the Grand Regent.
